# Chapter 20: The Fourth Cistern

*The Account · CR 929*

In the dark, somebody took hold of my wrists.

I didn't fight. I couldn't see; there was nothing to see. Twenty thousand people screaming in a black iron cavern, the carbines on the galleries firing at nothing, muzzle-flashes going off like lightning in the ribs of the ceiling and lighting, for a hundredth of a second each, a frozen picture of everything — faces, hands, banners torn loose and turning, a child upside down in the air with her mouth open — and then black again. Someone took hold of my wrists, and something cold slid between them, and there was a sharp snap, and the cuffs fell away.

"Up the Lantern stair," said Teodor Rask's voice in my ear. "Then left. Calla's got the broadcast room. Go."

"Thorne—"

"Your clerk's on the dais. Under the table, if he's got sense. Go."

The emergency lamps came on.

Red. All of them at once, on every rib, a dull blood-coloured glow that turned the Hall into the inside of a furnace and the grey banners into slow black smoke. The Mercy Guardsmen who'd held my arms were gone — down into the crowd, or dead; I didn't see. On the forward gallery beside me a man in ballast grey was lying on his back in the air with his arms out, turning slowly, and his face was gone. I took the carbine out of his hands. I'd never fired one. I took it anyway.

I didn't go up the Lantern stair. I went down.

It's a long way from the forward gallery to the dais, through a crowd in panic in zero gravity. You can't walk. You can't swim. You go hand over hand along whatever you can reach — a rail, a cord, a stranger's coat, a stranger — and the crowd moves round you like water in a tank that's been struck, in surges, one way and then another, and you hold on through the surges and go on in the slack between. I went down the gallery stanchions to the deck and along the deck by the bolts in the iron plates, flat, under the crowd, the way miners go along a gallery floor when there's gas. It took me four minutes. I counted.

He was under the table. He had sense.

Two of his ratings were crouched in front of it with their white backs to the crowd and their sidearms out, and they turned them on me as I came along the deck, and Isaak Thorne said from under the black wood, in his flat timetable voice, "Let him through."

I went under. There was just room. He was folded up against the table's pedestal with his document case held against his chest and his crystal eye glowing faintly gold in the red dark, perfectly calm.

"Assessor," he said. "I'd hoped you'd come."

"Commander. I lodge."

"Under what standing? You're charged with murder." He said it without any emphasis at all, as if reminding me of an appointment. "I can't accept the oath of a man under a cloud. I told you."

I took out my handset. I found the recording and turned it to full and held it up between us in the red dark, under the table, with the crowd roaring and the carbines cracking outside; and Bastian Oduya's voice came out of it, high and fast and shaking, the way it had come out of him in the cradle bay among the open lids. *He said she was going to give the Hold a weapon. The Chief. He called me in.* Thorne listened. His crystal eye clicked. *She said, Bastian, let go, very quietly, like you'd say it to a child, and I—* It went on. *The paint. The words. I'd never seen them on a wall. He told me to put the pen in your rooms.* I'd heard it four times by then. I hadn't got used to it. I don't suppose I ever would have.

When it stopped, Thorne was quiet for a moment.

"Is he alive?"

"I don't know."

"Will he swear to it?"

"No. He'll never be found. That's all there is."

"That's his voice," said Thorne. "Under his name, recorded, with the circumstances. And the Chief Assessor?"

"Told me himself. This morning. In his office. *Every Chief Assessor is told.* He's known for nineteen years." I hadn't recorded that. I hadn't thought to; I'd gone to him to put something on his desk, not to take something away. "You'll have my oath for it."

Thorne looked at me with both eyes for a long moment in the red dark under the table.

"Then make it," he said. "Formally. In full."

So I made it. Under the black wood table in the Hall of the Account on the Burning, with twenty thousand people screaming round us and a dead man's carbine across my knees. I gave my name and my rank and my office. I swore before the Bureau of Titles of the Concordance of Worlds that the Account of Liberty was false; that its founding page had been composed nine years after Landfall in Tamar water under the seal of the Founders' Council, and described an entry it did not contain; that the Office of the Account knew it to be false, its Chief Assessor having said so to me that morning in his own words; and that Keeper Ottilie Brask had been killed for proving it, by the hand of Assessor Bastian Oduya, on the Chief Assessor's order, as the recording I'd given the Bureau showed.

I said it level. It's the one thing they train you for.

"Lodged," said Isaak Thorne.

He closed his document case. He looked out past his ratings' white backs at the furnace-red Hall and the crowd and the turning black banners. Then he took a small flat thing from his collar — a disc of the same pale gold as his eye — and pressed it.

"*Probity*," he said. "Thorne. Open my line to the station net and to the squadron, all channels. And find me whoever's holding this station's broadcast room."

* * *

It was Calla who was holding it, of course.

Her voice came over every speaker in the Hall a minute later — hoarse, out of breath, furious, ice-script in every syllable — telling twenty thousand people to get down, get down on the deck, get their children down, the Hold had the doors and the Hold had the broadcast and nobody else needed to die today. Then she stopped, in the middle of a sentence, and there was a pause, and when she spoke again her voice had changed.

"There's a — someone wants to say something." Another pause. "The Concordance man. The clerk. He says it's about the Account. Everyone shut up and listen."

Then Thorne's voice, flat and planed and absolutely unhurried, coming out of every speaker in the Hulk, and the Crown, and every ferry, and every screen in Saltmarch and Gallows Reach and the Sump; and, Calla told me afterward, the bridge of every ship in the Concordance squadron, and the white-collared woman from Ostrava in her suite in the Crown, and the Speaker of the Houses in hers.

"This is Verifier-Commander Isaak Thorne of the Bureau of Titles of the Concordance of Worlds. I was asked to verify the title of the Houses of Absalom to the instrument known as the Account of Liberty, for the purpose of its conveyance. I have examined the instrument. I have received this day the sworn lodging of an officer of the issuing office, impeaching it. The Bureau finds that the instrument's founding entry is not what it purports to be." The smallest pause. "The Bureau of Titles declines to verify. Title is not proved. The conveyance is void. The Ostrava Labor Trust has no instrument in this system and may take no satisfaction under it." Another pause. "That is all."

The Hall was silent.

Not quiet. Silent. Twenty thousand people, in the red dark, in the zero gravity, with the banners turning over them like smoke; and nobody made a sound. I could hear the ventilation. I could hear someone, far off near the stern hatch, breathing very fast.

Then somebody started to laugh.

I don't know who. A woman, somewhere in the middle of the crowd. A high, cracked, astonished sound, like someone who's been told a joke at a funeral and can't help it; and it went on and on, and someone else joined it, and someone else, and then the whole Hall was making a sound I'd never heard a crowd make and have never heard since — not a cheer, not weeping, something between them and older than either, a great broken roar that went up into the iron ribs and came back down and went up again, and didn't stop.

Beside me, under the table, Isaak Thorne took off the gold disc and put it back in his collar and closed his eyes — both of them, the real and the crystal — for a moment, as if he were very tired.

"Thank you, Assessor," he said. "You'd better go and find your daughter."

* * *

She'd found the door.

I didn't know it until I got there. I went up through the crowd toward the wards' gallery, hand over hand through the roaring, with the carbine on its strap over my back, and the white dresses were all gone from behind the brass rail, and the service door at the end of it was standing open on a narrow stair going down. I went down. At the bottom there was a laundry passage, hot and wet, lit by the same red emergency lamps. And halfway along it, sitting on a pile of clean sheets in a white dress too big at the shoulders, with her hair coming down out of its Crown pins and Mags Tiller's big red arm round her, was Wren.

She looked up and saw me. She didn't move. She sat on the sheets and looked at me with her mother's face and mine, and her mouth opened, and nothing came out of it.

Then she was off the sheets and across the passage and I had her.

I don't remember what I said. I don't think I said anything. I held her, in the red hot wet laundry passage, with her face in my borrowed coat and my face in her hair, and she was shaking, and I was shaking, and Mags Tiller sat on the sheets behind her and looked at the wall and wiped her eyes with the back of her hand.

"Line seven," said Wren at last, into my coat. "Word four. *Door.* I remembered. I was so — Da, I couldn't remember line seven, I had to say the whole Catechism in my head under the blankets to find it, and the guard heard me and thought I was praying—"

"You found it."

"I found it." She pulled back and looked at me. Her face was wet. "They said you killed her. The Keeper. They said it every day. They made me—" She stopped. "You didn't."

"No."

"I knew you didn't. I told them. I told them you couldn't even kill the fish in Mam's tank when it got the rot, you made me do it." She laughed, and it turned into something else. "Da. It's my birthday. It was. Yesterday."

"I know, love. I'm sorry."

"Is it Clear? Am I?"

I looked at her. I thought of the clerk at the Clearances counter, and *pending the Speaker's review*, and four hundred and six marks lodged against a portion that wouldn't close.

"I don't know," I said. "I think — I think there might not be an Account to be Clear of any more. I don't know what that means yet. Nobody does."

The Hulk shuddered.

It came through the deck and the walls and the pile of clean sheets, a single deep blow, as if something enormous had struck the station very hard somewhere a long way off. The red lamps flickered. Somewhere above us, through decks of iron, there was a long rolling crash, and then a sound I knew from schoolroom drills and had never in my life heard for real: the slow grinding boom of emergency bulkheads closing, one after another, all along the spine.

Mags Tiller was on her feet. "That's not ours," she said. "We've nothing that does that."

My handset spoke. Calla's voice, very fast.

"Fen. Where are you?"

"Laundry, Crown underside. I've got Wren."

"Get her to the stern dock. Now. Severine's ordered the frigate to fire on the Hulk. She's trying to take the broadcast room back with the guns. The bow docks are holed — we've lost the forward spoke. I've got a ferry at the stern taking out wounded. Get her on it."

* * *

We went aft along the underside of the Crown through the service ways, Mags in front with her lamp and Wren between us, and twice more on the way the Hulk shuddered under us and the lamps went out and came back, and the third time a pipe burst over our heads and filled the passage with steam; and we came out at last onto the stern dock, where the ferry was.

It was one of the pilgrim ferries. Its ramp was down and its cabin was full of wounded — people from the Hall, on the floor and the seats and each other, with Hold people moving among them with dressings — and Calla Dray was standing at the foot of the ramp with a carbine and blood on her face, shouting at someone in the cabin. She turned when she saw us. She looked at Wren for a long moment.

"This is her," she said.

"Yes."

"She looks like you. Poor thing." She jerked her head at the ramp. "In. Find a seat or a floor. We go in two minutes."

Wren didn't move. She was holding my hand. "Da—"

"Go on, love. I'll come after."

"You'll come *now*."

"I'll come after." I took her face in my hands. "There's something I've got to fetch. It's mine. It was your grandmother's. Somebody's taken it and I want it back."

"The key," said Wren. She'd seen it every week of her childhood, swinging on its chain when I bent to hug her. "Your ice key. Da, it's an old key, it doesn't open anything—"

"I know," I said. "I want it anyway."

She looked at me for a long time. Then she nodded, slowly, the way she'd nodded at nine when I told her the cipher was a secret just for us; and she let go of my hand and went up the ramp into the ferry, and at the top she turned round and looked at me, and I lifted my hand, and she lifted hers.

"Fen, you fool," said Calla Dray beside me.

"Yes."

"Marsh has it?"

"Marsh has it. And Marsh walked off that dais with his hand in his pocket toward the vault before the lights went out. I saw him."

Calla looked at me. Then she turned her head and spat, deliberately, on the deck between us, the way she had in the cold room the first night. A line.

"Severine's yacht broke dock ten minutes ago," she said. "From the Crown hub. She's burning for the Lane with her House guard and half the Constance plate. Merriam says she took a copy of the Account with her — they made one last month, for the Trust's lawyers. A full copy, under the Council's seal." She gripped the carbine. "She'll sell it somewhere. Some day."

"Then let them try to collect on it."

"Oh, they will. Somebody always does." She stepped back onto the ramp. "We'll be at Saltmarch in forty minutes. I'll put her with her mother myself. Then I'm coming back for this station." She looked at me. "If you're in it when I do, I'll buy you a drink. If you're not, I'll tell her you said something brave."

"Tell her I'm sorry about her birthday," I said.

Calla Dray looked at me for a moment longer. Then something happened to her scarred face — I don't know what to call it; it wasn't a smile — and she nodded once, and turned, and went up the ramp, and it closed behind her.

* * *

The spine was dark when I went down into it. The emergency bulkheads had closed every hundred metres, and I had to go through the crawl-hatches beside them one at a time, with the carbine on my back and my handset for a lamp. Past the Old Hold, with its great double hatch shut and somebody hammering on the other side of it. Past the Index, where the cabinet doors had burst open in the shock and the passage was full of drifting cards, white in the handset's glow, turning slowly, like snow.

The vault door was open.

It shouldn't have been. It's a Charter door, a metre of iron, and it closes itself on any loss of pressure in the passage; the drills say so. But someone had wedged it — a steel chair from the Keepers' antechamber, jammed between the door and the frame — so that it stood a hand's breadth open, and there was light inside. Not red. White. The vault's own lamps, on their own cell, burning as they'd burned for three hundred years.

I went in.

He was at the wall under the brass plaque. He'd tied himself to the pedestal of the old cradle with his belt — looped it through a handhold on the iron and round his waist, so that he wouldn't drift while he worked — and he was floating there at the end of it in his grey coat with the Chief's chain round his neck, in front of the little steel safe in the wall. Its door was open. My mother's key was in the lock.

The Account sat behind him on the cradle where it had always sat, strapped down with its steel bands, a great grey crystal animal on a stool too small for it. He'd turned his back on it.

He had a bottle in one hand. A squat brown bottle with a broken wax seal. In the other, a pistol. He wasn't pointing the pistol at anything. He was holding the bottle up to the light, and looking at it, and laughing — very quietly, his shoulders shaking — the way a man laughs when something has turned out to be exactly as foolish as he'd said it would be.

"A bottle of something," said Lucan Marsh. "I told you. Didn't I tell you?" He turned his head and looked at me. He didn't seem surprised. "Three hundred years, and nobody could open it, and the Houses tried drills, and it ate the drills. And inside it there's a bottle of brandy. Callisian, I think. Somebody's drunk about a third of it." He held it out toward me, gravely, across the vault. "Would you like some? It's quite terrible."

"Where's the rest of it?"

"The rest of what?"

"What was in the box."

He looked at me for a moment. Then he opened the hand that held the pistol, and I saw that there was something else in it, held against the grip with his thumb: a small grey thing, flat, the size of a thumb.

"This," he said. "Only this." He held it up to the white light and read it, squinting. "*Art. Nine. A.V.*" He looked at me. "What does it mean, Hollis? You were always better at puzzles than I was. Ottilie thought so."

I didn't answer. I didn't need to look. I'd floated past the brass plaque on the wall above his head when I was thirteen and never once read it; I'd floated past it again six days ago with Thorne and never once read it. I read it now. Twenty-two articles in a cramped legal hand, and the ninth of them about a third of the way down, in letters so small you'd need to be close.

"*Should the vessel be taken*," I said aloud, "*by any party not lawfully in command, the Purser shall commit the Book to the ballast, that it be not suborned.*"

The vault was very quiet.

"The ballast," said Lucan Marsh slowly.

I watched him understand it. I watched him in the white light, at the end of his belt, with the brandy in one hand and the little grey plate in the other, the way I'd watched three hundred and nine people understand the figure on the tally: the stillness first, and then behind the stillness something beginning to move very fast. *A.V.* *The Purser would have sold us.* *Her name was Annick Vell, and it is written in ash.* His eyes went to the Account on its cradle behind him. Then down. To the floor beside the cradle's pedestal, where there was a small square hatch with a red lever, and beside it, flush in the iron, a round plate a metre across with eight bolts round its rim and a stencil on its edge I'd never in my life looked at.

"She didn't burn it," he said. Softly. Wonderingly. "Oh, Hollis. She didn't burn it."

And he raised the pistol.

I don't know what he meant to do with it. I've thought about it a great deal. I've thought he meant to shoot me, because I was the only other person alive who knew. I've thought he meant to shoot the plate in the floor, which wouldn't have done anything at all. I've thought — down here I keep coming back to it — that he meant to shoot himself, because he was sixty-eight, and he'd known for nineteen years that every ledger is a forgery, and he'd just learned that there was one that wasn't. I don't know. I'll never know. He raised the pistol, and I had a dead man's carbine on a strap across my chest with my hand already on it, and I'd never fired one in my life.

He fired first. I felt it go into my side, low, on the left, under the ribs — not pain, not yet; a blow, like being kicked by something very large — and the recoil sent him back on the end of his belt against the wall, slowly, and his free hand opened, and the brandy bottle turned away from him end over end through the white air.

I fired. Three times. I don't remember deciding to. The carbine bucked in my hands and threw me back against the door frame, and the noise in the iron room was so loud it wasn't a noise, it was a place, and when I could see again Lucan Marsh was floating at the end of his belt by the cradle's pedestal with his arms out a little in front of him and his white hair spread round his head, turning very slowly, with his back to me.

I don't know how long I stayed there. Long enough to start counting. I got to nine. That was where the man in Spoke Five had stopped, and I don't know why, but I couldn't go past it.

Then I went across the vault and took my mother's key out of the lock.

* * *

The little grey plate had drifted off his palm. I found it against the brass plaque, turning slowly, and put it in my shirt with Ottilie's papers. The bottle I let go.

I was bleeding. Not badly, I thought; not yet. A slow dark seep from the hole in my side that came away from me in small trembling beads in the zero gravity and drifted, and caught the white light, and hung. I pressed my hand over it and held it there, and it went on seeping between my fingers.

There's an emergency locker in every room in the Office. Fire hoods — soft clear bags with a collar seal and a little bottle of air on the side, ten minutes' worth, for smoke. I took two. I took the steel bar from the Keepers' fire kit. Then I knelt on the iron by the cradle's pedestal, beside the drop hatch with its red lever, over the round plate with eight bolts.

*Recovery port*, said the stencil. *Ballast 4 well. For use at port by lawful owner only.*

Ballast Four. I knew what that was. I'd hung with my forehead against its hatch six days ago in the dark, at the end of a dead-end corridor, listening to the water knock. *Fourth Cistern. Closed CR 718. Contaminated.* Two hundred years of poison, sealed up and left.

I put the bar to the first bolt.

It took a long time. They were three hundred years old and they'd never been turned, and every one of them fought me, and the hole in my side opened a little more with every heave. Twice the Hulk shuddered round me and the white lamps flickered, and once there was a long low grinding from somewhere aft that went on for nearly a minute, and I stopped and held on to the pedestal with my bloody hand and waited for it to finish. Lucan Marsh turned slowly at the end of his belt above me the whole time. I didn't look at him.

The eighth bolt came free at — I don't know. I'd stopped counting. I put my shoulder under the rim of the plate and heaved, and it lifted, slowly, a great iron disc that in any gravity would have taken four men; and I swung it up and over and let it settle against the side of the pedestal, and looked down.

A shaft. Two metres across, iron-walled, with rungs set into one side, going down. Three metres, perhaps. And at the bottom of it, filling it from wall to wall, black and perfectly still, water.

Not ice. Water. Cold enough that I could feel it on my face from up here, breathing up out of the well like the breath of something asleep. In the white light from the vault it didn't shine. It just lay there, black, a floor you could fall through.

*Contaminated.* Two hundred years. A fever in the water, hundreds dead in the Crown.

I put on the fire hood. I sealed the collar. I opened the little air bottle and heard it hiss. I tied my handset to my wrist with its own cord and turned its light to full. And I went down the rungs, one at a time, with my hand pressed to my side, into the Fourth Cistern.

* * *

I won't tell you about the cold. I can't. There aren't words for it that you'd believe. I'll tell you that it went through the borrowed coat and the shirt and the skin and into the hole in my side like a hand, and that my heart stopped — I felt it stop, I swear it, for a beat, two — and then started again, very fast and very hard, as if it were trying to get out of me. I'll tell you that the handset's light went about two metres into the black water and stopped, and there was nothing in it at all. No walls. No floor. Just black, and the fine pale drifting specks of three hundred years of silt, turning in my light like stars.

It's twelve metres to the bottom. I know that now. I knew only, then, that the rungs went on down the side of the well and then the well opened out into something much bigger, and that I let go of the last rung and kept going, down, kicking, with my hand on my side and the hood's little bottle hissing at my ear, into the dark.

I found the bottom by hitting it.

Silt. Soft, deep, cold, rising round me in a slow grey cloud that swallowed the light. I put my hands into it. I don't know what I thought I'd find. I don't know what I thought it would look like. I went along the bottom on my knees with my hands in the silt, sweeping, feeling, the way you'd look for a dropped coin in a dark room, and the cloud rose round me and the hood fogged and the bottle hissed and my heart went on trying to get out.

I nearly missed it. I'd gone past it. My left hand touched something hard and straight-edged under the silt and I'd gone on for two more sweeps before I understood what I'd touched and went back.

A sleeve. Lead. Grey, soft, dented, the length of my forearm and a bit more, open at one end, lying in the silt at the foot of a curving chute that came down out of the dark above. I put my hand inside it.

Iron.

* * *

I don't remember getting back up. I remember the rungs. I remember the hood's bottle running out somewhere near the top, the hiss stopping, and tearing the hood off my face in the well with the water up to my chest and breathing the air of the shaft — cold, stale, three hundred years old, the best air I've ever breathed. I remember that the thing in my arms was so heavy I couldn't climb with it and had to hold it against my chest with one arm and climb with the other, one rung at a time, and that the hole in my side was open now, properly, and I could feel it opening with every rung.

I was two rungs from the top when the frigate's salvo hit the spine.

It came through the iron of the well into my hands and my teeth and my spine. A blow so big it wasn't a sound. The whole shaft jerked sideways. The water in the well below me rose up in a black wave and slapped against the walls and fell back. And above me, in the round of white light at the top of the shaft, I saw the great iron disc of the recovery port — the plate I'd heaved up and leaned against the cradle's pedestal — slide. Slowly. Tilt, and slide off the pedestal, and come down across the mouth of the well like a lid on a pot.

It struck the rim. It didn't seat. It struck at an angle and jammed, a hand's breadth open on one side, a crescent of white light the width of my fingers; and the Hulk shuddered again, and something in the floor of the vault above groaned and bent, and the crescent narrowed to the width of a blade.

I put my shoulder to it. I put my back to it. I hung from the top rung with the Book clamped against my chest with one arm and pushed up against the iron with everything I had, and it didn't move. It didn't so much as tremble. A metre of Charter iron, wedged into a frame that the salvo had bent; it had been built to hold against vacuum and fire and a ship breaking up round it. It held.

Then the white light went out. All of it. The vault's lamps, on their own cell, burning for three hundred years.

* * *

That's where I am.

I'm sorry. I should have told you that at the start. I've been telling you all this as if I were somewhere else, somewhere warm, looking back. I'm not. I'm at the top of the well of the Fourth Cistern of the Hulk, in the dark, with my belt looped through the top rung so I won't fall, and about a metre of air between the water and the iron lid, and a hole in my side, and an iron box in my lap. My handset's light is on. It's getting dimmer. It's very cold.

I've said, once or twice, that I've had a long time to think about things since. I have. You'd be surprised how long an hour is, in the dark, with the water ticking. Long enough to go over all of it. Long enough to go over it again and check the figures.

And I've got her key.

I put it in the hasp. I didn't think about it. I sat here in the dark with the Book on my knees and my mother's key in my wet hand, and I turned the Book over until I found the slot — polished steel, a little bright line in the handset's glow — and I put the key in, and it went, all the way, the way a key goes into the lock it was made for. I turned it. And the case unlocked with a deep soft sound, like a door closing somewhere far away.

*The Purser is whosoever holds the Key and the Book together.*

I opened it. The inside of the lid lit up. Blue-white. Columns of tiny writing, trembling a little, as if the light weren't quite sure of itself. Three hundred years in the dark at the bottom of a tank of water and it lit up the moment I opened it, like it had been waiting.

I went to the end. I touched the last entry. And the Book gave me her voice.

I've listened to it four times now. I'm not going to tell you all of it. You'll hear it, I hope, if anyone ever does. I'll tell you that it's a woman's voice, quite low, very level, a clerk's voice — the voice you'd use to read out a bill — and that it doesn't shake. Not on Benn, whoever he was. Not on the captain. Not on *Liberty*, which is the word Edwin Quarry chose, she says, in the captain's day room at the Fork, with a glass of the captain's whisky in front of him. *Call it something they can love.* Not on the part about the person she gave to the Factor to save her sister's life, which she reads out against herself in the same level voice as all the rest. *Let it be written against me. It is owed.*

It doesn't shake on *I discharge every debt written in this Book*.

It doesn't shake on *I vest it in the people of the hold and their heirs, in common, to be held by no one person, and no council, and no house.*

It shakes once. At the very end. On the last word.

*Read aloud under seal, in the Hold, before witnesses. Who were asleep.*

I laughed when I heard that. The first time. I'm sorry; I did. I sat here in the dark at the top of the well with the blood going out of me and I laughed until I had to stop because it hurt. Then I didn't laugh.

*Her name was Annick Vell, and it is written in ash.*

She had a sister. She says so. And there were people sent down to the ice the year of Landfall, the first Struck, whose names nobody wrote; and in Gallows Reach, three hundred years later, a woman put an iron key round her son's neck at the ferry port and said *don't sell it*. I don't know if I'm hers. Her sister's, more likely. Or somebody she trusted with it, somebody she gave it to on the ice. I'll never know. It doesn't matter. *Purser's blood*, they used to say in the Crown, when they wanted to be cruel. I find I don't mind it.

* * *

My handset spoke a little while ago. It shouldn't have; it's iron all round me. But the well has a conduit up through the vault floor, and something must have been carrying, because it spoke — broken, half static — and it was Calla.

Wren is with her mother. Calla put her there herself, in the Clear quarter, and stayed until the door was locked. The frigate's done. She fired on the station with a Concordance verifier aboard, and the escort that had been sitting off our bow all day put two rounds into her drive and she's drifting now, dark, with her crew in the lifeboats. Severine's yacht is gone up the Lane. The Houses' guard in the Crown laid down their arms an hour ago. The transports have closed their hatches. The Hold has the Hulk.

They're singing in the Hall, she said. She held the handset up so I could hear. I heard it, through the static, very far off, the way you'd hear something through ice. Twenty thousand people singing the ice miners' song, the one my mother sang, with the last line the way they sing it in Gallows Reach. *Heave and heave and we'll be free, when the ash is paid, says he.*

Then she said, *Where are you, Fen? We're coming down the spine. Where are you?*

I nearly told her.

I want you to know that. I had my mouth open. And then she said, before I could, in the same breath, the way you'd mention the weather — *we're going to need a council, tomorrow, to sort out who did what. Who fought and who didn't. Who's owed. Somebody's going to have to pay for all this.*

I closed my mouth.

She didn't mean anything by it. I know she didn't. She's a good woman, and she's right, and somebody is. That's the trouble. That's always been the trouble. And I sat here in the dark with Annick Vell's Book on my knees and her voice in my head, *to be held by no one person, and no council, and no house*, and I thought about Lucan Marsh at the end of his belt saying *the only question there's ever been is who holds the pen*; and I thought that if I told Calla Dray where I was, she'd come down the spine with a cutter and lift this lid and find me and the Book, and she'd take it up into the light and hold it. And she'd mean well. And then she'd hold it.

So I said, *Tell Wren I'm sorry about her birthday.*

And she said *Fen*—

And the handset stopped.

* * *

I've made my entry. I'll tell you what it says, in case it's too cold for you to hear when you find it. I said it into the Book with my thumb on the bead of my mother's key, the way the woman before me did. I tried to keep it level.

*Entry. Hollis Fen, Assessor of the Second Rank of the Office of the Account, holding the Key and the Book together. In the well of the Fourth Cistern of the Hulk, over Tamar. Charter Year nine hundred and twenty-nine. Burning Day, the night.*

*I record that the Account of Liberty is false. Its founding page was composed nine years after Landfall, in Tamar water, under the Founders' Council's seal, without a voice. The Bureau of Titles of the Concordance has read it so, by Verifier-Commander Isaak Thorne, and on my sworn lodging has this day declined to verify it.*

*I record that Ottilie Brask, Keeper of the Index, was killed for proving it, by the hand of Bastian Oduya, Assessor, on the order of Lucan Marsh, Chief Assessor, who told me so himself, and is dead in the vault above me by my hand.*

*I record that the Houses of Absalom, by their Speaker Severine Constance, sought to sell the Account and every person written in it to the Ostrava Labor Trust; and that the Speaker has fled this night toward the Lane carrying a copy of it under the Council's seal. Let any court that ever sees that copy know it for a forgery, impeached before the Bureau on this day.*

*I record that I struck three hundred and nine people to the Deep Ice under that Account. Ewan Haskell was the last. Jory Dray was one. Let it be written against me. It is owed.*

*I record that Annick Vell was not a traitor.*

*I don't know what else it's lawful for me to say in here. I'm not a purser. I've got her key and her Book and a hole in my side and not much time. So I'll say this. I have a daughter named Wren. She was sixteen yesterday. By the law of every year but this one she'd be Clear, and by the entry above mine she's been Clear since before she was born, and so has everyone. I'd like her to know that her father knew it, at the end.*

*I'm going to read this aloud. There's no one to hear. The Purser was right about that too.*

And I read it. Aloud. In the dark, with my thumb on the bead, to the water and the iron and the lid. And then I said *sealed*, and it was.

* * *

That's the entry. That's all of it. Everything I've told you, whoever you are — that's what it adds up to. You can't read the bottom line of a ledger until you've written every entry above it, I told you that at the start, and by then of course it's far too late to change any of them. I've written them now. All of them. I've added them up. I didn't like the figure. I worked it out again to check.

It's very cold. The handset's nearly out. I've clipped the Book's strap to my belt so it won't go back down into the water when I let go of it, and I've left the key in the hasp, because that's where it belongs, and because whoever you are, you'll need both.

I'm going to sit here and count for a while. It's what I do. I'll count the rungs, and the drips from the lid, and my breaths, the way the bonded did in the Crossing, she says, to save the air. Four in. Hold. Four out. I'll count until I lose count, and then I'll start again.

I hope you're awake. That's all. Whoever you are, wherever you are when you open this, I hope there are a great many of you, and that you're all awake, and that somebody reads it to you out loud.

Four in.

Hold.
