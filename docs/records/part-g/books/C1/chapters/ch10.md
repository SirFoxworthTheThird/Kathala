# Chapter 10: The Price of a Name

*The Crossing · CR 611*

The proctors' brig was on the second deck of the ring, aft, behind the armoury: six cells in a row along a short corridor, each two metres by one and a half, with a bunk and a drain and a door of steel mesh so that the sergeant on duty could see into all of them at once from her desk at the end. Annick had been there perhaps a dozen times in eleven years, always with her tally, to enter a fine or a confinement. She had never seen more than two of the cells occupied at once.

All six were full.

She took the numbers. Kolar had asked her to, and it was her job, and she had found, standing in the corridor behind Tank Two with the stingers at her sister's neck, that she could think of nothing else to do but her job. So she went down the row of cells with her tally and her stylus, and the proctor on duty opened each mesh door in turn, and she held the tally to each wrist and read the name and spoke the confinement. *Held pending the Factor's inquiry, on suspicion of mutiny.* The gang chief from the coil, Hal Dremmer, with his burned face, who looked at her as she took his wrist as if she were a wall. Two bonded she knew only by their numbers. The electrician, Kofi, who had good ears. Dace.

And the boy with the knife scar.

He was in the last cell. He was lying on the bunk on his side with his knees drawn up, and when the proctor opened the door he did not get up, and Annick saw why. His face had been beaten until it was a different shape. Both eyes were swollen shut. His nose was flat to one side. There was blood dried black in his ears and down his neck and soaked into the collar of his coveralls, and when she knelt by the bunk and took his wrist he made a sound she had never heard a person make, a thin whistling moan through his broken mouth, and tried to pull his hand away.

*Imre, Joss. V5-3011. Bonded. Coil gang.* Nineteen.

"He resisted," said Kolar from the end of the corridor.

Annick spoke his confinement. She did not look round.

"I'll want the doctor for him," she said, when she had finished.

"He can have the doctor in the morning."

"He'll have the doctor now, Sergeant, or I'll enter in the Book that a bonded colonist in your custody was denied medical care contrary to Article Six, and the Factor can explain to the Shale Concern why one of the assets he's selling them arrived with a fractured skull." She stood up. Her voice did not shake. She would hear it in her head later, that level clerk's voice, and wonder at it. "It's billable. They'll deduct it."

Kolar looked at her for a long moment with her face like a fist. Then she nodded to the proctor, who went.

Dace was in the fourth cell, sitting on the bunk with her back to the wall and her knees up and her arms round them. She had a cut over one eye and a bruise darkening along her jaw, and her hair had come loose from its wire, and she watched Annick through the mesh the whole time Annick was taking the other numbers, without moving, without blinking, the way a cat watches through a window.

When the proctor opened her door, she held out her wrist without being asked.

Annick took it. Her sister's skin was cold. She could feel the pulse under her fingers, fast and light, and she held the tally to the number and read it and did not speak at once.

"Go on," said Dace quietly. "Say it."

"Vell, Dace," said Annick. "V5-2231. Held pending the Factor's inquiry, on suspicion of mutiny."

"Sealed," said Dace. And then, lower, so that the proctor at the door could not hear: "Don't you dare. Whatever he asks you. Don't you dare."

* * *

Quarry was waiting in the sergeant's office at the end of the corridor.

It was a small square room with a desk and two chairs and a locker of stingers, and he was sitting in the sergeant's chair with his legs crossed and his soft hands folded on one knee, looking at nothing. He had changed out of his dinner clothes. He wore a plain dark coat now, buttoned to the throat, and he looked, for the first time since Annick had known him, like what he was: a lawyer, at work.

"Sit down, Purser."

She sat.

"You'll have seen what was found," he said. "Forty-one weapons, of one kind or another. Some of them quite ingenious. A store of food taken from the galley over several weeks — I gather it's been coming out in the slab buckets. Eleven coil-gang cutting tools with their safeties removed. A map of the Lantern deck, very good, drawn from memory by someone who'd worked behind the command ring." He paused. "It's a mutiny, Purser. I don't think either of us is going to insult the other by pretending otherwise."

She said nothing.

"Under the Ordinances," said Quarry, "a bonded colonist taken in mutiny against the Company's officers forfeits the protection of the voyage. Article Seventeen. You'll know it." He waited, and when she still said nothing, went on pleasantly. "The debt is called in full. The person is held to be Company property in satisfaction of the debt, and may be disposed of by the Factor as the Company's interest requires. In practice, on a voyage, that means one thing. We haven't room to keep them. We haven't food to feed them. And the Concern does not buy mutineers." He tilted his head. "In practice, it means the forward lock."

"You can't," said Annick.

"I can. I'd rather not. It's wasteful. Every one of those people in the cells is worth something to the Concern, and I'm paid a percentage." He smiled slightly. "But I'll space every one of them before the Wake rotation at the next watch, in front of the rest of the Wake, if I have to. Including the boy. Including your sister. It's the only thing that will stop this. You understand that. You've read the Book; you know what happens on ships where the Factor doesn't." He uncrossed his legs. "Or."

He let it hang.

"Or you can give me a name," he said.

"I don't know any names."

"Of course you don't." Quarry sighed, as if she had disappointed him in some small way. "You've only been in the Hold on eleven nights out of the last fourteen. Sergeant Kolar keeps excellent notes. You've been reconciling the cradle manifests, I understand. Very conscientious." He leaned forward. "I don't care, Purser. I want you to understand that. I don't care what you've been doing in the Hold. I don't care whose side you think you're on. I care that on the day we reach the Fork, there's a purser in the strongroom with the key, who'll seal what I put in front of her; and I care that between now and then this ship stays quiet. Those are the only two things I want. I'll pay a great deal for them."

"And the price is a name."

"Not the boy's. Not one of those six. They're nobody; they're hands. I want a head." He sat back. "Somebody planned this. Somebody chose where to hide forty-one weapons and how to get food out in the slab buckets and who to send behind the command ring with a pencil. Somebody who's still free, and will try again, and will try better next time. Give me that person, and your sister walks out of that cell tonight and goes back to the coil gang and nobody says another word about it. The others too, if you like. I'll be generous. It's a good price."

Annick looked at him. He looked back, pleasantly, his pricing eyes on her face.

"You have until the morning watch," said Edwin Quarry. "Six hours. After that I'll assume you don't know any names after all, and I'll do what the Ordinances require." He stood up and buttoned his coat. "Goodnight, Purser. I'm sorry. I truly am. It's a very hard thing to be asked."

* * *

She went to the strongroom because she did not know where else to go.

She did not turn on the lamp. She floated in the dark beside the cradle with one hand on the Book's iron lid and her back against the cold of the bulkhead — on the other side of which, she knew, was the black water of Tank Four, twelve metres deep, under the ship's shielding — and she did what she had always done when there was a thing she could not bear. She kept accounts.

She began with what she knew.

If she gave no name, Quarry would space six people at the morning watch, and one of them would be Dace. She did not doubt it. She had watched his face. He had not been bluffing, because a man like Quarry did not bluff; he made offers, and if they were refused he did what he had said, so that the next offer would be believed.

If she gave Tobiah Ruck, the rising was over. He was its heart. Without him the three hundred in the Wake would not move, and the ship would go to the Fork and through it to Shale, and the bonded would be sold, and she would enter them one at a time for six to nine years as they died. And Quarry would let Dace go, and Dace would go to Shale, and Dace would die there too, slower.

If she gave Kofi, or Dremmer, or one of the others in the cells, Quarry would know she was lying. They were hands. He had said so.

If she gave Marit Osei—

She stopped.

She made herself go on. That was the discipline of it; that was what Benn had taught her, at that desk, in this room, for eleven years. You did not stop at the line you did not like. You wrote it, and then you wrote the next one.

If she gave Marit, Quarry would have his head. Marit planned. Everyone in the Wake knew it. It was Marit who had chosen the crawlway behind Tank Two, Annick was nearly certain; Marit who had worked out the slab buckets; Marit who had sent Kofi behind the command ring. Quarry would believe it. Quarry would space Marit, and only Marit, and let the others go, and the ship would be quiet. And the rising — the rising would lose its mind but not its heart. Tobiah would still be free. The three hundred would still be there. And the friend on the bridge, whoever it was. In seventeen days the ship would reach the Fork, and there would still be a chance.

Marit was fifty-six. Marit had been an advocate for twenty years. Marit had said to her, in the blue cold among the cradles, *Have you ever, once, in your whole career, kept a Book of what's owed to us?* Marit would understand it, Annick thought. Marit would do the same sum. Marit would get the same answer.

That was what she told herself. She told herself it several times, in the dark, with her hand on the Book. She did not believe it any of the times. She understood perfectly well what she was doing. She was taking the life of a woman who had done nothing to her and putting it in the column against her sister's, and finding her sister's heavier, and calling it arithmetic.

She thought of going down to the Hold. Now, tonight. Finding Marit in her cradle bay and telling her to hide. There were places on the ship a person could hide for seventeen days — in the Hold itself, among the frozen, if you had a cradle tech to help you. She thought it through. Then she thought through what would happen at the morning watch, when Quarry came for his name and there was none, and she understood that there was no version of it in which Marit hid and Dace lived. He had made sure of that. That was what he was good at.

She did not go down to the Hold.

At five hours and ten minutes, by the strongroom clock, she opened the Book.

She did not know why. She had nothing to enter. She turned the key and the page-glass woke, blue-white, trembling, and the voyage's columns filled it, and she scrolled back through them without reading, past the confinements she had entered tonight, past Benn and Emeric Saal and a hundred and forty others, and stopped at an entry from two years ago that she had not thought of since. *Osei, Marit. V5-2207. Bonded. Clerk.* Boarded at Callis. Debt assumed under the guild settlement: thirty-eight thousand marks. Dependents in cradle: *Osei, Abena. V5-2208. Granddaughter. Age seven.*

She touched it. The Book gave her the voice of the clerk who had entered it, a young man from the Callis office who had made the boarding entries and was not aboard. It was a bored voice. It read Marit's name the way you read the name of a crate.

She closed the lid.

At five hours and forty minutes she went back to the brig, and knocked on the door of the sergeant's office, and when Quarry opened it — he had not gone to bed; he was still in his dark coat; he had been waiting — she said it.

She said it level. That was the worst of it. Afterward, of everything, that was what she would remember: that her voice did not shake, not on the name, not on anything. The Book would have kept it if it had. She had always thought there would be some sign.

"Thank you, Purser," said Quarry gently. "I know that was difficult."

* * *

They spaced Marit Osei from the forward lock at the change of the morning watch, in front of the Wake.

Quarry had the whole rotation brought up — two hundred and thirty bonded, out of their bunks and their shifts, marched by proctors down the spine to the forward hub and packed into the long staging bay behind the lock, where the shuttles were loaded at port. They stood shoulder to shoulder in the zero gravity holding the rails and each other, in their dun and their sleeping greys, and the proctors stood along the walls with their carbines, and nobody spoke. The six from the brig were at the front, released, under guard. Dace was among them. Annick saw her and did not look at her again.

She was there because the Ordinances required it. A death in forfeiture was entered by the Purser at the place where it occurred, before witnesses. She stood by the lock with her tally and her stylus, beside Quarry and Kolar and two proctors, and she did not look at anyone.

They brought Marit up from the brig with her hands bound in front of her.

She had been arrested an hour before, in her cradle bay, while she slept. She had not resisted. She was in her sleeping greys, with her locs loose round her shoulders and her reading lenses still pushed up on her forehead, as if she had been interrupted in the middle of something and meant to get back to it. She looked smaller than Annick remembered. She looked, Annick thought, like what she was: a woman of fifty-six who had been woken in the night and told she was going to die.

They put her in the lock. The inner door was open. She stood in it, in the little chamber with the outer door at her back, and looked out at the Wake.

"Has the condemned anything to say?" said Quarry. It was in the Ordinances. He said it as he said everything, pleasantly.

"Yes," said Marit Osei.

She did not raise her voice. She did not need to; the bay was so quiet that every one of the two hundred and thirty could hear her breathe. She looked along the rows of them, slowly, face by face, the way Annick had seen her look at a column of figures.

"They'll make you choose," she said. "Between each other. That's all they've got. That's the only trick they know. They'll put your sister on one side and your neighbour on the other and tell you to pick, and you'll pick, because anyone would, and then they've got you, because you've done their work for them." She paused. "Don't let them. That's all. When they make you choose between each other, don't. Choose them. Choose to make them pay for it." She looked down at her bound hands, and then up again. "I'm not angry with whoever gave them my name. I want you to know that. Whoever you are. They'd have had someone. It may as well be me."

She turned her head. She looked at Annick.

It was not a long look. Perhaps two seconds. There was nothing in it that anyone else in the bay could have read: no accusation, no reproach, nothing at all except a kind of tired attention, the look of someone checking a figure and finding it was what they'd expected. Then Marit Osei said, quite quietly, in a voice meant only for her:

"Write it truly, Purser."

Quarry nodded to Kolar. Kolar touched the panel. The inner door closed.

There was a viewport in the inner door, a square of thick glass, and nobody was made to look through it. Annick looked. It was her job. She had to enter the hour of death, and the manner, and she could not do it truly unless she saw. So she watched through the glass as the light over the outer door went from green to amber to red, and the air went out of the lock in a single white gasp of frost, and the outer door opened on the dark and the dull red coal of Pell, and Marit Osei went out into it with her bound hands in front of her and her locs streaming, turning slowly, small and then smaller, until she was a speck against the coal and then nothing.

Behind her, in the bay, someone began to sing. A man's voice, cracked and low. *Heave the iron, heave it slow.* A proctor said something sharply, and the singing stopped. Then another voice took it up, a woman's, further back. *The Book is heavy, down we go.* And another. And another. They did not sing loudly. They did not need to. Two hundred and thirty people in a steel bay, singing under their breath, without moving, without looking at the proctors, until the proctors stopped telling them to stop.

Annick put her thumb to the bead of her key and made the entry.

"Marit Osei," she said. "V5-2207. Forfeit for mutiny under Article Seventeen. Executed by order of the Factor at the forward lock, at—" she looked at the clock — "six hours and four minutes, ship's time. The balance of her debt, thirty-four thousand two hundred and eleven marks, passes under the Ordinances to her household. To Abena Osei, V5-2208. Her granddaughter."

It shook.

Not much. On *granddaughter* — one word, the last one. A tremor so small that nobody in the bay could have heard it, under the singing. But she heard it, and she knew that the Book had heard it too, and taken it, and grown it into the lattice where it would stay forever: a woman's voice, very level, saying a child's name, and then, at the end, not.

*Write it truly.*

"Sealed," she said.

* * *

They released Dace and the others at the second bell.

Annick did not see it. She was in the strongroom, entering the release, and the order came down from the Factor's office with the six names on it and the word *discharged from confinement* and Quarry's seal, and she sealed it under her own, and that was all. It was a very short entry. It took eleven seconds. She timed it.

Dace found her in the spine an hour later, outside the strongroom door.

She had washed the blood off her face. The bruise along her jaw had come up purple and green. She had tied her hair back with a new length of wire, very tight, and she floated in the corridor with one hand on a rail and looked at Annick, and Annick looked back, and for a long moment neither of them said anything at all.

"They let us go," said Dace.

"Yes."

"All of us. Even Joss. Haddad's got him in the infirmary. He'll keep the eye, Haddad says. Maybe." She swallowed. "Kolar came to the cell and opened it and said *out*. That was all. *Out.* And an hour after they took Marit."

Annick said nothing.

"Why did they let us go, Annick?"

"I don't know."

Her sister's eyes were very dark in the dim corridor. They went over Annick's face slowly, feature by feature, the way they had gone over it through the mesh of the cell door; and Annick held still under them and let them, because there was nothing else she could do.

"Marit looked at you," said Dace. "At the end. I saw. Everybody saw. She looked right at you."

"She looked at everyone."

"She said something to you."

"She said what the condemned always say to the purser. To write it truly." Annick heard her own voice, level, level. "It's a formula. It's in the Ordinances."

"Is it?"

"Yes."

Dace looked at her for a long time. Then she said, very quietly, "I don't believe you." She let go of the rail. "I don't know what I believe. But I don't believe you."

She went. Annick watched her go down the corridor toward the spoke, hand over hand, without looking back; and she understood that she had paid for her sister's life with Marit Osei's, and that the price had been a good one, as Quarry had said, and that what she had bought with it was a woman who would never again believe a word she said.

* * *

She ate in the mess that evening because she had always eaten in the mess, and not eating would have been noticed.

It was very full. The whole Wake seemed to be there, at the long tables in their dun, eating slab and talking low; and when Annick came in with her tray the talk did not stop, exactly, but it changed, the way the sound of the coil changes when a segment closes. She sat at the end of a table by herself. Nobody looked at her. She ate.

Quarry passed behind her on his way out. He did not stop. He leaned down as he passed, as if to pick something up from the deck, and said in his soft voice, very close to her ear, so that nobody else could hear:

"Watch the First Officer, Purser."

Then he was gone.

She sat very still with her spoon in her hand. *Watch the First Officer.* It made no sense. It made a great deal of sense, and she could not see how. She thought of Nadia Szell at the viewport with her back to the room, saying *Ask the doctor*. She thought of the last of the whisky, and *What would you do, Purser, if you could do anything at all?* She thought of a concentrator wrapped in a towel, and a valve with scratches on its casing, and a list that was not long.

She looked up.

Across the mess, at the far table, Tobiah Ruck was sitting with the coil gang. He was not eating. He had his great scarred hands folded on the table in front of him and he was looking at her — not with anger, not with anything she could name, only looking, steadily, across the whole width of the room, the way Marit had looked at her through the lock door. He did not nod. He did not look away. He did not say anything at all.

After a while she looked down at her tray. When she looked up again, he was gone.
