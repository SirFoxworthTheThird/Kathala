# Chapter 16: The Fork

*The Crossing · CR 611*

The Fork was two stars that had been dying together for a billion years.

Annick saw them on the strongroom's little repeater screen as the ship came out of fold: a white dwarf, small and fierce, burning blue-white like the tip of a cutter, and close beside it a swollen red companion whose outer layers it was slowly drinking. Between them, a bridge of glowing gas. It was the only waypoint on the Long Lane with a name that wasn't a number or a person; the old navigators had called it the Fork because from here the Lane split, one branch running down to Shale and the other on to Absalom, and because, Benn had told her once, it looked like a thing you'd eat with. She had thought it was a joke. Looking at it now, the white star with its thread of red fire, she thought it looked like a wound.

*Fold complete. Coil cooling. Six hours to next jump. All hands stand down from fold stations.*

She did not stand down. She unstrapped herself from the purser's fold seat and checked the Book's straps, and then she sat down again and did not move.

The message from the Factor came in the first hour, on the purser's comm, in his soft voice. *Good morning, Purser. I hope the fold was easy. I'll come down at the end of the cool with the order, as we discussed. Five hours and fifty minutes. Do have some breakfast.*

She did not have breakfast. She sat with her back to the cold bulkhead and her knees drawn up and the key at her throat, and listened to the ship.

Ships have a sound. Every purser learns it. Not the hum of the ventilation or the tick of the coil, but under those, a kind of murmur made of everything at once — feet on decks, voices through bulkheads, hatches, pumps, the slow shift of water in the tanks — that tells you, if you've listened to it long enough, whether the ship is well. For eleven years the *Merciful Increase* had murmured at Annick like a sleeper. For the first four hours at the Fork she murmured the same. Then, at four hours and forty minutes, she began to hold her breath.

Annick heard it. She could not have said how. The murmur thinned. The feet on the decks above her stopped moving in their usual patterns. The ventilation went on, and the coil ticked, and the water in Tank Four shifted on the other side of the bulkhead; but the voices were gone, and the hatches had stopped, and there was a silence underneath everything, very faint, like the silence in a room where people are waiting for someone to speak.

At four hours and fifty-one minutes, the Hold began to scream.

* * *

It came through the bulkhead on her right, the one that divided the strongroom from the Hold, and it was not a human sound at first. It was the cradles. Every cradle in the Hold had an alarm, a small sharp two-note tone that sounded when its sleeper's temperature rose outside the cold-sleep band, and Annick had heard one or two of them go off in eleven years, briefly, before a tech came and set them right. Now they were all going. Hundreds of them. A vast shrill pulsing chorus that rose and rose as more of them joined, until it filled the spine and came through the iron into her teeth.

The fast thaw. They had talked about it in the hollow among the cradles, Marit and Tobiah and the cradle techs, in low voices. *It can be done, if you've someone who knows the systems and is willing to kill a few to wake the rest.* Four hundred sleepers, brought up from the cold in twenty minutes instead of the six hours the Charter's medical regulations required. Their hearts would race and stutter. Their blood would thicken. Some of them would fit, and some of them would wake screaming and not stop, and some of them would not wake at all. The rest would come out of their cradles into the blue cold of the Hold confused and naked and shaking, and the techs would put a tool in each of their hands and point them at the hatch.

She sat with her hands pressed flat against the deck and listened to it.

Under the alarms, after a while, there were voices. Shouting. Someone sobbing, close to the bulkhead, on and on. And then a new sound, which she had never heard on a ship in her life and recognised at once: the flat hard crack of a flechette carbine, firing in a closed space, and the long shriek of the needles striking iron.

The proctors had come down to the spine.

*Security. Security.* The ship's voice, everywhere. *Disturbance in the Hold. All proctors to the spine. All crew remain at stations.*

The carbines fired again. And again. Someone screamed — not the confused scream of the thawed; a scream with a name in it — and then there was a sound like a door slamming, very loud, and a roar of voices, many, coming from aft, and the carbines firing faster, and the voices coming on.

Annick got up. She went to the iron door of the strongroom and put both hands on it. Then she threw the bolts.

There were three, on the inside, massive steel tongues that slid into sockets in the frame. They had been put there by the Charter for exactly this — so that a purser might lock herself and the Book in, in the event of a ship being taken, and hold out until she could do what Article Nine required. She had never thrown them. They were stiff. She had to set her feet against the frame and pull with her whole body to move them, and they went home one by one with a sound like the Book's lid closing, deep and soft and final.

Then she went back to the cradle and opened the Book, and turned on the strongroom comm, and waited.

* * *

Kolar came at five hours and four minutes.

Annick heard her before she saw her: the sergeant's voice in the passage outside, flat and hard through her helmet grille, giving orders. Then a fist on the door, three blows.

"Purser."

Annick did not answer.

"Purser. Factor's orders. You and the Book are to come with me to the Factor's cabin for your own protection. Open the door."

"No," said Annick.

There was a pause. Through the iron she could hear the battle going on in the spine — farther off now, aft, toward the coil, and it seemed to her that the carbines were fewer and the voices more. Then Kolar said, quite calmly, "Cutter," to someone beside her, and a moment later the door began to tick.

It was a thermal lance. Annick knew the sound; she had heard the coil gang use them on the housing. A slow deep ticking as the iron took the heat, and then, very faintly, a smell — hot metal, coming through the seams of the door — and on the inside of the iron, in the dim strongroom lamplight, a small dull spot that slowly began to glow.

It was at the height of the middle bolt. They were cutting the bolt.

She watched it. There was nothing else to do. The spot glowed dull red, then brighter. She could feel its heat on her face from two metres away. The Book lay open in its cradle beside her with the page-glass awake and blue and the stylus clipped beside it, and her key was at her throat, and she had no idea at all, for perhaps the first time in her life, what she was going to write.

The comm spoke.

It was Szell. Not her voice on the ship's channel; her voice on the purser's line, the hard line that ran from the Lantern down the spine to the strongroom and nowhere else. Very quiet, very clear, a little out of breath.

"Purser. The Lantern is mine."

Annick put her hand on the bead of the key.

"Orme's on the command ring. The captain's—" a pause, a breath — "the captain's down. There were bonded on the bridge deck. We've got the doors. The Factor's proctors are all in the spine. I need you to seal me. Now. Forty seconds, Annick. Seal me and I'll lock the spine hatches and they're done."

Behind her, the spot on the door was turning orange.

She thought of the captain at his grandfather's table with the decanter two-thirds empty. *Aurelio would have done it. That's why he's dead.* She thought of two blank lines in a maintenance log. *Choose the one who can fly.* She thought of Tobiah in the hollow with his hand on Lise Saal's window. *And I'd still need her to fly.*

The captain was down. There was no one else.

She put her thumb to the bead.

"Entry," she said. Her voice was perfectly level. "Annick Vell, Purser of the *Merciful Increase*, holding the Key and the Book together. Under Article Fourteen, the Master's authority of this ship is vested in Nadia Szell, First Officer, without restriction or countersign. From this hour."

"Sealed," she said, and took her thumb away.

The page-glass flickered. Somewhere far off through the iron and the length of the spine, she felt it go — a faint shudder in the deck, a change in the note of the ventilation, as every lock in the Lantern dropped the old codes and began to search for the new. She counted. She could not help it. One. Two. Three.

At twenty-six the spot on the door went from orange to yellow.

At thirty-one the lance stopped.

She heard Kolar's voice in the passage, sharp, and another voice answering, and then the sergeant shouting something she couldn't make out. Boots. A hatch slamming. And then a new sound, aft, very loud — the great grinding boom of the spine's emergency bulkheads closing, one after another, sealing the spine into sections — and the voices in the passage outside the strongroom rising, confused, as the proctors understood that the doors behind them were shut.

At forty, the comm spoke again.

"Got it," said Nadia Szell. "Thank you, Purser. Stay where you are."

* * *

It was another hour before anyone came.

She sat in the strongroom with the bolts thrown and the Book open and listened to the end of it. It did not take long. The proctors were twenty-four, and the bulkheads had cut them into three groups, and the Lantern held the ship. On the other side of the iron the voices came and went: the proctors shouting at each other, then shouting at someone else, then the carbines firing in short bursts, and voices answering that were not proctors' voices, many of them, getting nearer. A long silence. Someone beating on a hatch. Then, very close outside the strongroom door, a sound she would remember for the rest of her life, though she never told anyone: a woman's voice, Kolar's, saying *no* — just that, *no*, twice, not loudly, in a tone of mild surprise — and then a number of other sounds, wet and heavy, and then nothing at all.

At six hours and twenty minutes, someone knocked on the door. Gently. Three times.

"Annick." It was Haddad. "It's over. You can open it."

She opened it. It took both hands and her feet against the frame for each bolt, and the middle one was so warped from the lance that she had to strike it with the strongroom's fire extinguisher to move it, and when she finally swung the door open the doctor was standing in the passage outside with blood on his hands to the elbows and on the front of his coat and in his hair, and his embarrassed face was grey.

"Dace," she said.

"Alive." He caught her arm before she could move. "Alive. A needle in the shoulder and one along the ribs. She lost a lot of blood. I've packed it. She's in the infirmary." He held on. "Annick. Joss Imre's dead."

She looked past him down the passage.

They had laid them along the wall. She did not count them; she would count them later, in the Book, every one. Bonded in dun, and bonded in nothing at all, naked from the cradles, their skin still grey and wet with gel. Proctors in grey. Two crew. At the near end, where the passage turned toward the Hold, Vashti Kolar lay on her back with her helmet off and her pale cropped hair stuck to her forehead, and her face like a fist was not like anything now. Someone had closed her eyes.

Joss Imre was a little way along. The boy with the knife scar. Haddad had saved the eye; she could see that. It was closed.

"There's a lot of them from the thaw," said Haddad quietly. "Forty-one. Their hearts. I couldn't—" He stopped. "There'll be more. Some of them are still fitting."

"Where's Tobiah?"

"Lantern. They all are. Ruck and the First Officer and the rest." He looked at her. "Master Szell sent word. She wants the Purser and the Book in the captain's day room."

"The captain."

"Dead. On the bridge deck. Shot." Haddad wiped his hands on his coat, uselessly. "There were bonded up there. They came through the forward spoke while Szell's people were taking the Lantern. There was fighting on the deck outside the day room. He came out to stop it." He did not look at her. "That's what they're saying."

"And the Factor?"

"Alive. They took him in his cabin. He didn't resist." The doctor's mouth moved. "He was dressed for it. He had his good coat on."

* * *

She carried the Book to the Lantern herself.

She would not let anyone else touch it. Two bonded with carbines took from proctors fell in on either side of her at the spoke — young men she did not know, still shaking, who looked at her with a kind of awe — and she let them, and they went forward up the spine past the bodies and the scorched bulkheads and the great Hold hatch, where the alarms were silent now and the cradle techs were moving among the racks with lamps, slowly, checking the ones that had not woken. She held the Book against her chest in both arms. In zero gravity it had no weight. It had never felt so heavy.

The forward hub was crowded. Bonded and crew together, in a strange uneasy mixture, standing about the passage outside the Lantern with weapons and without them, talking low; and as Annick came through with the Book in her arms they fell quiet and made room for her, and she heard someone say *the Purser*, and someone else say *she sealed it, she held the door and sealed it*, and a hand touched her shoulder as she passed, briefly, the way you touch someone for luck.

The day room door was half open. She stopped outside it to shift the Book in her arms. And through the gap, before she had decided to listen, she heard Edwin Quarry say:

"— need a legal basis. That's all I'm telling you. You can't simply announce it."

She stood very still.

"The patent," said Quarry's soft voice. "That's the whole of it. The Charter's patent for the Absalom system is recorded in the Book. At Landfall it vests in the colony — in whoever the Book names as holding it for the colony. If you want to hold this world, Master Szell, you need the Purser to record a council as trustee of that patent. *The Founders*, call it. Something with a ring. You and Mr Ruck and whoever else you need to keep happy."

"And the debts?" Szell's voice.

"Burn them." Quarry, pleasantly. "Burn the Book. Publicly, at Landfall, in front of everyone. They'll weep. They'll worship you for it. You'll never in your life have a better day." A pause; the clink of glass. "And then, when they've stopped weeping, you raise an account. Not the Charter's debts. Nothing like it — you're merciful; you've burned all that. Something smaller. Something that sounds fair. What each of them owes for the ship that carried them, and the air, and the tools, and the stores, and the work of the people who'll keep them alive on that ice for the next fifty years. You'll need it. You'll need some way of deciding who works and who eats, and nothing works like a debt. Call it something they can love." He seemed to think. "Liberty. Call it Liberty. *What do we owe? We owe our Liberty.*"

There was a silence.

"They'll never accept a debt," said Tobiah Ruck.

It was the first time she had heard his voice since the hollow. It was very tired.

"They'll accept anything after a fire," said Quarry. "Trust me. I've been doing this for thirty years."

The silence went on. Annick stood outside the door with the Book against her chest and did not breathe.

"Somebody's got to pay for the air," said Tobiah at last, quietly. Not to anyone. As if to himself. "That's the trouble. That's always been the trouble. She said it to me — the Purser. *How will you feed them? Who'll decide?* I told her we'd work it out." A long breath. "Somebody's got to pay for the air."

"Then we're agreed," said Nadia Szell.

"And the Purser?" said Tobiah.

"She'll seal what you put in front of her," said Quarry. "She sealed the Master for you today, didn't she? She's very good. She gave me Osei for her sister, without a tremor. She'll give you this."

"Leave her sister out of it."

"I'm only telling you what she is."

"I know what she is," said Tobiah Ruck. His voice did not rise. "I've known since the brig. Leave her sister out of it."

Another pause. Then Szell, lightly, the way she had said *I liked Benn* in the Lantern: "We'll need her willing. Not frightened. Frightened clerks make mistakes. The old man would never have sealed any of it, Edwin, you know that. He'd have sat in this room and said no until we were all dead of old age. I made sure he'd never be asked."

"I wondered," said Quarry softly.

"Did you."

"I wondered why you were so very sure, at Pell, that the new purser would be easier." The clink of glass again. "I'm grateful. It's made my life a great deal simpler."

Tobiah said nothing. Annick waited for him to say something. She waited for what felt like a very long time, outside the half-open door, with the hub murmuring behind her and the Book's iron edge pressing into her arms. He did not say anything at all.

"And Lindqvist," said Quarry.

"Caspar did it cleanly," said Szell. "On the deck outside. There were bonded right there — they'd come through the spoke. Nobody will ever think anything else." A pause. "He'd have handed them all to the Receivers' court with an apology. I told the Purser so."

* * *

She went in.

She did not knock. She pushed the door open with her shoulder and went in with the Book in her arms, and they all turned.

Szell was in the captain's chair at the head of his grandfather's table, in her First Officer's coat with the collar open and a smear of something dark along her jaw. Quarry sat at her right hand in his good coat, unbound, with a glass of the captain's whisky in front of him and his soft hands folded. Tobiah stood at the viewport, where Szell had stood the day Annick was made purser, enormous against the white star and its thread of red fire, with a proctor's carbine slung over his shoulder and blood on his sleeve. There were two others she did not know well — a bonded woman from the coil gang and Lieutenant Orme, a lean fair officer with very still hands — and there was the decanter, on the table, nearly empty, in the ring it had left on the wood.

"Purser," said Nadia Szell, and stood, and smiled her warm surprising smile. "Well done."

Tobiah crossed the room in two strides and put his arms round her.

He did it carefully, as he did everything, so as not to crowd; he put his great arms round her and the Book together and held them, briefly, and she felt his chin rest for a moment on the top of her head. He smelled of blood and cordite and the cold of the Hold. "You held the door," he said into her hair. "Haddad told me. You held it with the lance coming through and you sealed it. Thank you. Thank you."

She said nothing.

He let her go. He looked down at her, and his broken face was wet, and she could not tell — she would never be able to tell, not then and not in all the years afterward — whether it was for the dead in the passage, or for her, or for what he had just agreed to on the other side of the door.

"Sit down, Annick," said Szell. "You must be exhausted. Edwin, pour the Purser a drink."

Quarry poured. He held the glass out to her across the table with a small polite inclination of his head, the way he had held out the film with the Receivers' order on the day she was made purser, and his pricing eyes went over her face, and she saw him see that she had heard.

He said nothing. He only smiled very slightly and set the glass down in front of her chair.

She did not sit. She set the Book on the captain's grandfather's table, beside the decanter, in a patch of white light from the dying star, and stood back from it.

"We'll jump for Absalom as soon as the coil allows," said Szell. "Six hours. The Absalom branch is two jumps; three weeks, perhaps less. And then we'll be home." She lifted her own glass. "There's a great deal to decide. We'll need you, Purser. For all of it. There's no one else who knows the Book."

"No," said Annick. "There isn't."

Szell looked at her for a moment with her dark considering eyes. Then she nodded, as if a reading had come back as she expected, and drank.

Outside the viewport, very slowly, the Fork began to turn. Annick felt it through the deck: the thrusters firing, the long body of the ship coming about on its axis, away from the branch that ran down to Shale. The white star and its red companion slid across the glass, and out of it, and were gone, and there was nothing beyond the viewport but the thin stars of the Long Lane and, somewhere ahead of them, too far to see, a gas giant and a moon made of ice.

Annick stood by the table with her hands at her sides and the key lying cold at her throat. She did not drink. She looked at the Book in its patch of light, and at the people round it, and at the ring the captain's decanter had left on his grandfather's wood. And in her head, where she had kept her other accounts for eleven years, she began without meaning to on a new page; and found, for the first time in her life, that she knew exactly what she was going to write on it, and that there was nobody in the world she could tell.
