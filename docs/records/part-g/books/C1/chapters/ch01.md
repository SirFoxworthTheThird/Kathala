# Chapter 1: What Is Written

*The Crossing · CR 611*

The man's name was Emeric Saal, and he had eleven thousand four hundred and six marks left to pay when the coil housing closed on him.

Annick Vell knew the figure before she knew his face. It came up on her tally as she pulled herself down the aft gallery hand over hand, the alarm still whooping in the spine, and she read it the way she read everything, quickly, with her mouth shut. *Saal, Emeric. V5-1180. Bonded, Wake rotation thirty-one. Coil gang. Balance outstanding: 11,406 m. Dependents: Saal, Lise, V5-1181 (cradle). Saal, Tam, V5-1182 (cradle). Saal, Orrin, V5-1183 (cradle).*

Three cradles. She put the tally in her coat and kept climbing.

The fold coil of the *Merciful Increase* was a ring of black segments forty metres across, and after a jump it was the hottest thing for a light-year in any direction. It bled heat for weeks. To help it, the segments of its housing rolled slowly open and shut like the gills of something enormous, venting into radiator fins, and in the hours after each opening the coil gang went in with scrapers to clear the crust of coil-salt that grew on the inner faces. It was not complicated work. It was the kind of work you gave to people whose time was cheaper than a machine's.

The segment had closed early. That was all. A relay had stuck and then unstuck, and a housing plate weighing four tonnes had swung back into place with Emeric Saal's upper body between it and its seat.

There were twenty of the gang in the gallery, holding the rails, their dun coveralls dark with sweat. The air back here was hot and tasted of metal. Two proctors in grey kept the hatchway, stingers clipped to their belts and their visors up because of the heat. The doctor was already at the plate with a pry bar he had no hope of using. Yusuf Haddad was a narrow, tidy man who looked, in any emergency, slightly embarrassed for everyone involved. He glanced up at her and shook his head once.

"They're bringing the jack," he said. "There's no hurry for it."

Saal's legs hung out from under the edge of the plate. One boot had come off and floated a little way off, turning slowly, and nobody had caught it. On his left wrist, where his sleeve had ridden up, Annick could see the edge of his number in faded blue and, above it, the band of his air meter. The meter's little window was dark. It had stopped counting when he stopped breathing. She noticed, because it was her job to notice, that it had stopped on 4,113 litres for the shift.

She took out her tally and her stylus.

The gang watched her do it. She felt them watching the way she felt cold through a suit: a pressure, a temperature, not anything she could argue with. She had grown up in the stacks of Callis with people like these, had eaten what they ate, had been, for the first seventeen years of her life, one of them. On the ship they had a name for her. They said it now, somewhere at the back, low enough that she could choose not to hear it.

*Ink.*

"Time of death," she said to Haddad.

"Call it eleven forty ship. Crush asphyxia, I'd guess, before the rest of it. It would have been quick."

"Would it."

"No," said Haddad, after a moment. "But you can write that it was."

She wrote eleven forty. She wrote *accident in the course of labour*. She wrote the plate's segment number and the name of the gang chief, a big Callisian with a burned face who was holding the rail beside her with both hands and staring at the boot.

Then she came to the part she had to say aloud.

Field entries were provisional. The binding record was made in the Book itself, under the key, and spoken there; but the Charter liked its pursers to announce a transfer at the place where it occurred, before the people it concerned, so that no one could say afterward that they had not been told. It was meant as a courtesy. Annick had long ago stopped trying to decide what it was.

"Emeric Saal," she said, "V5-1180, is dead in the course of labour. His balance of eleven thousand four hundred and six marks passes under the Ordinances to his household. To Lise Saal, his wife. To Tam Saal and Orrin Saal, his children." She did not raise her voice. She did not need to; the gallery was very quiet now, with the coil ticking as it cooled. "The transfer will be sealed in the Book at the end of the watch. Any person who disputes it may come to the purser's office before then."

Nobody would come. Nobody ever came.

She was turning away when a voice said, clear and flat from among the gang, "Will you tell them?"

Annick knew the voice. She would have known it in a storm. She looked along the rail and found her sister holding a scraper against her chest like a shield, a smear of coil-salt across one cheek, her dark hair tied back with a twist of wire.

"They're asleep," said Dace. "The wife and the boys. Will you go down to the hold and tell them? Or will they wake up at Absalom owing it and find out from the bill?"

The proctor at the hatch shifted. The gang chief said, not looking around, "Leave it, Vell."

"I'm asking the purser a question."

"The assistant purser," said Annick, "will enter the transfer at the end of the watch." It came out exactly as level as she meant it to. "The household will be informed at waking, in the usual way."

Dace looked at her for a long moment. Then she turned back to the plate and put her hand flat on it, beside the dead man's leg, the way you would put your hand on an animal to calm it.

Annick went back up the gallery and did not count her breaths until she was out of sight. Then she counted them, in, out, the way she had counted them as a child in the stacks when the air was bad and the meters on the walls ran red: four in, hold, four out. It did not help. It never had. It was only something to do with the part of her that wanted to do something else.

* * *

The strongroom of the *Merciful Increase* sat in the spine of the ship, in the long weightless core that ran from the Lantern at the bow to the coil at the stern, and it sat there for a reason. The Book must be near the people it binds. That was the Charter's phrase, written into the architecture of every transport the Company had ever built, and Annick suspected that whoever first wrote it had meant it as a piety and whoever first built it had understood it as a convenience. The strongroom was a bulkhead away from the Hold, where three thousand six hundred and twelve bonded colonists lay frozen in their cradles. On the far side of it, beyond a thickness of shielding, was Ballast Tank Four, one of the four great water tanks that wrapped the spine like the segments of a seed and gave the ship its mass and its protection against the radiation of the dark.

She went in through the iron door. Over the lintel, in letters of Charter red as high as her hand, someone long ago had painted WHAT IS WRITTEN IS OWED. The paint had flaked at the corners and been touched up, flaked and been touched up, so that the letters had a slightly swollen look, like the knuckles of an old man.

The old man himself was at the cradle, coughing.

Aurelio Benn had been purser of the *Merciful Increase* for twenty-eight years and four voyages. He was seventy-one, and something was wrong with his lungs that Haddad had a name for and no cure for, and he wore an oxygen line looped over his ears and down to a concentrator clipped at his belt. In zero gravity his white hair stood out around his head like the down of a seed. He had a long, mournful, humorous face, and he was, as far as Annick had been able to discover in eleven years as his clerk and then his assistant, the only honest man in the employ of the Meridian Charter Company. She did not hold it against him. He had been honest for so long that it had worn smooth, like a step.

"Saal," he said, when he could. "I heard."

"Yes."

"Did you announce?"

"Yes."

He looked at her over the cradle. "Your sister was on the coil gang."

"Yes."

"Ah," said Benn, and left it there, which was one of the things she loved him for.

The cradle was a padded iron bracket on a pedestal, sized to hold one object and hold it absolutely still. The object it held was the Book.

It was an iron case forty centimetres by thirty by twelve, heavy enough in gravity that she had to lift it with both arms. On its cover was the Charter's sunburst over a pair of scales, worn nearly flat at the high points by a century of hands. Its hasp was a slot of polished steel. Inside it, protected by the iron and by a cushion of grey foam, was the lattice: a block of grown crystal the colour of weak tea, in which every debt of every person aboard was inscribed atom by atom and could not be scraped out again. The Book did not forget. That was its whole purpose. A ledger on paper could be burned, a ledger in a machine could be wiped, but a lattice grown under seal held its entries the way a tree holds its rings, and like a tree's rings they could be read by anyone who knew how, and dated, and trusted.

On the wall to the left of the cradle hung a brass plaque engraved with the Purser's Ordinances, twenty-two articles in a cramped legal hand. To the right was the dead shelf, where the Books of the ship's four previous voyages lay strapped in a row, identical to this one in every respect except that their hasps had been closed with lead and their cover plates stamped VOYAGE I, VOYAGE II, VOYAGE III, VOYAGE IV. Their debts were all discharged or transferred, their ships' companies long since landed and dispersed across the colonies of the Charter, and the Books were kept only because the law said a Book could not be destroyed. Below the shelf, set into the wall, was the privy box — a little steel safe a hand's breadth square, which opened to the purser's key and nothing else, and in which, Benn said, generations of pursers had kept their wills, their letters and on one occasion a bottle of Callisian brandy that he had been too frightened of to drink. In the floor beside the pedestal was a square hatch with a red lever and a seal-wire looped through it.

Annick took the key out of her collar.

It was iron, too, a long old-fashioned key the length of her palm, its bit cut in an intricate pattern of notches, with a bead of lattice set into the bow like a dull eye. Stamped on the shank, very small, was the number 0447. It was the assistant purser's key — Benn wore the master on a cord around his neck — but the Book did not care which of the two opened it. It cared only that the key was a purser's key. Whose hand turned it was, by the first of the Ordinances, beside the point.

She set the bit into the hasp and turned it. The case unlocked with a deep, soft sound, like a door closing somewhere far away. She opened the lid. Across the inside of it, the page-glass woke and filled with the dense columns of the voyage's entries, blue-white, faintly trembling, as though the light were not quite sure of itself.

"Speak it," said Benn.

She put her thumb to the bead and spoke it. She gave the date, the hour, her name and her office. She gave Emeric Saal's name and number and the manner of his death. She gave the balance, and the household, and the names of the children, and she said that under the Ordinances the debt now passed to them in equal parts and would bear interest at the rate of the voyage from this hour. She said it all in the same voice she had used in the gallery, and as she spoke the page-glass inscribed it in neat columns, and the lattice took it, and somewhere in the depths of the crystal a pattern grew that would outlast every person who had ever heard of Emeric Saal.

"Sealed," she said, and took her thumb away.

"Play it back."

She did not want to. She touched the glass and the Book gave her own voice back to her, clear, a little thin with the small speaker:

*— to Tam Saal and Orrin Saal, his children, in equal parts —*

It did not shake. She listened for it, and it did not.

Benn listened too, his head tilted. "Do you know why the Book keeps the voice?" he said. "I've told you."

"So that the record can be heard in court."

"That's what the clerks' manual says. It's not why." He coughed, and waited, and went on. "A hand can be forced. You can hold a man's hand and make it sign. But you cannot make a voice tell the truth in the way it sounds. A voice shakes. That's why the Book keeps it. So that someday, someone listening can tell." He smiled at her with his long mournful face. "Yours didn't shake."

"Is that good?"

"I don't know," said Benn. "Is it?"

She closed the lid. The page-glass went dark, and she took the key from the hasp, and the Book was only a block of iron again.

"Recite," said Benn.

It was a habit of his, older than her time with him; he said his own purser had made him do it every watch for nine years, and he had resented it for eight and a half. She hooked her foot under the rail beside the plaque so as not to drift and recited, without looking at it, the articles he cared about. The clerks' manual taught four of the twenty-two. Benn had made her learn them all, and then he had made her learn which ones mattered.

"Article One. The Purser is whosoever holds the Key and the Book together."

"Why together?"

"Because a key without the Book is a piece of iron, and a Book without the key is a locked box. Only the two together make the office."

"Go on."

"Article Four. What is written under seal is owed."

He nodded at the door, at the letters she could not see from here. "Go on."

"Article Nine. Should the vessel be taken by any party not lawfully in command, the Purser shall commit the Book to the ballast, that it be not suborned."

"The drop," said Benn, and looked at the red lever with something like affection. "Every Charter hull has one. A lead sleeve, a chute, and twelve metres down to the bottom of Tank Four. Pirates used to take ships and make the purser write them in as owners. So the Charter decided that if the ship were ever taken, the Book would go into the water and stay there, and whoever had taken her would have a ship and no title to it." He wheezed. "No purser's used one in a hundred years. Pirates found it cheaper to buy ships than steal them. Go on."

"Article Eleven. An entry read aloud under seal before witnesses stands as the Book itself."

"Which means?"

"That a purser can read out what the Book says, and if enough people hear it and it's sealed when it's read, it counts even if the Book is lost."

"It was meant for wrecks. A purser in a lifeboat with the key and no Book, swearing to what was in it. It never worked very well. Witnesses forget. Go on."

"Article Fourteen. The Master's authority is that which the Book records."

"And so?"

"And so the ship obeys the Master recorded in the Book. The Lantern's locks are keyed to the entry. A captain the Book does not name cannot fly her."

"Twenty."

"A debt discharged under seal is discharged forever, against all heirs and assigns."

"Which no purser of the Meridian Charter Company has ever done," said Benn, "except by payment. Good." He closed his eyes. "That'll do. You don't need the other seventeen tonight. They're mostly about fees."

She unhooked her foot and drifted a little toward the door. Behind her, Benn had begun coughing again, a long dry rattle that went on and on. She waited it out. When it was done he said, with his eyes still closed, "She's angry with you."

"She's angry with everyone."

"No," said Benn. "Just you. You're the one she expected something of." He opened his eyes. "Go and eat. You've had nothing since the alarm. I'll close."

* * *

She did not eat. She had got as far as the spoke lift to the ring, where the gravity of the spinning habitat would come up under her feet like the floor of a slow elevator and she would be able to sit down at a table and look at a bowl of slab without having to hold it, when the ship's voice spoke in every corridor at once.

*Courier contact. Courier contact. Purser's office to the Lantern.*

It was a sound she had heard four times in eleven years. The *Merciful Increase* was eight jumps out from Callis on the Long Lane to Absalom, drifting at the dim waypoint called Pell while the coil cooled, and no ordinary signal could catch a ship between fold points. Only a courier could: a fold-runner, a thing like a black spearhead three metres long, all drive and no room, launched from a Charter port with its little sealed packet and a navigational solution that would bring it out of fold within a few thousand kilometres of a ship whose position it had been given weeks before. They were expensive. They were dangerous. The Charter sent them for one of two reasons, and the second reason was war.

She turned around in the spoke and went forward.

The Lantern was the bridge of the ship, and it was named for the dome of armoured glass that capped the bow, so that the officers on watch sat in a bowl of light with the whole sky above them. At Pell there was not much sky to see. The waypoint's brown dwarf hung off to port like a coal at the end of a fire, a dull red disc that gave less light than the instruments, and beyond it the stars of the Long Lane were thin and few. The courier had come out of fold four hundred kilometres off the starboard bow and was closing on its own small fire. She could see it from the hatch: a spark moving against the dark.

There were six people on the Lantern's watch. Three of them mattered.

Captain Harrow Lindqvist stood at the rail of the command ring with his hands behind his back. He was sixty, grey all over — grey hair, grey stubble, a grey uniform coat worn to the threads at the cuffs — and he had the stillness of a man who had learned to save his strength for things he could change. He nodded at her as she came in, which was more than many captains would have done for an assistant purser.

Beside him, holding a stanchion with one hand and a slate with the other, was the First Officer. Nadia Szell was forty-five and looked younger, a compact woman with close-cropped black hair and eyes the colour of strong tea. She was the best pilot Annick had ever seen. The crew said she could thread a fold point with her eyes shut and probably had, and they said it with the particular mixture of pride and unease that crews reserve for officers who are better than the ship deserves. She did not nod at Annick. She looked at her, steadily, as if Annick were an instrument whose reading she was not sure of.

The third was Edwin Quarry, Factor of the Meridian Charter Company, who was not an officer of the ship at all.

Every Charter transport carried a Factor, as every Charter transport carried a purser, and the two offices were meant to watch each other. The purser kept the Book. The Factor kept the Charter's interest — its contracts, its instructions, its law. He commanded the ship's twenty-four proctors. He could countermand the Master on any matter touching the Company's property, and on a colony transport the Company's property included everything aboard that could be sold, which was everything. Quarry was fifty, tall, beautifully barbered, with a soft voice and soft hands and a habit of looking at people as if he were pricing them. He was looking at Annick like that now.

"The purser's not with you," he said.

"Purser Benn is closing the Book, Factor. I have the office's seal." She held up her slate. "Courier receipt must be logged before the packet is opened."

"Then log it."

She did. The Charter's procedures were precise about couriers, because couriers were the only way the Company could speak to its ships, and the Company did not like to be misquoted. A dock tender went out on a line and brought the runner in, its black skin still smoking with fold-frost, and two crew in hard suits cracked the packet housing in the lock and carried in a sealed cylinder the size of a forearm. Annick recorded the hour of contact, the runner's serial, the integrity of the seal. She photographed the cylinder. She put her thumb to her slate and attested that the seal was unbroken at receipt, and the slate passed the attestation along a line to the Book in the spine, where it would be grown into the voyage's annex. Then she held out the cylinder to the captain.

"Factor," said Lindqvist, without moving.

Quarry took it. The seal was a Charter seal, a disc of lattice the size of a coin, and it opened to the Factor's thumb. Inside was a single sheet of film and a smaller sealed packet, which Quarry put away in his coat without looking at it. He unrolled the film and read it.

Annick watched him read. She had watched a great many people read things that changed their lives. It was part of her work: the moment the bill was presented, the moment the transfer was explained, the moment a man understood what his father had left him. People's faces did not do what you expected. They did not fall or crumple. Mostly they became very still, and something behind the stillness began to move very fast.

Quarry's face became very still. Then he smiled slightly, the way a man smiles who has been told something he has been expecting for a long time, and handed the film to the captain.

Lindqvist read it. He read it twice. Annick watched him reach the bottom and go back to the top, and then he did a thing she had never seen him do: he sat down. There was a seat at the command ring that captains used only in fold, and he lowered himself into it as if it were in gravity and his knees had gone.

Nadia Szell had not taken her eyes off Annick. She did not look at the film at all. She looked at the assistant purser, who was looking at the captain, and when Annick turned and met her gaze, the First Officer's mouth moved very slightly at one corner. It was not a smile. It was the expression of someone noting a reading, and filing it.

"Thank you, Assistant Purser," said Quarry gently. "That will be all."

* * *

Dace was waiting for her at the spoke.

She was off shift. She had washed the coil-salt off her face but not quite off her neck, and she had changed into the grey knit that bonded wore for sleeping, and she was sitting on the lip of the lift hatch with her feet hooked under the rail, the way they used to sit on the edge of the stack roofs on Callis and look at the lights of the port. She was twenty-two. Annick had been twelve when she was born and fifteen when their mother died, and for two years after that she had been the only thing between Dace and the stacks, and then she had taken the Charter's clerk bond and gone into the Company's service and left Dace with an aunt and a promise. She had sent money every month for fifteen years. She had been very careful about it. She had kept a ledger.

"What was it?" said Dace.

"I can't tell you that."

"A courier. Everyone heard. What was it?"

"Packets are for the Master and the Factor. I don't read them."

"No. You just write down that they came." Dace pushed off the hatch rim and drifted closer, catching the rail beside her. Up close she smelled of the bonded quarters — of too many people, and the cheap soap, and the faint chemical sweetness of the cradle-gel that never quite washed out of anyone who had been frozen. "You wrote his children into the Book."

"I recorded a transfer."

"Two boys. One's six. I asked the gang chief. He's six, Annick. He's lying in a box in the hold with frost on his eyelids and he owes the Company four thousand marks he didn't owe this morning, and when he wakes up at Absalom somebody's going to hand him a bill with his father's name on it and that's how he'll find out."

"That's the law."

"I know it's the law. You're the law. That's what I'm saying."

Annick said nothing. There was nothing to say that she had not already said, years ago, in the cramped office on the Callis docks where she had found Dace's name on the voyage manifest — *Vell, Dace. Bonded. Passage to Absalom on credit* — and understood, too late, that the money she had sent every month for fifteen years had not been enough, that the aunt had died owing, that the debt had come down to Dace as debts did, and that her sister had looked at the stacks and the debt and the long grey future of them and chosen instead to sign herself onto a ship going to the end of the known world. The ship her sister worked on. Annick had gone to the Factor's office that afternoon with her savings. It had been two hundred marks short of the buy-out. It had been two hundred marks short, and her own wages were bonded to the Company for another nine years and could not be borrowed against, and she had gone back to her desk and entered the manifest and sealed it.

"You could have told me not to," Dace had said, the first time they spoke aboard.

"You'd have done it anyway."

"You don't know that. You never asked."

They had had the same conversation, in different words, perhaps twenty times since. They were having it again now, without saying any of it.

"Go to sleep, Dace," said Annick.

Her sister looked at her for a long moment. Then she said, in a different voice, quite quietly, "What was on the courier? Just tell me if it's bad."

Annick thought of the captain sitting down.

"I don't know," she said, which was true, and then, because it was also true and she was tired, "Yes. I think so."

Dace nodded slowly, as if this were what she had expected. She let go of the rail and drifted back toward the hatch. At the rim she stopped and said, without turning, "There's a song the coil gang sings. Did you know? When they're scraping. *Heave the iron, heave it slow, the Book is heavy, down we go.*" She said it rather than sang it; Dace had never been able to carry a tune. "They sang it for Emeric after you left. While they were jacking the plate. Thought you'd want to know. For your records."

* * *

Benn came back to his cabin in the ring at four in the morning, ship's time. Annick knew because she was awake. Her cabin was beside his — the purser's office had two berths, a desk and a cupboard of forms, on the ring's third deck between the paymaster and the chaplain who had died at Callis and not been replaced — and the bulkhead was thin, and she had been lying on her bunk in the ring's light gravity listening to the hum of the ventilation and counting.

She heard his door. She heard him sit down on his bunk, heavily, and the click of his concentrator, and then for a long time nothing at all. Then he knocked on the bulkhead, twice, the way he did when he wanted her and did not want to shout.

He was sitting with his elbows on his knees and his oxygen line in his hand, as if he had forgotten what it was for. In the gravity of the ring he looked older; his hair lay flat, and his shoulders were an old man's shoulders. He had been in the captain's day room for five hours.

"Shut the door," he said.

She shut it.

"Meridian is gone."

She stood in the middle of the tiny cabin and waited for the rest of it. It did not come at once. Benn put the line back over his ears, carefully, and took two breaths from it, and looked at the floor.

"The Bank of Vey called its loans," he said. "All of them. Six weeks ago, or seven — the packet's dated from Callis. The Charter couldn't pay. There was a run. There's been — I don't know what there's been. Riots at the port. A Receiver appointed. The Company's in liquidation, Annick, and everything it owns belongs to the Receivers of Vey until they've sold it, and they intend to sell it."

"Everything it owns," said Annick.

"Yes."

The ship. The cargo. The Book, and everything written in the Book. Three thousand six hundred and twelve debts in the hold and two hundred and forty awake, and the debts of the dead passing to the sleeping.

"What do they want us to do?"

Benn was silent for so long that she thought he had not heard her. When he spoke again his voice had changed. It was the voice he used in the strongroom when he made her recite: patient, insistent, a teacher's voice.

"They will want me to seal something," he said. "I won't."

"Seal what?"

"I'm not going to tell you. Not tonight. Not until I have to." He looked up at her. "I'm seventy-one, Annick. I have perhaps a year left in me with this—" he lifted the line — "and I'd rather spend it saying no than saying yes. It's a luxury. I've had very few of them. Let me have this one for a day or two before you know about it."

"And if they don't take no?"

"Then they'll come to you."

"Why would they come to me?"

He looked at her steadily. It was a look she had seen him give to bonded colonists at the moment of the bill — not unkind, not anything, only clear.

"Because you're the assistant purser," he said, "and there are two keys, and I'm old. And because you're ballast-born, and your sister is in the Wake, and the Factor has known both of those things since the day you came aboard." He let her take that. "When they come to you — and they may not; perhaps I'm wrong, perhaps I'll live forever out of spite — I want you to remember the Ordinances. All of them. Not just the four they teach clerks. Not just the ones they'll quote at you."

"I know them."

"You know the words. Remember what they're for." He lay back on the bunk with a grunt and closed his eyes. "Go to bed. I'm sorry. I shouldn't have told you at four in the morning. I wanted to tell someone, and you were awake."

"How did you know I was awake?"

"Because you're always awake," said Benn, with his eyes closed. "You lie in there counting. I can hear you through the wall."

She went back to her own bunk. She lay down. The ring turned, and the gravity held her, and the ventilation hummed, and the ship drifted at Pell with the coil ticking as it cooled and the courier's empty hull clamped to its side like a tick. Somewhere far below her in the spine, in the dark beside the Hold, the Book lay in its cradle with Emeric Saal's children newly written into it, and three thousand six hundred and twelve people slept on in their cradles with frost on their eyelids, owing.

She counted. Four in. Hold. Four out. She lay there and kept her accounts, as she had kept them every night for eleven years, the shadow ledger she carried in her head and had never written anywhere: the names of everyone she had entered dead. She added Emeric Saal to the bottom of it. Then, because she could not help it, she lay awake and wondered what it was that the courier had asked for that would make a captain sit down, and a Factor smile, and an old man who had said yes for twenty-eight years decide to say no.
