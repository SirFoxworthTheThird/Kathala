# Chapter 4: Article One

*The Crossing · CR 611*

On the third morning after the courier, Annick woke to silence.

It took her a moment to understand what she was hearing. The ring turned; the ventilation hummed; somewhere above her on the fourth deck a hatch banged and someone laughed. Everything was as it always was. But for eleven years, every morning, she had woken to the sound of Aurelio Benn coughing through the bulkhead — a long dry rattle that began at the change of watch and went on, with pauses, until he had dressed and taken his first strong tea — and this morning there was nothing on the other side of the wall but the faint, steady hiss of his concentrator.

She lay and listened to the hiss for perhaps ten seconds. Then she got up and went next door without dressing.

He was lying on his back on his bunk with the blanket pulled up to his chest and the oxygen line over his ears, and his eyes were half open, and his lips were the colour of a bruise. The concentrator was clipped to the rail beside him where he always put it at night. Its little indicator glowed green. It was working perfectly. It was hissing pure oxygen down the line into the nose of a man who had stopped breathing some hours before.

She did not touch him. She stood in the doorway in her sleeping shift, in the ring's light gravity, and she did a thing she would think about for a long time afterward: she counted. Not her breaths. His. She counted to sixty, watching his chest under the blanket, as if there were some figure it might reach. Then she went to the comm on the wall and called the doctor.

Yusuf Haddad came up from the infirmary in four minutes with his bag and his embarrassed face. He knelt by the bunk and did the things doctors do, briefly, and then he sat back on his heels and was quiet for a while.

"His lungs," he said at last. "It was always going to be his lungs. I told him a year. I'm sorry, Annick."

"In his sleep?"

"It looks like it." He reached up and took the oxygen line gently from the old man's face and held the end of it against the back of his own hand, feeling the flow. Then he unclipped the concentrator from the rail and turned it over and looked at its readout for a long moment. He frowned.

"What is it?"

"Nothing." He set it down. "The reservoir's full. That's all."

"Is that wrong?"

"It's—" Haddad hesitated. He was a careful man; he disliked saying things he could not prove. "It's unexpected. If he'd been drawing on it all night, it would be lower. Unless he stopped drawing on it early." He rubbed his face. "It doesn't mean anything. He might have taken the line off in his sleep and put it back. People do strange things when they can't breathe."

"Can you check it?"

"Check it for what?"

She did not know. She stood looking down at Benn's face — the long mournful face, the down of white hair lying flat against the pillow in the gravity — and found that she could not say it.

"I'll keep it," said Haddad, after a moment, quietly. He wrapped the concentrator in a towel from the rail and put it in his bag. "For the record. I'll look at it properly when I have time." He looked up at her. "You should get dressed. And you should take the key, before anyone else comes."

* * *

The master key hung on a cord around Benn's neck, under his shift. It was identical to her own except for the number on the shank, 0001, and the bead in its bow, which was larger and older and very slightly cracked. She lifted the cord over his head as gently as if he could feel it. Then she dressed, and put both keys inside her collar, and went down the spoke to the spine and along the dim weightless corridor to the strongroom, with the dead man's key lying cold against her breastbone beside her own.

The Ordinances were precise about the death of a purser, because the death of a purser was the most dangerous moment in the life of a ship. A Book with two keys and one purser was a Book with a spare. A key without a hand to hold it was a key that might be found, or bought, or taken. So the Charter required that when a purser died, his key went with him. The assistant's key became the master. The dead man's key was broken in the strongroom, before witnesses, and the pieces were committed with his body, so that there would never again be more keys than there were people entitled to hold them.

She had sent for witnesses: Haddad, who came, and the Master or his deputy, and the Factor. Lindqvist sent his apologies and the Third Officer, a nervous young man named Brandt who kept wiping his hands on his trousers. Quarry came himself. He floated in the strongroom doorway in his beautiful coat with his soft hands folded in front of him and watched her with polite attention, as he might have watched a clerk at a counter.

The bead press was bolted to the wall below the Ordinances: a small steel vice with a hardened jaw, designed for one purpose. Annick set Benn's key in it with the bead between the jaws. She turned the handle. The lattice resisted, and resisted, and then went with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and when she opened the press the bead was a little heap of dull brown grit. She brushed it into an envelope. She sealed the envelope. She put the envelope and the broken key into a second envelope, and sealed that, and wrote on it in her plain clerk's hand *A. Benn, Purser, Voyage V. Key 0001, broken*, and handed it to Haddad to put in the shroud.

Then she turned to the cradle.

It was only a box. She had lifted it a thousand times. She had dusted it and polished its hasp and checked its seals and opened it to make entries, and carried it in her arms down this corridor and back, the year they reshielded the strongroom at Callis. She knew its weight to the gram. But she understood, as she put her hands on it, that she had never before lifted it with no one else in the world entitled to lift it after her, and she found, absurdly, that her hands would not close.

*Article One*, Benn said, in her head, in his patient teacher's voice. *The Purser is whosoever holds the Key and the Book together.*

She lifted the Book out of its cradle. In zero gravity it had no weight at all, only mass, so that it moved in her arms like a sleeping animal, reluctant to be disturbed. She held it against her chest. Her key was at her throat. There was a moment — very short, nothing anyone in the room could have seen — when something passed through her that she had no name for, and would not have used if she had: a sense of the whole ship around her, the Lantern and the ring and the coil and the Hold, three thousand six hundred and twelve sleepers in their frost and two hundred and forty waking and a hundred and eighteen crew, every life aboard written somewhere in the block of crystal she was holding, every one of them owed or owing. The Charter had a phrase for it. *The office devolves.* She had read it in the clerks' manual at seventeen and thought it sounded like something mechanical, a valve opening. It did not feel like a valve.

"Purser," said Edwin Quarry from the doorway, gently, like a man greeting someone at a party.

"Factor."

"My condolences. He was a man of great principle." He let that sit for a moment, so that she would hear it. "I'll expect you in the captain's day room at the second bell. There are matters of the voyage we need to discuss."

He went. The Third Officer went after him, wiping his hands. Haddad stayed.

Annick set the Book in the cradle and opened it with her key and put her thumb to the bead and made the entry. She gave the date and the hour and her own name and her new office. She gave Aurelio Benn's name and the manner of his death, as the doctor had certified it: *failure of the lungs, in sleep.* He had no debt. He had been Clear for forty years. His estate — some savings, a few books, a box of letters, the bottle of Callisian brandy in the privy box that he had been too frightened to drink — passed under the Ordinances to his sister in the Callis hill district, who was eighty-four and might or might not be alive to receive it.

"Sealed," she said.

"Play it back," said Haddad softly from behind her.

She did not want to. She did. The Book gave her voice back:

*— failure of the lungs, in sleep —*

And it shook. Not much. Only on *sleep*, very slightly, a tremor so small that she might not have heard it if she had not been listening for it. But it was there, grown into the lattice now, permanent. Someday, someone listening would be able to tell.

She closed the lid.

* * *

The captain's day room was a small panelled cabin off the Lantern, in the forward hub where the spine met the ring, so that it had a little gravity and a great deal of light. It had a table of real wood that Lindqvist's grandfather had carried out of Callis, and a sideboard with a decanter on it, and a viewport looking out at the dull red coal of Pell. There were four chairs at the table. The captain was in one, and the Factor in another, and the First Officer was not sitting at all but standing at the viewport with her back to the room. Annick took the fourth chair because there was nowhere else to stand.

Lindqvist looked as if he had not slept since the courier. He nodded to her heavily. "I'm sorry about Aurelio. He was the best purser I ever sailed with."

"Thank you, Captain."

"You've been told nothing."

"No, Captain."

"No. He wouldn't have." Lindqvist looked at the Factor. "Go on, Edwin. You've been waiting three days."

Quarry smiled slightly. He took a sheet of film from his coat and laid it on the table and turned it round so that she could read it, the way she turned a bill round for a bonded colonist. It was the courier's order. She recognised the Receivers' seal at the foot: the sunburst of the Charter, struck through with a bar, and above it the black tower of the Bank of Vey.

She read it. It did not take long. The Receivers wrote plainly.

*The Meridian Charter Company being in liquidation, all property of the Company is vested in the Receivers of the Bank of Vey, to be realised for the benefit of the Company's creditors. The vessel* Merciful Increase, *with all her stores and the debts of her voyage, is property of the Company. The Master is directed to divert the vessel at the Fork of the Long Lane to the Shale system, where the Receivers have contracted with the Shale Concern for the sale of the vessel and the assignment of the debts of her voyage at a price agreed. The Purser is directed to seal the assignment in the Book at the Fork, and to record the Master's authority as subject to the Concern's pilots on arrival. The ship's company will be paid off at Shale with a bonus of six months' wages in recognition of their service. The Factor will see to the execution of these directions.*

Below that, in the Factor's own hand, was a figure: the price the Shale Concern had agreed to pay for the debts of the hold. It was not very much. It was a great deal less than the debts were worth on the face of the Book. It was, she understood, the price of a bulk lot of something that would not last.

She read it again. Then she laid it face down on the table.

"Shale," she said.

"A fine opportunity," said Quarry. "The Concern's very well capitalised. Heavy industry. There's a great demand for labour."

"It's two-point-one gravities."

"I believe it's two-point-one-three."

"There's no colony at Shale. There's a mine." She did not look at him; she looked at the captain. "The Concern buys bonded debt from bankrupt carriers. It doesn't settle anyone. It works them."

"It employs them," said Quarry, "until their obligations are discharged. Which is exactly what would have happened at Absalom, Purser. The only difference is the address." He spread his soft hands. "And, of course, that Absalom no longer has anyone to receive them. The Charter's colony office at Absalom was a desk in a building on Callis that has been, I understand, burned down. There's no one there. There's a gas giant and a moon made of ice. If we take these people to Absalom we'll be dropping them on the ice with nothing — no stores, no tools, no relief ship, no market for anything they produce — and leaving. That isn't mercy. That's murder with extra steps."

"How long do they live at Shale?"

He did not answer. Lindqvist did not answer. At the viewport, Nadia Szell said without turning round, "Ask the doctor."

"I'm asking you, First Officer."

Szell turned. In the light from the port her eyes were very dark. "Six to nine years," she said. "For unacclimatised adult labour under the Concern's contract terms. The children do better, oddly. They grow into it." She said it flatly, without any expression at all, as she might have read off a fuel figure. "Haddad has the tables. The Concern publishes them. It's a very honest company."

"Thank you, Nadia," said Quarry.

"You asked."

Annick looked at the captain. "Captain Lindqvist."

"It's done, Purser." He was staring at the table. "It's the order of the owners. It's the law. I've got a hundred and eighteen crew who haven't been paid since Callis, and some of them have families on Callis, and Callis is on fire, and the only money any of them will ever see for this voyage is at Shale." He looked up, and she saw that his eyes were wet, and that he was ashamed of it, and that it would not change anything. "I don't like it. Aurelio didn't like it. He said so, in this room, for five hours. And I'll tell you what I told him. I'm sixty years old. I've carried bonded for the Charter for thirty-four years. I've never once asked where they ended up. I'm not going to start pretending now that I'm the sort of man who would."

"Purser Benn refused to seal it."

"Purser Benn," said Quarry gently, "is dead."

The room was quiet. Through the viewport, the coal of Pell burned on, dull red, giving no heat to anything.

"The Fork is thirty-five days out, at the present rate of cooling," said Quarry. "There's no hurry. I don't expect you to be easy with it today. I'd think less of you if you were. But I do expect you to understand it." He leaned forward a little, and for the first time his soft voice had something under it, like a blade under silk. "You're the only purser this ship has, Purser Vell. The only key. If you die, the Book is locked, the debts can't be assigned, and the Concern won't pay — and then there's no bonus and no wages, and a ship full of very angry crew at Shale, with a hold full of bonded who can't be sold and can't be fed." He let that settle. "So you see, it's very important to all of us that you stay well. And that the people you care for stay well. I want you to know that I'll do everything in my power to see to it."

She looked at him. He looked back, pleasantly.

"Of course, Factor," she said.

* * *

Szell caught her in the passage outside.

"Purser." The First Officer fell into step beside her, one hand on the rail. "Drink with me."

"I'm on watch."

"You're the Purser. You don't stand watch, you stand next to it. Come and drink with me. There's real whisky in the officers' mess and nobody's touched it since Callis."

There was. Szell poured two glasses from a square brown bottle and pushed one across the little steel table, and sat back with the other held in both hands, and looked at Annick over the rim of it with her dark considering eyes. The mess was empty at this hour. The ring's gravity was light here, close to the hub, and the whisky moved in the glass in a slow, heavy way, like oil.

"Benn was a good man," said Szell.

"Yes."

"Good men are expensive. Did you know that? Everyone thinks they're cheap. They come with principles; principles look like they're free. But you have to pay for them somewhere. Usually someone else does." She drank. "He'd have cost all those people their wages. Lindqvist knew it. That's why he sat there for five hours letting the old man talk. He was hoping Benn would talk him into something. He didn't."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're not a good man," said Szell, "and neither am I, and I thought it would be pleasant for once to talk to someone who isn't pretending." She smiled. It was the first time Annick had seen her smile; it changed her face completely, made it younger and almost warm. "I've watched you for three years, Purser. You're the only person on this ship who does exactly what she's supposed to do, every day, and never once tells herself a story about it. I find that restful."

Annick turned her glass on the table. She did not drink. "What do you want, First Officer?"

"Want?" Szell looked at her for a moment. Then she said, as if it were an idle question, the kind you ask in a bar at the end of a long watch, "What would you do, Purser, if you could do anything at all?"

"My job."

"Yes," said Szell softly. "That's what I thought." She finished her whisky and stood. "Don't let it go to waste. It's the last of it. There's none at Shale."

* * *

Dace came for her after the second watch, when the ring had dimmed its lights for the ship's night.

She did not knock. She opened the door of the purser's cabin with a bonded tech's override, which she was not supposed to have, and stood in the doorway in her dun coveralls with her hair tied back with wire, and said, "Come with me. Don't bring anything that talks."

"Dace—"

"Leave your slate. Come on."

Annick left her slate. She went.

They went down the spoke into the spine and aft along the main corridor, past the strongroom door with its red letters, and through the great double hatch into the Hold.

The Hold of the *Merciful Increase* was the largest single space aboard her: a cylinder two hundred metres long and sixty across, running down the centre of the spine, and filled from end to end with cradles. They were racked in tiers on a lattice of black struts that rose from every surface toward the axis, so that wherever you floated in the Hold, cradles surrounded you — above, below, on every side — receding into a blue dimness lit only by the small cold glow of each cradle's status lamp. Three thousand six hundred and twelve of them. Each one was a coffin-shaped shell of white composite with a window of frosted glass over the face, and behind each window, if you went close enough, you could see a face: still, grey, rimed with frost on the lashes and the brows, like a face seen through ice on a pond. The air was cold enough here to make the breath smoke. It smelled of cradle-gel and coolant and, faintly, of something sweeter underneath that Annick had never let herself put a name to.

The bonded called them coffins. The crew called them cradles. The Charter's manifests called them berths, and charged for them by the cubic metre.

Dace led her through the struts hand over hand, deep into the racks, three tiers in and halfway down, where a gap in the lattice made a kind of hollow among the cradles, out of sight of the main hatch. There were perhaps a dozen people waiting there. All bonded, in dun, with the blue lamplight on their faces. Some of them she knew by sight from the Wake rotations: the gang chief with the burned face from the coil, a bonded electrician from the Lantern's maintenance crew, a slight young man with a shaved head and a knife scar on his chin whom Dace drifted over to stand beside, very close. And two she knew by name, because everyone in the Wake knew them by name, and because she had read their entries in the Book the day they came aboard.

*Ruck, Tobiah. V5-0319. Bonded. Quarryman.* He was enormous. Even in zero gravity, where size meant less, he took up space the way a bulkhead does: two metres tall, broad as a door, with a shaved scarred head and hands like shovels and a face that had been broken and set crooked at least twice. He had worked eighteen years in the Charter's granite quarries in the hills above Callis before he signed for Absalom. She had read his debt. It was very large; quarrymen's families borrowed against their wages for generations. And he had a voice — she heard it now for the first time — that did not belong to the rest of him at all: low and soft and unhurried, a voice for talking to animals, or children, or the dying.

"Purser," he said. "Thank you for coming."

*Osei, Marit. V5-2207. Bonded. Clerk.* Except that she had not been a clerk; that was what the manifest said, because the Charter had no category for what she had been, which was an advocate for the dockworkers' guild on Callis for twenty years, until the guild was broken and its officers bankrupted by suits and their debts sold to the Charter. She was fifty-six, small and upright, with grey locs tied back and reading lenses pushed up on her forehead, and she looked at Annick the way Annick looked at a column of figures she suspected had been added wrong.

"You know why you're here," said Marit Osei.

"Shale," said Annick.

"Yes." Marit glanced at the electrician. "Kofi was on the Lantern the night the courier came, re-running conduit behind the command ring. He has good ears. We've known for three days." She folded her arms. "The question is what you're going to do about it."

"I'm not going to do anything about it. I'm the purser."

"That's rather the point."

"Dace," said Annick, without looking at her sister, "you shouldn't have brought me here."

"I know," said Dace. "I did anyway."

Tobiah Ruck drifted a little closer. He moved carefully, as big men learn to, so as not to crowd. "Nobody's asking you to do anything you don't want to, Purser," he said. "Not yet. We just want you to hear us. That's all. Can you do that much?"

She could have said no. She could have turned around and gone back up the struts and out through the hatch and along the corridor to the Factor's cabin, and knocked, and told him that there were fourteen people in the Hold plotting mutiny, and given him their names; and she knew, with perfect clarity, as she floated there in the blue cold among the coffins, that Edwin Quarry would have thanked her and sent proctors and that by the next watch there would be fourteen fewer people in the Wake and her sister would be one of them. She knew that Dace knew it too. That was why Dace had brought her. It was the oldest bargain in the world: *I have put my life in your hands. Now what will you do?*

"I'll listen," said Annick.

They told her. Not everything — she could see them choosing what to say, see Marit's eyes flick to Tobiah's before each answer — but enough. There were three hundred of them in the Wake who would move when Tobiah said move. There were more in the Hold, in the cradles, who could be woken: a fast thaw was dangerous, but it could be done, if you had someone who knew the cradle systems and was willing to kill a few to wake the rest. There were twenty-four proctors and a hundred and eighteen crew, and most of the crew would not fight for the Factor, not for Shale, not if it came to it. And there was the Fork, thirty-five days off, where the ship would come out of fold and drift for its cooling, and where the Factor would come down to the strongroom with his order for the Purser to seal.

"And then?" said Annick.

"And then," said Tobiah, "we take her."

"And then what? You take the Lantern. You lock the Factor in his cabin. And then you have a ship you can't fly." She looked round at them. "Can any of you fly her? Can any of you jump her? The coil needs a pilot who's been trained on it, and a navigator who knows the Lane, and the Lantern's locks won't answer anyone the Book doesn't name as Master. Article Fourteen. You can take the bridge and sit in the captain's chair and she'll drift at the Fork until the air runs out."

"That's why we need you," said Marit.

"To do what?"

"To write one line." Tobiah's soft voice. "That's all, Purser. Not to fight. Not to carry anything. At the moment we take the Lantern, you're in the strongroom with the Book and the key, and you write one line that says who the Master is. And she flies."

"Who?"

He did not answer. Marit did not answer.

"Who do you want written in?" said Annick. "You?"

"We'll have help," said Tobiah after a moment. "When the time comes. Somebody who can fly her." He held up one great hand before she could speak. "I'm not going to tell you more than that. Not tonight. I'm not asking you to trust me tonight, Purser. I'm only asking you to listen."

"And if I won't write it?"

"Then you'll seal the Factor's assignment," said Marit Osei, "and we'll all go to Shale, and you'll keep the Book for the Concern for six to nine years, and enter us one at a time as we die." She unfolded her arms. "You've been keeping a Book of what we owe since the day we came aboard, Purser Vell. Every litre of air. Every meal. Every death. You're very good at it; everyone says so. I've only one question, and you needn't answer it now." She leaned forward slightly, into the blue light. "Have you ever, once, in your whole career, kept a Book of what's owed to *us*?"

Somewhere in the racks behind them, very softly, someone was singing. A man's voice, low, with no tune to speak of — one of the Wake's night watch, drifting among the cradles, checking the lamps, singing to himself the way people sing in the dark to know they're not alone.

*Heave the iron, heave it slow,
the Book is heavy, down we go.
What is written, that is owed,
and the dark's a long cold road —*

"I'll listen," said Annick again. It was all she could find to say. "That's all. I'll listen."

"That's all we asked," said Tobiah Ruck.

* * *

She did not go back to her cabin. She went to the strongroom.

It was the middle of the ship's night, and the spine was empty, and her own breath was the loudest sound in it. She let herself in through the iron door under the red letters and closed it behind her and floated for a while in the dark before she turned on the lamp. Then she took the Book out of its cradle and held it — just held it, against her chest, the way she had held it that morning with the Factor watching — and then she put it back, and opened it, and turned the key.

The page-glass filled with light. She scrolled back through the columns, past Benn's death this morning, past Emeric Saal three days ago, past a hundred and forty other entries of the voyage, births and deaths and transfers and fines, back to the beginning, and found what she was looking for. *Voyage the Fifth. Opened at Callis. A. Benn, Purser. A. Vell, Assistant Purser.* She touched it, and the Book gave her his voice:

*— in the name of the Meridian Charter Company and under the Ordinances, I open this Book, and bind myself to keep it truly —*

It was a much younger voice than she remembered. Not young, but younger; less air in it, less patience. He'd been sixty, opening this voyage. She had stood beside him in this room and heard him say it and thought nothing of it at all.

*— truly, and without fear or favour, so that what is written here be owed, and only what is written here —*

She stopped it.

She sat there for a long time with the Book open in front of her and the stylus in her hand. She did not write anything. There was nothing to write. There was the Fork in thirty-five days, and the Factor's order, and the six to nine years, and the fourteen people in the Hold whose names she knew now and could not unknow, and her sister standing very close to a young man with a knife scar on his chin, and the First Officer's dark eyes over a glass of the last whisky in the ship. There was a concentrator in a towel in Haddad's bag with its reservoir full.

She held her thumb above the bead and did not lower it.

At last she closed the lid. The page-glass went dark. In her head, quite without meaning to, she began to keep her other accounts, the ones she had never written anywhere; and for the first time in eleven years she found she did not know what column to put them in.
