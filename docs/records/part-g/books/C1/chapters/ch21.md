# Chapter 21: Before Witnesses

*The Salvage · CR 1317*

She doesn't sleep. She sits on the bottom bunk in the dark with Pim's slate in her lap and the pictures from her helmet camera on it, and looks at a brass plaque.

It's the only thing she brought back from the vault that isn't an old ship's ledger, and she can't sell it. She knows that. She's known since she took it. Nobody pays for a picture of brass. But it's the only thing she has left to look at, and so she looks: column by column, the way the camera took it, swimming slowly down the wall in the lamp light, the verdigris and the white crust and the tiny cramped letters underneath.

Pim taught her how to sharpen a picture on the slate, the winter he was eleven and wanted to read the House names in her dive pictures. She does it now. The crust goes grey and then pale and then, in places, transparent, like ice going thin, and the letters come up through it one at a time.

Old Charter capitals. The kind on the writs. She can read those; everyone in the Gutter can, now.

*THE PURSER'S ORDINANCES.*

Twenty-two of them. She doesn't read them in order. She reads what comes up clear.

*ARTICLE ONE. The Purser is whosoever holds the Key and the Book together.*

*ARTICLE FOUR. What is written under seal is owed.* She knows that one. It's on the door. What's left of it.

*ARTICLE ELEVEN. An entry read aloud under seal before witnesses stands as the Book itself.*

*ARTICLE TWENTY. A debt discharged under seal is discharged forever, against all heirs and assigns.*

And a third of the way down the first column, under a thick white crust that the slate takes a long time to see through, so that the words come up slowly, a letter at a time, like something surfacing:

*ARTICLE NINE. Should the vessel be taken by any party not lawfully in command, the Purser shall commit the Book to the ballast, that it be not suborned.*

She reads it four times.

*The ballast.*

She doesn't know what *suborned* means. She knows what *ballast* means. Everybody in the Gutter knows. It's what you call the poor; it's what the Houses called the people who cut the ice; it's what the old ship carried in her tanks to keep her steady in the dark. Water. Four great tanks along the spine. BALLAST 3. BALLAST 4. Stencilled on the walls in the old Charter letters, newer-looking than the House names, because nobody bothered to carve them.

And on Quell's drawing, pinned to the ice wall of a cell behind the altar, drawn forty years ago in pencil, there's a square for the vault with *DON'T BOTHER* under it, and right beside it, between the vault and the Old Hold, a smaller shape, round, with a line through it. *BALLAST 4.* And under that, in darker pencil, added later: *WIDOW.*

She sits very still on the bunk.

She thinks of the gap on the shelf in the vault, between the second box and the fourth. She thinks of the empty cradle, built for something the size of a big book. She thinks of the Ash Case, sitting on its felt in the blue church under a column of glass: a box the size of a big book, black and blistered and burned. *They burned the Book.* She doesn't understand it. She doesn't try to. She thinks of a grey man in an empty vault, tied to the pedestal of an empty cradle with his own belt, and an empty steel safe in the wall with its door open; and she thinks of an Assessor of the Office of the Account, who would have known every one of these twenty-two articles the way divers know the House names, stealing something from the Office on the Night of the Hulk, something the people who lost it would have killed to keep, and carrying it here, to the vault, to the strongroom of the old ship, with the plaque on the wall above him saying in letters a child could read what a purser does with a thing that must not be taken.

*Commit it to the ballast.*

And then staying. Tying himself to the cradle. Waiting in the dark for nobody.

And she thinks of the first dive, a lifetime ago, twenty-one days, the night she found the seal-reader: hanging in the spine corridor with Mitya on her line, outside a great round iron hatch with a skull painted on it and two names, and hearing through the iron, through the water in the corridor and the water in the tank, very faint, a slow dull knocking. Like something heavy turning over and over in its sleep.

*Ice*, Mitya said.

She gets up. She gets her shell down off its hooks.

* * *

The last window is short.

The Assembly's engineers have stopped putting numbers on their bulletins. The last one, at the twentieth hour, says only: *Structural failure of the spine is expected at or before the next perigee. Divers are advised that the cold window is estimated at not more than twelve hours and may be considerably less. The Wardenship accepts no responsibility.* Under it, someone in the Gutter has scratched a skull on the screen's frame, small and neat, and under the skull, *HULK*.

She's the only one on the dock. Gorse's crews aren't going. Kell Amory's sitting in the bar with Ferro, who has a dressing over the back of his head where his ear was, and they watch her go past the window in her mother's unpainted shell with the box marked IV clipped to her harness and Mitya's cutter on her back, and they don't come out.

There's a light following her sled when she clears the dock. Small, black, a long way back. A Company drone, the kind the bailiffs use to watch the satisfaction lines. It doesn't come closer. It doesn't need to.

The Hulk is barely there.

She has to look twice to find it against the face of Absalom. It isn't a ship any more. It's a long dark smear inside its own glittering cloud of vapour, twisted along its length like a wrung cloth, its leading face a single scab of black glass, pieces of it — whole sections, whole decks — hanging off it on threads of torn iron and turning slowly in the sunlight, catching the light, going dark. As she watches, one of them lets go. It drifts away from the Hulk very gently, a slab of iron the size of a can, and tumbles, and is lost in the fog.

The Mouth is still there. She has to wait for the glow to go out of its rim. Then she goes in.

* * *

The water is warm.

That's the first thing, and it frightens her more than anything else on the way in. It's not the cold-off-the-edge she felt on the last dive; it's warm, warm like a bath gone tepid, and full of silt and bubbles and the long white streamers of things that have been in the Hulk for seven hundred years and are coming apart. The spine groans round her constantly now — not the long low groan of before but a continuous grinding moan, rising and falling, with sharp cracks in it like ice breaking on a lake. The corridors have changed shape. A hatch she's gone through a dozen times is bent half shut. A wall in the Hall has opened along a seam, and through the seam, a long way off, she can see a thread of daylight.

She goes through the Hall, where the last of the banners are down. She goes down the service passage. At the corner, the skull. *M.S.*

He's there. Under the beam, with his lamp still burning. She stops beside him and puts her gauntlet on his shell.

"Forty-one the kilo," she says, on the suit band. "I told him."

She goes on into the vault.

It has melted. The perigee has reached in through the walls and taken the floor — the ice that covered it is gone, or nearly, a skin of grey slush lying on the iron, stirring in the warm water. The empty cradle stands up out of it on its pedestal. The steel bands that were frozen into the ice lie loose on the floor like dropped ribbons. The grey man is where he was, tied to the pedestal by his belt, his white hair drifting, his face to the floor. She doesn't look at him.

She looks at the floor.

Beside the pedestal there's a small square hatch with a red lever. And beside the hatch, flush in the iron, a round plate a metre across, with bolt-holes round its rim — empty; the bolts are gone — and a stencil on its edge in the old Charter letters, faded almost to nothing, that her lamp brings up slowly out of the iron.

*RECOVERY PORT. BALLAST 4 WELL. FOR USE AT PORT BY LAWFUL OWNER ONLY.*

The plate isn't flat. It sits crooked in its frame, jammed, one edge riding up over the rim. The frame round it is bent, the iron of the floor buckled up in a long low ridge, as if something struck it very hard from somewhere a long time ago. On the low side there's a gap. A crescent, a finger wide, black. Through it, when she puts her lamp down close, she sees nothing at all.

*You don't cut a door*, says Mitya, in her head, in his pricing voice. *You cut what holds it.*

She unslings his cutter.

* * *

It takes forty minutes. She doesn't cut the plate; she cuts the frame, along the buckled ridge, where the iron is bent and thin, the way he'd have done it. The water boils white round the arc. The Hulk grinds and shudders. Twice the vault lurches round her and the empty cradle swings on its pedestal and the grey man drifts out on his belt and back, and she stops and holds on and waits, and goes on.

When the last of the frame goes, the plate drops.

Not up. Down. It tilts, and slides, and falls through its own frame into the dark below, slowly, turning, a great iron disc going down through the water in her lamp like a coin dropped into a well, and she hears it a long time later strike something far below with a sound like a bell.

She puts her lamp into the hole.

A shaft. Two metres across. Iron walls, with rungs set into one side. Water in it, grey with silt, all the way down past where her light can reach.

And three metres below her, at the top of the rungs, a man.

He's facing up. That's the first thing. Facing up, toward her, toward the hole where the plate was, as if he's been waiting for it to open. His belt is looped through the top rung and buckled, so that he hangs there in the water on his back, very still, his arms folded across his chest. He's in grey. A long grey coat, too tight across the shoulders, with a burn on one cuff. On the lapel there's a pin — small, brass, a pair of scales — catching her lamp. He's a man of about forty, with a few days' stubble and dark hair, and his face — she makes herself look at his face — his face is whole. Grey and still and whole. Four centuries in the ice at the top of a sealed tank in a dead ship, and then a few weeks of thaw; and it hasn't touched him. He looks like a man who's gone to sleep at the end of a long shift, counting.

His eyes are closed.

In his folded arms, clipped to his belt by a short strap, held against his chest the way you'd hold a child, there's an iron box the size of a big book. And in the box's hasp, so that she can see its bow standing out from the steel, there's a key.

She goes down the rungs.

She stops beside him. She doesn't touch him at first. She hangs on the rungs in the warm grey water with her lamp on his face. Then she reaches into his coat — gently; she's never been gentle with anything in the Hulk in her life — and finds, in the inside pocket, a flat thing, an Office tally, the handheld kind the bailiffs' machines are copied from, with brass corners and a dead screen. There's a name stamped into its back.

*H. FEN. ASSESSOR 2ND RANK.*

She turns it over in her gauntlet. She reads it again.

Then she looks up — up through the hole she cut, into the vault, where, at the end of his belt, tied to the pedestal of the empty cradle, the grey man with the white hair is turning very slowly in the water, with an Office chain round his neck.

Not Fen.

Somebody else. Somebody who came into the vault on the Night of the Hulk and never left it. And Fen went past him, and down.

* * *

She unclips the strap from his belt. She lifts the box out of his arms.

It's heavy. Even in the water she can feel it, the way it doesn't want to move. Iron. A sunburst on the cover, worn nearly flat, over a pair of scales. The same sign as on the seal-reader on her hip. The same sign, she understands, slowly, as on the burned box in the Ash Church, under its column of glass, melted and run and set again.

The key turns in the hasp. It's already turned. The case is closed but not locked; whoever turned it last left it ready. She opens the lid.

The inside of it lights up.

Blue-white. Columns of tiny writing, trembling a little, as if the light isn't quite sure of itself. In the warm grey water at the top of a well in the bottom of a dying ship, it lights up the moment she opens it, the way the seal-reader's eye lights up when the bell rings. Like it's been waiting.

She takes the seal-reader off her hip. She opens its panel the way Pim showed her and holds its dull glass eye against the bead in the key's bow, where it stands out of the hasp. The eye glows amber. A click. A line of tiny letters, scrolling.

*M.I. VOYAGE V. OPEN. PURSER'S KEY 0447. SEAL TRUE.*

She hangs on the rungs beside Hollis Fen with the open Book in her arms and the key in its hasp under her hand, and for a moment she doesn't understand anything at all.

*The Purser is whosoever holds the Key and the Book together.*

She looks at the page-glass. At the last of the columns, at the bottom. She touches it, the way you'd touch a slate. And the Book speaks — through the iron, through the water, through her gauntlets and the arms of her shell and into her helmet, small and clear, a man's voice, low, tired, very level, the voice of someone who's been trained to keep it level and has nearly run out of whatever it takes.

*Entry. Hollis Fen, Assessor of the Second Rank of the Office of the Account, holding the Key and the Book together—*

She listens to all of it.

Then she touches the entry above it. And the Book gives her a woman's voice.

* * *

Afterward, Yara will never be able to say how long she hung there. Long enough. Long enough for the Hulk to lurch three times round her and the water in the well to slosh against the rungs. Long enough to hear both of them twice. A woman she has heard of all her life as a word for cheating — *don't vell me* — reading out, in a clerk's voice that doesn't shake, the debts of the dead and her own crime against herself. A man she has heard of all her life in a skipping song, *he counted the Hold and he cut every throat*, reading out three hundred and nine strikes against his own name, and asking that his daughter know.

*Nothing written in this Book is owed by anyone.*

*I record that Annick Vell was not a traitor.*

*Let any court that ever sees that copy know it for a forgery.*

*Who were asleep.*

*There's no one to hear. The Purser was right about that too.*

When it's done the second time, she finds she's holding the Book against her chest in both arms, the way he held it. The way, she thinks, the woman must have held it, before she put it down the chute. She's crying, in the helmet, which she hasn't done since she was sixteen, and the helmet's fogging, and she can't wipe it.

She doesn't stop. She can't. She hangs on the rungs in the warm water and cries, and the Hulk grinds round her, and at her hip, very faintly, the seal-reader clicks.

The bell. It's the hour. Seven notes falling on the old band, from the tower on the ice above Saltmarch, coming down through the Hulk to her the way they come to every receiver in the system.

*The array sends what you give it.*

She stops crying.

* * *

Kade answers on the second call.

She calls the drone. It's still out there, hanging off the Mouth in the dark where it's been since she came in, and its band is open; and when she speaks into it, the Factor's voice comes back almost at once, warm and calm and attentive, as though he's been sitting by the speaker waiting.

"Miss Tull."

"I've got it."

A pause. "Sealed?"

"It's sealed." It is. She isn't lying. "Iron box. Lead on the hasp. The size of a big book. It was in the vault." That isn't a lie either. "I've got it on my harness."

"Unopened?"

"I'm not stupid, Factor."

"No," says Aurel Kade. "No, I don't believe you are." She can hear him thinking. "Bring it out to the drone. It'll take it."

"No. Pim first."

"Miss Tull—"

"Pim first. Put him on your shuttle and take him down to the Ringfall dock and give him to the Advocate. Mireille Dunn. She's on the dock; I asked her to be. When she tells me on the open band that he's off your ship and standing next to her, I'll clip it to a line at the Mouth and your drone can take it. Not before."

The silence goes on for a long time. The Hulk groans. The water in the well slops against the rungs.

"I'd want to see it first," says Kade.

"You can see it on the drone's camera. I'll hold it up." She does. She unclips the box marked IV from her harness with one hand and holds it up toward the hole above her, toward the vault and the passages and the far-off Mouth and the drone, as if it could see; and she says, "Iron. Sunburst. Lead seal. You've seen the Ash Case. It's the same."

Another silence. Then: "Very well," says the Factor. "The shuttle's leaving now. Twenty minutes."

It takes thirty-one.

She waits them out in the well beside Hollis Fen with both books on her harness, the dead one and the live one, and Mitya's cutter, and her air going down, and the water getting warmer. She counts. She can't help it. She's never been able to help it.

At thirty-one minutes the band crackles and a woman says, sharp and breathless and very clear, "Tull. This is Dunn. I have him. He's here. He's standing next to me on the Ringfall dock and he's—" A pause, a sound that might be a laugh. "He's breathing. He wants to say—"

"*Yara.*" Pim. Strong. All the way down. "Yara, I'm here, I'm off, they let me—"

"Stay with the Advocate," she says. "Don't move."

She goes back up the rungs. She goes back through the vault, past the grey man on his belt, and down the service passage past Mitya, and through the Hall, as fast as the water will let her, to the Mouth; and she clips the dead book of the fourth voyage to a line at the lip of the breach, where the drone is hanging in the dark with its small black eye, and lets it go.

The drone takes it. She watches its light go away toward the black ship.

Then she goes back down into the Hulk for the last time.

* * *

It's twenty minutes back to the well. She has three hours of air and the engineers' twelve hours of window and she doesn't believe either of them. The spine is shrieking now. Somewhere toward the bow something gives way with a long tearing roar and the whole corridor jerks sideways and she's thrown against the wall and hangs there, holding the rail, until it stops.

At the well, she goes down the rungs and hangs beside him again.

"Hello," she says.

She takes Quell's lead out of the pocket of her jacket — the short coil of braided copper in its cracked grey sheath, with a brass fitting at each end. She finds the socket on the side of the seal-reader, under the coil of fine wire, where Quell said it would be. It fits. She finds the band jack on the chest of her shell, where every diver's comms come in. The other end fits that. She holds the seal-reader's dull glass eye against the bead in the bow of the key, in the hasp of the Book, and the eye glows amber, and stays amber.

"Mother," she says, on the old band. "It's Yara. Are you there?"

Static. The groaning of the Hulk. Then, very faint, very old, cracked: "I'm here, girl."

"Ring the bell."

A silence.

"It's not the hour," says Mother Quell.

"I know. Ring it anyway. Leave it open. Send what I give it." Yara takes a breath. "Everything. Every receiver. Ringfall and Saltmarch and the ice towns and the ferries. And the black ship. Especially the black ship. The Clerk's got a bead on his chest like the one on this key; Dunn says it hears the old band. Make sure he hears it."

Another silence. Longer.

"What have you got, girl?"

"Ring the bell, Mother."

She hears it. Through the lead, through the seal-reader, through the shell, through the water: far off, in a tower on the ice above Saltmarch, a heavy clockwork thing engaging, and then the chime. Seven notes, falling. Out of hour. Every radio in the system.

And then nothing. An open band. A silence that she can feel, somehow, all the way from the Gutter to the Deep Ice, the way you can feel a room full of people holding their breath.

She puts her thumb on the bead of the key.

"This is Yara Tull," she says. "I'm a diver. From the Gutter, on Ringfall. My mother was Hana Tull. She did two hundred and nine dives." She doesn't know why she says that. She says it anyway. "I'm in the Hulk. In the Widow. In the well of the fourth ballast tank. I've found Fen's cache."

She waits. She doesn't know for what.

"It's not gold," she says. "It's not what Fen stole. He didn't steal it. He found it. It's the Book. The Purser's Book, from the Crossing. It didn't burn. They burned the wrong one. It's here, and Fen's here with it, and I'm holding it, and I'm holding her key." She swallows. "Article One. *The Purser is whosoever holds the Key and the Book together.* That's me, I suppose. For now."

The band is silent.

"Article Eleven," she says. "*An entry read aloud under seal before witnesses stands as the Book itself.*" She looks down at Hollis Fen, at his closed eyes and his folded arms. "So I'm going to read it to you. All of you. The last two entries. I'm going to let them read it to you themselves, because they can. The Book kept their voices. That's what it's for." She touches the page-glass. "This is Annick Vell."

* * *

It goes out.

All of it, on the old band, through the seal-reader and the lead and the jack and the shell, to the tower on the ice above Saltmarch, and from the tower to every receiver on Tamar and every can on Ringfall and every ferry and every gallery in Gallows Reach and the Deep Ice and the Sump, and to the long black ship hanging in orbit with a key and a sword on its flank. A woman's voice, very level, from seven hundred and six years ago. A clerk's voice, the voice you'd read a bill in.

*Entry. Annick Vell. Purser of the* Merciful Increase, *Voyage the Fifth, holding the Key and the Book together—*

Yara doesn't listen. She's heard it. She hangs in the water at the top of the well with her thumb on the bead and her eyes on Hollis Fen's face and thinks about the people listening. She can't see them. She can see them. Pim on the dock beside the Advocate with his slate out, writing it down, because somebody should. Quell in the tower with her huge scarred hands on the cabinet. The noodle-cart man. Petra and her three children. Tobin Rusk's wife. Kell Amory in the bar. The people in grey on their bunks behind the long window in the satisfaction hold. The Warden in his chair. The Factor in his office with a dead book in front of him, and a seal-reader of his own, perhaps, beginning to click.

*Now therefore. Under Article Twenty of the Ordinances, I discharge every debt written in this Book—*

The Hulk shudders. The water in the well slops up the rungs and over her helmet and back.

*—to be held by no one person, and no council, and no house—*

*—They will burn the dead book of the third voyage in its place tomorrow, and believe they have burned this. Let them.*

*What is written is owed. I have written what we owe each other.*

*Read aloud under seal, in the Hold, before witnesses. Who were asleep.*

The voice shakes. On the last word. Everyone in the system hears it.

She touches the next entry. And a man's voice says, tired, level, very low:

*Entry. Hollis Fen, Assessor of the Second Rank of the Office of the Account—*

* * *

When it's done there's silence on the band for so long that she thinks the lead has failed. She checks it. It hasn't. The seal-reader's eye is amber. The band is open. It's only that nobody, anywhere, is saying anything.

Then a voice. Not Quell's. Not Dunn's. Dry and very clear and without any warmth at all, coming to her down the old band from a long way off.

"This is the Clerk-Notary of the Chancery of Vey."

She holds the rung.

"The Clerk has heard," says Ansel Rhee, "under Article Eleven of the Purser's Ordinances, which the Accords of Settled Space incorporate, the reading under seal of two entries of an instrument styled the Book of the *Merciful Increase*, Voyage the Fifth. The Clerk has examined the seal transmitted with the reading. It is a Charter purser's seal, key four-four-seven, and the Clerk finds it true." A pause; the faint scratch of a stylus. "The instrument is prior in date to the Account of Absalom and of superior authority, the Account claiming to be raised upon it. Its entry of Charter Year six hundred and eleven discharges, under Article Twenty, the obligations of all persons of the hold and their heirs and assigns, forever. Its entry of Charter Year nine hundred and twenty-nine records the impeachment of the Account before the Concordance Bureau of Titles as forged, and identifies the copy from which the Company's instrument descends." Another pause. "It is a certified contrary record. The writ is defeated."

Static. Somewhere behind the Clerk's voice, very faint, another voice, raised, warm no longer — Kade's — saying something she can't make out.

"The Factor will be silent," says the Clerk-Notary. "The Clerk makes no finding as to the justice of the matter. The Clerk is not competent to make such a finding. Paper is paper." The stylus again. "The Clerk withdraws certification of all satisfactions taken under the writ. Persons held aboard the *Lawful Remedy* in satisfaction are released and will be returned to Tamar at the Company's charge. Persons held under medical satisfaction will have their treatment completed, at the Company's charge, before return." A pause. "The Clerk is obliged to the reader. That is all."

The band goes quiet.

And then — far off, very faint, through the lead and the reader and the shell and the water and the long iron length of a dying ship — she hears something she doesn't understand at first, and then does. It's coming from the tower. Quell's left the band open. And under the hum of the array, there's a sound like the Hulk itself makes in the deep night: a vast low roar, rising. It's people. It's the sound of a great many people, a very great many, somewhere far away under the ice, all at once.

She doesn't listen to it for long. She can't. She puts her thumb back on the bead.

"Entry," she says.

She doesn't know how. She does it the way they did. "Yara Tull. Diver. Holding the Key and the Book together. In the well of the fourth ballast tank of the Hulk, over Absalom. Charter Year—" she has to think — "thirteen hundred and seventeen. The last pass."

The Hulk lurches. She holds on.

"I record that I read this aloud under seal," she says. "Before witnesses."

She looks at Hollis Fen's closed eyes.

"Who were awake."

Her voice doesn't shake. She notices. She thinks, distantly, that she'd like it to have.

"Sealed," she says.

* * *

She has to go.

The water's warm as blood now. The rungs are hot through her gauntlets. The groaning of the spine has stopped being a sound and become a place, the way the arc of the cutter becomes a place when you're close to it, and every few seconds the whole well jerks sideways and the water slaps up over her helmet and falls back. The engineers' twelve hours were a lie. She's known it since the dock. She has an hour, perhaps. Perhaps less.

The Book is in her arms.

She could take it. She thinks about it — she does, she lets herself think it all the way through, the way her mother taught her to think through a dive before she made it. Eighteen kilos. Mass, not weight, in the water. Up the rungs, through the vault, down the passage, through the Hall, to the Mouth, to the sled. An hour. Perhaps. If the corridors hold. If nothing comes down. And then, on Ringfall, the Book. In her can, on the galley shelf. With the key. *Whosoever holds the Key and the Book together.* The Advocate would want it. The Assembly would want it. The Warden. The Company, whatever's left of it. The Church. Somebody would come for it, and somebody would hold it, and they'd mean well.

*To be held by no one person, and no council, and no house.*

It's been read. It's in all of them now. Four hundred thousand people heard it at once, on the old band, out of hour, and Pim wrote it down.

She closes the lid. The page-glass goes dark. She turns the key in the hasp, and the case locks with a deep soft sound like a door closing far away, and the Book is a block of iron again. She clips its strap back to Hollis Fen's belt, where he put it. She folds his arms back over it, carefully, the way they were.

Then she takes the key out of the hasp.

It comes out easily. It's long and iron and the length of her palm, with a notched bit and a dull bead in the bow, and a number on the shank so worn she can barely read it. It weighs almost nothing.

"They heard," she says to him. "All of them. They were awake."

She goes up the rungs and doesn't look back.

* * *

She doesn't remember most of the way out. She'll try, later, and get pieces. The vault, and the grey man with the white hair turning on his belt, and her lamp on the chain round his neck, and not stopping. The service passage, and Mitya, and her gauntlet on his shell, and not stopping. A section of the Hall that isn't there any more — just a ragged wall of torn iron and, beyond it, open space, and Absalom, enormous, banded, so close she can see the storms moving — and going round it on her line, outside, in vacuum, with the Hulk's skin glowing dull red under her hands. The Mouth. The sled. The clamps.

She launches as the Hulk starts to fall.

From the sled she watches it go. She doesn't mean to; she has to fly, and she flies; but she turns the sled as she climbs, the way you'd turn your head, and watches. It goes down out of the dark toward the top of the cloud deck, a long twisted shape inside its cloud of glittering fog, the way she's seen it go down from the Ringfall gallery a hundred times; and it meets the air, and lights.

Red. Then orange. Then white. A fierce small point sliding across the banded face of Absalom with a tail behind it that lengthens and lengthens, a line of fire a hundred kilometres long, two hundred — and then, about halfway across the face of the giant, without any sound at all, it breaks.

Not into two. Into everything. A spray of fire, hundreds of points of light, thousands, spreading out from where the Hulk was like sparks thrown up when you drop a log on a fire; each one trailing its own small thread of flame across the rust and cream and ochre of the clouds, falling, falling, all of them together, down toward the storms, and going out, one by one.

Somewhere in one of them there's an iron box with every debt in it discharged and every name in it free, in the arms of a man who counted, at the top of a well, in the ballast. She watches until the last spark goes out.

At her hip, the seal-reader clicks once, and is still.

* * *

The Ash Church is full for a week. Then it isn't, and Mother Quell lets her in at night, when there's no one.

The blue is the same. The cold is the same. The Ash Case sits on its felt on the altar of ice under its column of glass, black and blistered, and the bowl of grey ash at its foot is nearly empty, because for a week everyone on Tamar came and took some.

"I want to see it," says Yara. "Properly. Out of the glass."

Quell looks at her with her one good eye for a long moment. Then she takes a ring of keys from her belt and unlocks the column, and lifts the glass off — it takes both of them — and sets it on the ice. She doesn't touch the Case. She stands back.

Yara picks it up.

It's heavy. Iron. She knows the weight of it now; she held one like it in her arms at the top of a well. She turns it over in the lamp light. The sunburst on the cover, melted and run. The hasp, fused. The crack along one side with the dark glassy stuff inside it, like burnt sugar.

And low on the cover, below where the sunburst was, a small plate. She'd never have seen it under the glass. She doesn't think anyone has, in seven hundred years; nobody's ever held it. The plate is warped and blackened and half melted into the iron, but there are letters stamped into it, and the fire didn't reach quite deep enough to take them all.

*VOY. III*

She laughs.

She doesn't mean to. It comes out of her in the blue cold church, loud, and goes up into the vault of the ice and comes back down, and she has to put the Case back on its felt because her hands are shaking.

"What?" says Quell. "What is it?"

"They burned the wrong book," says Yara. She can't stop laughing. "She said so. *They will burn the dead book of the third voyage in its place, and believe they have burned this.* She said it, Mother. On the band. Everyone heard her say it, and nobody thought to come and look." She wipes her eyes. "It's not the Book. It's the third voyage. Some ship's ledger from before the Crossing, with all its debts paid. They've been praying to the wrong box for seven hundred years."

Quell looks at the Case. Then, slowly, the old woman begins to laugh too — a deep cracked sound like ice moving on a lake — and she puts one huge scarred hand on the altar to hold herself up, and laughs until she has to sit down on the step.

"Well," she says at last, when she can. "Well. It's still ash, isn't it?"

"It's still ash."

"And it's still our freedom."

"It is now," says Yara.

Quell looks up at the brass plate on the column of glass, lying on the ice where they set it down. *They burned the Book. And the ash was our freedom.*

"Should I change it?" she says.

Yara thinks about it.

"No," she says. "Leave it. It's true now. It just took longer."

They put the glass back over the Case together. It takes both of them.

* * *

Pim is on the step of the can when she gets home, sitting in the Gutter's dim bulb-light with his slate on his knees, the way Mitya sat there, and Sabeth Orrin before him. He doesn't cough when he sees her. He hasn't coughed in nine days. The Company's doctor finished his treatment on the *Lawful Remedy*, on the Clerk's order, at the Company's charge, before the black ship went back into the Lane with its satisfaction holds empty and its Factor confined to his cabin; and now Pim breathes the way other people breathe, without thinking about it, and every time Yara hears it she has to stop whatever she's doing and listen.

"I've written it all out," he says. "Both of them. Every word. I had to do the end of hers twice; I kept getting it wrong." He holds up the slate. "Advocate Dunn wants a copy for the Assembly. Quell wants one for the Church. A man came from Gallows Reach and asked if he could have one for his grandmother. I said yes." He looks at her. "Is that all right?"

"That's what it's for."

He nods. He doesn't ask about the Hulk. He hasn't, once, in nine days; he only looks at her sometimes, when he thinks she isn't watching, as if he's checking she's still there.

She sits down on the step beside him. She takes the key out of her jacket.

She's carried it for nine days. She hasn't known what to do with it. It's an iron key the length of her palm, old, worn, with a notched bit and a dull bead in the bow and a number on the shank so faint you have to know it's there. It doesn't open anything any more. The thing it opened is in a thousand pieces at the bottom of Absalom's clouds, and the thing it unlocked is in everyone.

She puts its chain over Pim's head.

He looks down at it on his chest. He picks it up and turns it over in his fingers in the bulb-light, the way he turns over every old thing he finds, and reads the number on the shank, and looks up at her.

"What's it for?"

"Nothing," says Yara. "Not any more." She looks at it, swinging on its chain against her brother's chest, over his lungs. "Don't sell it."

He laughs. He leans back against the hatch with the key on his chest and her mother's marks on the paint behind his head, two hundred and nine of them in fives and two short lines underneath, and he takes a breath — a long one, slow, all the way down, the way he couldn't for years — and lets it go.

And nobody, anywhere, is counting.
