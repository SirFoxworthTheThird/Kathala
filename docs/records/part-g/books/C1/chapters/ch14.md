# Chapter 14: We Were Cargo

*The Account · CR 929*

They took me down to Tamar in a sewage barge.

I want to be accurate about this, because it's the sort of thing people get wrong in the telling. It wasn't a sewage barge. It was a waste tender, a squat steel tank with an engine bolted to one end, that carries the Crown's night soil and kitchen slops and laundry sludge down to the reclamation plant under Saltmarch every evening, where it's broken down into water and fertiliser and gas for the refinery furnaces. Nothing on Tamar is wasted; the Houses are very proud of that. The tender has a cab for two crew and a crawlspace behind the cab, between the tank and the engine, that's warm from the exhaust and just big enough for a man to lie in if he doesn't mind the smell. Mags Tiller put me in it. She said it had carried a great many things the Office would have liked to know about over the years, and none of them had ever complained.

The pilot was a ballast man of about seventy with no teeth who sang the whole way down. Forty-one minutes. I counted. I lay in the crawlspace with my face against the warm steel and Ottilie's papers and Thorne's wafer against my chest and the key on its chain under them, and listened to him sing, and tried very hard to think about nothing at all.

I'd been a free man for about eleven hours. It felt like a great deal longer.

They'd put my face up on every screen in the station within an hour of my running. Mags showed me, in the laundry, before the barge: a public notice, in the Office's crimson border, with the photograph from my Assessor's file, which was nine years old and made me look like a man who'd just been told a joke he didn't get. *Hollis Fen, formerly Assessor of the Second Rank. Wanted for the murder of Keeper Ottilie Brask. Dangerous. Do not approach.* And underneath it, a statement from the Speaker's office, which I read three times before I understood it.

*The Houses grieve with the Office for the loss of a faithful Keeper, murdered by one of its own. It is now clear that Fen, a turncoat Assessor in the pay of the so-called Hold, was the instrument of that organisation's campaign of terror against the institutions of Liberty. The people of Tamar are asked to remain calm and to trust in the Mercy.*

"Turncoat," I said.

"Sounds nice, doesn't it?" said Mags. "Like a song." She folded the screen away into her apron. "They'll be singing it in the Crown by tomorrow. You'll see."

* * *

Calla Dray didn't spit this time. She looked at me for a long moment across the same table in the same cold room in the Sump, with the lamp between us, and then she laughed.

It wasn't a kind laugh. It wasn't unkind either. It was the laugh of someone who's been told the weather has changed in a way she'd predicted and nobody believed her.

"The Hold's man," she said. "Hollis Fen, Marsh's boy, three hundred and nine strikes, *in the pay of the Hold*." She shook her head. "Do you know how much the Hold has in its treasury, Assessor? Eighty-one marks. I counted it this morning. We couldn't pay you to tie your boots."

"I know."

"Sit down. You look like the Deep Ice." She pushed a bulb of something across the table. It was salt tea, the bitter kind they drink in Gallows Reach, and I hadn't had it since I was twelve, and my hands shook so badly picking it up that I spilled some on the table and had to watch it bead and freeze. "Tell me."

I told her. All of it: the vault, Thorne's eye, the Tamar water, the nine years, the silence. The founding page that described an entry it didn't contain. *Interesting. Not sufficient.* The lodging. The pen in my quarters and the coat I'd sold and the dealer who'd kept it. Bastian's face in the Chief's doorway. *He said it was the only way to keep her safe.* Wren.

She listened without moving. When I'd finished she sat back.

"So you have it," she said. "What Ottilie found. You've got it in your shirt. Proof the Account's a lie."

"Proof the founding page isn't what it says it is."

"That's what I said."

"It's not what the Concordance says." I put the bulb down. "Thorne won't refuse verification on that alone. He told me so. A forgery of a lost original can still be the best evidence of what the original said. He needs one of two things. The Book — which is ash — or a sworn lodging from an officer of the Office, uncompromised, saying the Office knows the Account is false."

"And you're an officer of the Office."

"I was. As of eleven hours ago I'm a murderer in the pay of the Hold." I laughed; I couldn't help it. "He'll never take my oath. Nobody would. Not with the pen and the coat and the Speaker's statement. I'm the most compromised man in the system."

Calla looked at me across the lamp. "Then we broadcast it."

"Calla—"

"We broadcast it. Everything. Ottilie's papers, your Concordance man's crystal, all of it, on every screen in Saltmarch and every ferry and every gallery in Gallows Reach. We tell them the Account was written nine years after the Burning by a council of thieves, and they've been paying a debt that never existed for three hundred years, and on Burning Day their Houses mean to sell them to the Concordance by the shipload. And then we see." Her voice was quite level. "We see what one point six million people do with that."

"I know what they'll do with it."

"So do I," said Calla Dray. "That's rather the point."

* * *

They gave me a cot in a room off the fish-gutting shed, and I didn't sleep, and at some point in the night an old man came and sat on the end of it.

He was a cistern man. I could tell by his hands, which were white and wrinkled and soft as a drowned man's from forty years of working wet, and by the smell of him, which was the flat chlorine smell of the station's water plant. His name was Teodor Rask, he said. He'd worked the Hulk's cisterns since before I was born — the great tanks along the spine that hold the station's water, that the old ship once used for ballast. He'd worked the night shift for twenty years, because at night there was nobody to watch him, and the Hold had found that useful.

"Calla says you're asking about the Keeper," he said.

"Yes."

"I'm on the Index level most nights. There's a valve gallery behind the Index wall, for the Third Cistern feed. I do the rounds at ten and twelve and two." He looked at his soft white hands. "The night she died. I was on the ten o'clock round. There's a grille in the valve gallery looks out on the Index hatch, through the wall. I always look. Habit. You see who's going in late."

I sat up.

"There was a boy," said Rask. "Young. Grey coat. Office pin. Nice-looking. He came along the passage at about ten past and stopped at the hatch and looked both ways — not like a thief; like a boy who's been told to look both ways and is doing it properly — and then he went in." He paused. "I came back on the twelve o'clock round. Half past eleven, it was, when I got to the grille. I'm slow these days. He was coming out. He had his gloves in his hand. He was holding them by the cuffs, like this—" he showed me, two fingers pinching the air — "away from his coat. Like they were wet."

"Did you tell anyone?"

"I told Calla. Nobody else." He looked at me with his old eyes. "Who'd I tell, Assessor? The proctors? The Office?" He stood up. "I don't know his name. I'd know him again."

"I know his name," I said.

I lay there for the rest of the night with my eyes open. I thought about tea in a bulb, and eighty marks lent without a question, and *you look terrible, sir, are you all right?* I thought about a boy of twenty-four from a steward's family in House Temperance, who wanted so badly to be useful, to be seen being useful, to rise. Who'd been my junior for a year, and stood beside me in aisle nine with his face the colour of cheese, and said *Sir, do you think it was the Hold?*

*He said it was the only way to keep her safe.*

I don't know whether he meant Wren or Ottilie. I've never known. I thought about that too.

* * *

The next day was the day before the day before the Burning, and I spent it trying to persuade Calla Dray not to start a war.

I won't give you all of it. It went on for hours, in the cold room and the gutting shed and once, briefly, shouting, in a passage down by the black sea at the bottom of the Sump, where the water laps against the ice in the dark and you can hear things moving in it that nobody's ever seen. I said she'd get thousands of people killed. She said the Houses were going to get a million and a half of them killed slowly instead, on worlds nobody could pronounce, and she'd rather do it quickly, here, at home, where at least they could see who was doing it. I said the Mercy Guard had a frigate and four cutters and three hundred armed men in the Crown. She said the Hold had one point six million people who'd been told for three hundred years that they owed their lives to the Houses, and it would be very interesting to see what happened when they found out they didn't.

"And Thorne?" I said. "The Concordance? They've got a squadron coming. If there's a rising in the station on the day the Bureau's verifying title, what do you think they'll do?"

"Load faster," said Calla.

In the end we came to it the way you come to anything with someone you'd both rather kill: one piece at a time, with long silences between.

The Hold would rise on Burning Day. That wasn't mine to change, and she wouldn't have let me if it was. Every year on Burning Day the ferries bring twenty thousand people up from Tamar to the Hulk for the memorial in the Old Hold — to stand among the empty cradles and mark their wrists with ash — and this year the Hold would be among them. Laundry workers and cistern men and waste haulers and kitchen staff were already inside. When the signing began in the Hall, they would take the Hall. They would take the station's broadcast room, which is two decks above the Hall, and they would put Ottilie's papers and Thorne's reading on every screen in the system.

But they would wait. Not long. As long as I could buy them.

"If you can get your lodging to the clerk," said Calla, "and he refuses, and the Sale falls without a shot — then we'll put down our pipes and go home and let the Houses explain to everyone what the clerk said and why. And you can have your peaceful little revolution of paper." She looked at me steadily. "But if he verifies, or if they stop you, or if you're not there — we go. We don't wait for anybody. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"And to get your lodging, you need to be clean."

"Yes."

"And to be clean," said Calla Dray, "you need the boy."

* * *

That night I wrote to my daughter.

The laundry serves the House wards' rooms in the Crown — clean sheets every day, clean towels, clean shifts for the children — and Mags said the girl's laundry came down with the rest, from a guest room in House Prudence's section, under the name of a House Prudence ward. She said a note could go up in the folds of a towel. She said she'd sent a great many things up in towels in her time.

I didn't know what to write. I sat at the table in the cold room with a scrap of paper and a pencil for a long time. Then I remembered.

When Wren was nine, and I was still allowed to see her every week, I'd taught her Ottilie's cipher. The Catechism of Mercy, line and word and letter. I don't know why. Because it was a game, and I'd loved it at twelve, and I wanted her to have something of mine that wasn't a portion. She'd been very good at it. Much better than me. She'd sent me messages in it for a year afterward, on the backs of her school drawings — *DA YOU SMELL OF FERRY*, *MAM SAYS NO* — and then she'd turned ten and lost interest, the way children do, and I'd kept the drawings in a box that had burned, I supposed, with everything else in Lower Crown Seven.

I wrote it in the cipher. I don't know if she'd remember. I hoped she would.

*Happy birthday. I am sorry I am not there. I did not do what they say. Be brave. On the Burning, stay by a door.*

It came to eleven lines of numbers. I folded it very small and gave it to Mags, and she tucked it into the hem of her apron, and looked at me for a moment with her boiled pink face, and said, "You've got a girl."

"Yes."

"How old?"

"Sixteen," I said. "Tomorrow."

Mags nodded slowly. "Mine was sixteen when they struck her man," she said. "First time. Twenty-two years ago. Lot of good it did me, being sixteen." She patted the hem. "I'll put it in the softest one."

* * *

Calla came and sat with me later. I don't know why. I think she couldn't sleep either.

She brought a bottle — not tea, this time; ice spirit, the colourless kind they distil in the galleries out of refinery alcohol and kelp, that burns like the Deep Ice — and two bulbs, and sat on the end of the cot where the cistern man had sat, and poured, and didn't say anything for a while.

"What's that?" she said at last.

I'd taken the key out from under my shirt without noticing. I do it when I'm thinking. I was turning it over in my fingers on its chain, the way I'd turned it a thousand times, and I looked down at it as though somebody else had put it there.

"My mother's," I said.

"Can I see?"

I held it out on its chain. She took it in her damaged hand, the one with two fingers gone, and turned it over in the lamplight. A long iron key the length of my palm, its bit cut in an intricate pattern of notches, its bow set with that dull bead of something that might have been glass. Very old. Very worn. You could see where generations of thumbs had rubbed the shank smooth.

"Ice families all have something," said Calla. "Did you know that?"

"I know."

"Mine had a spoon. Pewter. With a ship on the handle — a long thing, with a dome at one end, like a seed. My grandmother said it came from the Crossing. She used to let me hold it on Burning Day." She turned the key over. "My mother sold it the year Jory was struck, to pay the shortfall. Forty marks. The dealer said it was the oldest thing he'd ever bought. He's probably sold it to some House family by now, to put in a cabinet."

"Mine said not to sell it," I said. "That was all she said, when they lifted me. *Don't sell it.*"

"And you didn't."

"I didn't."

"What does it open?"

"Nothing. Nobody knows. It's just old." I took it back from her and let it hang. "Every family in Gallows Reach has some piece of junk like it. You know that."

"Gallows Reach," said Calla. "The first ones sent down." She drank. "Do you know who they were? The first Struck? The very first ones, the year of Landfall?"

"No."

"Neither does anyone. The Houses never wrote it down. Or they did and it's in a drawer somewhere in your Office." She looked at the key swinging on its chain between us. "Strange, isn't it. That they'd send people down to the ice to found the oldest town on Tamar and not write down who they were. You'd think they'd want to remember. You'd think they'd want them in the Catechism. *Who did we send to the ice?*"

"Maybe they were ashamed."

"The Houses?" Calla laughed. "The Houses have never been ashamed of anything in three hundred years."

She was quiet for a while. Then she said, without looking at me, "What did he say? Jory. When you struck him. You remember them all, they say."

I remembered.

"He asked me how long," I said. "I told him two years. He nodded. Then he said, *Will you tell my sister I'm sorry about the boots.* That was all. I didn't know what it meant. I wrote it down. It was in the file." I looked at her. "I never told you. I should have. It was in the file for seven years."

Calla Dray sat very still on the end of the cot with the bulb in her damaged hand.

"I had a pair of boots," she said at last. "Good ones. Saltmarch leather. I saved for them for a year. He borrowed them the week before, to go to a dance in the Clear quarter, and he got drunk and lost one in a canal." She laughed — a strange, short, surprised sound — and put her other hand over her face. "I didn't speak to him for three days. Then you came." She sat with her hand over her face for a long time. Then she took it away and poured another for both of us, very steadily, and drank hers, and stood up.

"Get some sleep, Fen," she said. "Tomorrow we go and fetch your boy."

* * *

The squadron came out of the Lane the next morning.

I watched it in the gutting shed with forty Hold people and two hundred fish, on a screen somebody had wired to the Saltmarch public feed. There's a camera on the ice shelf that watches the sky for the ferries, and someone in the feed office had turned it toward the Lane point, the way you'd turn your head toward a noise, and kept it there.

You could see the Lane point from Tamar if you knew where to look: a faint smear of light in the black above Absalom's rim, where space is thin. Mostly nothing came out of it. For three hundred years nothing came out of it at all. Then six years ago the first Concordance survey ship, and since then, every year or so, one or two more. Never more than two.

Five came out that morning, one after another, and slid into Tamar orbit in a line.

Two were escorts. Long and white and smooth, like the *Probity*, but meaner, with their weapons showing now — clusters of dark blisters along their flanks. They took station at either end of the line. Between them came the transports.

I've tried to describe them since and I can't do it. They were bigger than the Hulk. Each one. Bigger than the Hulk and the Crown and the docks and everything else in Increase Station together: vast blunt white cylinders, ribbed like the inside of a fish, with rows of hatches down their sides, hundreds of them, standing open. They had no lights showing except at their bows. The rest of them was dark. Not dark like a ship at night. Dark like a room nobody lives in yet.

"What are those?" said a child at the front of the shed.

Nobody answered her.

I stood at the back with my arms folded and counted. I couldn't help it. Ottilie's lot list was in my shirt, and I knew the figures by heart: lot one, Gallows Reach, eleven thousand four hundred. Lot two, the Deep Ice. Lots three to seven, the refineries. Eight, the Sump. Nine, the Clear quarter partials. Three hundred and six names. Eleven lines below my daughter's.

The transports hung in the black above Tamar with their hatches open. I didn't know how many people each of them could hold. I didn't want to know. I found I was working it out anyway — from the length of them, and the spacing of the hatches, and the size of a cradle in the Old Hold, which I'd stood among every Burning Day of my life — and I stopped, because I didn't like the number, and then I worked it out again to check.

"Like hulls," said Calla softly, beside me. I hadn't heard her come. "Waiting to be filled."

On the screen, very slowly, the five ships settled into their places in orbit and were still. A caption came up across the bottom of the picture, in the Speaker's office's elegant script: *The Houses welcome our friends of the Concordance of Worlds, who come to share in the celebration of the three hundred and eighteenth Burning.*

Somebody at the front of the shed began to laugh, and then stopped.

Wren was sixteen that day. I hadn't forgotten. I stood in the gutting shed with forty people and two hundred fish and the transports hanging on the screen, and I thought: she's sixteen, she's Clear by the law of every year but this one, and she's in a room in House Prudence with a guard on the door and a note in a towel that she might not be able to read. I thought: tomorrow. I thought: Bastian. And I found that my hand had gone up under my shirt and was holding my mother's key, hard, the way you'd hold the rail of a spoke car that has stopped between levels, and I couldn't make it let go.
