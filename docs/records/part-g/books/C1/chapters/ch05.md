# Chapter 5: The Dead Accounts

*The Account · CR 929*

I went into the dead accounts at three in the morning, ten days before the Burning, because three in the morning is when the Index is empty and the cleaners have gone and the only people awake in the spine of the Hulk are the proctors on the vault door, who don't care where an Assessor goes as long as it isn't through them.

I'd never been that far in. Nobody goes that far in. The living accounts are near the hatch, where the light is good and the cabinets are new and the clerks come and go all day with their trays of cards; the Clear accounts are further back, in the older steel; and past them, past a bulkhead where the gallery narrows and the light panels are spaced so far apart that you swim from one pool of light to the next through the dark, are the dead. Three hundred years of them. Every family that ever held a portion and died out, or was extinguished — struck to the last surety, or wiped out in the fevers of the 700s, or simply stopped — closed and cross-referenced and filed away in cabinets so old that the steel has gone brown and the labels are handwritten. Nobody looks at them. There's nothing in them anyone can collect.

D-0447 was in the sixth cabinet, eleventh row, a drawer at knee height that stuck when I pulled it and then came out all at once with a squeal I felt in my teeth. I held my breath and listened. Nothing. The scrubbers hummed. Somewhere far back toward the hatch, a proctor coughed.

The card at the front of the drawer was what it should have been. *D-0447. Portion of MATTOCK, Gallows Reach. Closed CR 744 — extinction of line (fever).* A family of ice-cutters, dead for nearly two hundred years, with a closing balance of nil. There were a few dozen cards behind it — the Mattock portion's history, quarter by quarter, payment by payment, strike by strike, down to the last of them. I thumbed through them, slowly, by the light of my handset. Nothing. Births and deaths and quarters paid. A strike in 731. A Clearance refused in 738.

Then, right at the back of the drawer, where the cards stopped and there was a finger's width of space before the steel, something that wasn't a card. A fold of paper. Thin, almost translucent, the kind Ottilie bought in Saltmarch by the ream for her notes, and on it, in her blue-black ink, in her small precise hand, rows and rows of numbers.

I'd have known it anywhere.

When I was twelve and a new scholarship boy, frightened of everything in the Hulk and sure I'd be sent back down to the ice any day for some failure I didn't understand yet, Ottilie used to set me puzzles. I think she did it to keep me from crying. She'd write a message in numbers and leave it on my bunk, and I'd have to work out what it said, and when I did there'd be a treat waiting somewhere — a sweet, a book, once a little clockwork bird she'd had made in Saltmarch that sang four notes. The key was always the same. It was the Catechism of Mercy. *Because it's the only text*, she told me, *that every person on Tamar knows by heart and nobody ever listens to.* Each number was a word: the line of the Catechism, then the word in the line, then the letter in the word. *Three-one-one* was *W*, from *What*. *Seven-four-two* was *U*, from *our*. It was a child's cipher. It would have taken the Office's code clerks about four minutes.

But the Office's code clerks don't open the dead accounts.

I didn't decode it there. I folded it into my coat and closed the drawer, slowly, so it wouldn't squeal, and swam back down the dark gallery from pool to pool of light, and at the bulkhead where the dead accounts end I found I was holding the paper against my chest through my coat with my hand flat, the way you'd hold a wound.

* * *

It took me most of what was left of the night, in my quarters, with the door locked. Not because it was hard. Because my hands kept stopping.

She hadn't written much. A dozen fragments, some only a few words long, as if she'd been writing them over days, a line at a time, whenever she had a moment alone. Some of them I couldn't make sense of. Some of them I could make sense of and wished I couldn't.

*The Account was not copied. It was composed.*

That was the first. Then:

*Founding page claims true copy of the Book's last state at Landfall, sealed by the Purser. Seal is not a purser's seal.*

And:

*The Account is silent. Every Charter book keeps the purser's voice. Listened to the founding page twice. Nothing.*

And, by itself, underlined twice:

*Ask the rings.*

I sat looking at that one for a long time. *You read it like the rings of a tree.* Every entry grows a layer in the lattice; look at the layer and you can see when it was written. Every Keeper knew it. Every Keeper who'd ever been trained by Ottilie Brask knew it twice over, because she wouldn't let you forget. But you can't read the rings of the master Account without being in the vault with it, and nobody goes into the vault but the Chief Assessor and the Keepers of the Account and, on the first day of every quarter, the Speaker of the Houses, who comes to watch the quarter's strikes and Clearances grown into the lattice and says a prayer over it. And even if you got in, you'd need a reader much finer than anything the Office has — fine enough to count rings three hundred years old, one from the next, the way you'd count the rings on a pine stump with a lens.

The last fragment was the shortest.

*H — if you are reading this, I was right to be frightened. I'm sorry. Burn this.*

I didn't burn it. I sat there with it in my hands until my handset told me it was six o'clock, and then I washed my face and shaved and put on a clean shirt and my grey coat and my brass pin, and went to work.

I remembered, while I was shaving, the one time I'd been in the vault.

I was thirteen. It was the day of my first Burning on the Hulk, and Ottilie, who had no business doing it, had taken me in for a minute while the Keepers were at the ceremony in the Hall and the proctors on the door were drunk on House wine. She'd wanted me to see it. *You should know what you're serving*, she said. I remember the cold, and the dark iron walls, and the smell — old metal, old air, something like cloves. I remember the Account itself: a great cube of crystal the colour of smoke, a metre on a side, lying in a cradle that was much too small for it, a padded iron bracket built for something a tenth its size, so that the Account sat on top of it rather than in it, strapped down with steel bands. *That was the Book's cradle*, Ottilie said. *They put the Account in it after the Burning. For continuity.* She'd said *continuity* in a way I didn't understand then.

I remember a brass plaque on the wall, covered in tiny writing. I remember a little steel safe set into the wall beneath it, a hand's breadth square, with a keyhole and no handle. *The purser's box*, she said. *Nobody's opened it in three hundred years. Nobody has the key. The Houses tried drills on it once and it ate the drills.* And I remember a shelf beside the cradle, with three iron boxes strapped to it in a row, each the size of a big book, each with a lead seal across its hasp and a number stamped on its cover. VOYAGE I. VOYAGE II. VOYAGE IV.

*Where's Three?* I asked her.

*Lost*, she said. *Before Landfall. Nobody knows how.* She shrugged. *Things get lost on a voyage. Come on, before the proctors sober up.*

I hadn't thought of it in twenty-eight years. I thought of it now, with the razor at my throat, and I didn't know why, and it made my hand slip, and I bled into the basin for a while and watched it go round.

* * *

"You look terrible," said Lucan Marsh.

"I didn't sleep."

"No. None of us did." He poured me tea. The Chief Assessor's office is in the old Charter quartermaster's cabin, near the vault, in zero gravity, and he takes his tea from a bulb with a sipping straw like everyone else in the spine; but he serves it from a real pot, a squat brown glazed thing with a magnetic base that he'd had made on Tamar, because he says a man ought to be able to pour something for his guests. In the corner of the office, floating in a glass sphere the size of a head, was his juniper — a bonsai, two hundred years old, which he said had come up from the ice in the time of his great-great-grandmother and which he watered every morning with a mist from a little brass bulb. It had grown in zero gravity all its life, and so it had no idea which way was up, and its branches went everywhere at once, twisted and dense and very beautiful, like something drawn by a child who'd never seen a tree. He told me once it was the only thing in the Hulk he'd save in a fire.

He looked old this morning. Older than I'd ever seen him. He'd known Ottilie longer than I had. They'd come up through the Office together, forty years ago, and there'd been a story, in my time as a junior, that once they'd been more than colleagues. I'd never asked.

"Tell me," he said.

I told him about the ferry passengers. The proctors had traced the two ballast tickets in the night: false names, bought for cash at a Saltmarch kiosk that didn't keep faces. I told him about the paint, Charter script and not ice-script, and the beads on the wrong side of her coat. I told him about her fingers, and her spectacles.

I didn't tell him about the shoe. I don't know why. I'd meant to. I'd come into the office meaning to lay the card on his desk and the paper beside it and say *Look what she left me*, the way I'd have laid my sums in front of him at twelve. I opened my mouth to do it, and then I looked at the juniper going every way at once in its sphere, and I closed my mouth again.

"So you don't think it was the Hold," he said.

"I think someone painted the Hold on it."

"The Hold paints itself on everything." He sighed. "Hollis. I want you to listen to me, because I'm going to say this once and then I'm going to let you get on with it. Ottilie had—" he searched for the word — "enthusiasms, these last months. She'd been working very late. She'd been going into the dead accounts, which she had no reason to do, and into the old Charter manifests, which she had less reason to do. She'd started saying things at Keepers' meetings about the founding of the Account that made the Keepers of the Account uncomfortable. She was sixty-three. Some people become antiquarians as they get older. It's a kind of grief, I think. You start looking back for something you've lost and you think it's in the archives." He looked at me over his tea. "I don't know what she found, if she found anything. I don't want to know. I want whoever killed her struck, and I want it done before the Burning. Don't let her ghosts pull you down with her. Find the killer. Leave the rest alone."

"Yes, sir."

"Good." He put down his bulb. "There's something else. The verifier docks at eleven."

"I thought he was due tomorrow."

"He made good time. The Concordance always makes good time; it's one of their charms." He said it lightly, but there was something under it. "Speaker Constance will meet him at the dock. The Office will provide his liaison for the duration of his visit — escort him, show him whatever he asks to see, answer his questions. I want it to be you."

I stared at him. "Sir, I'm on Ottilie."

"You can be on both. He'll be in the vault with the Keepers most of the time; he won't need you there." He smiled slightly. "And frankly, Hollis, I want someone with the verifier whom I trust to tell me what he looks at. Not what the Speaker's people tell me he looks at. What he actually looks at. Do you understand?"

I understood. I understood that the man who'd lifted me out of the ice at twelve had just asked me, in the kindest possible voice, to spy on the Concordance for him; and I understood, a moment later, with a coldness I can still feel, that it put me beside the one person in the system who'd be allowed into the vault with a reader fine enough to count rings.

"Yes, sir," I said.

* * *

The Concordance survey cruiser *Probity* came in to the station's long dock at eleven exactly, and I'd never seen anything like her.

Our ships are ugly. The Mercy Guard's cutters are squat armoured wedges studded with sensor blisters and gun ports, and the ferries are boxes, and even the Speaker's courier yacht, which is the prettiest thing in the system, is only a sleek grey dart with a gilded stripe down its side. The *Probity* was four hundred metres long and white — white all over, smooth as an egg, without a seam or a port or a weapon showing anywhere on her — and she slid up to the dock on thrusters so fine I couldn't see their flames, and touched, and was still. Through the dock windows, the crowd of House stewards and Office clerks and Mercy Guard officers in dress blue went silent, the way a crowd goes silent when it sees something it can't afford.

Severine Constance was at the front of the welcoming party, of course.

I'd seen the Speaker of the Houses perhaps a dozen times in my life, always at a distance: at the Burning, in the Hall, reading the Mercy; once, in the Office, coming out of the vault after the quarter's prayer with her face very still. She was fifty-four. She was tall and thin and grey-eyed, with silver hair cut close to her skull in the Crown fashion, and she wore the Speaker's robe of plain unbleached linen — the Houses dress plainly; it's part of the Mercy — so well that it looked like the most expensive thing in the room. House Constance was the first of the Seven. Its Speakers had led the Houses for most of three centuries. You didn't say that, in the Crown, any more than you said the names of the Founders. You only knew it, the way you knew where the light was coming from.

The *Probity*'s lock opened. One man came out.

I'd expected — I don't know what I'd expected. An admiral. A delegation. The Concordance of Worlds is two hundred and eleven settled systems and seven hundred billion people and the largest navy in the history of the human species, and it had been six years since its first survey ship came out of the Long Lane after three centuries of silence and told us we weren't alone any more. I'd expected trumpets. What came out of the *Probity* was a lean, middle-aged man in a plain black uniform with a narrow band of silver at the collar, carrying a document case, with close-cut grey hair and a long, patient, rather tired face, and in his left eye socket, where an eye should have been, a lens.

It was the size of an eye and the shape of an eye, and it moved like an eye, but it was faceted crystal, pale gold, and when the dock lights caught it it threw back tiny sparks like a cut stone. It moved independently of his other eye. As he came down the ramp toward the Speaker, the right eye looked at her and the left looked at the room — swept it, end to end, quickly and thoroughly, like a clerk running a finger down a column — and then, for no more than a second, stopped on me.

"Verifier-Commander Isaak Thorne," he said to the Speaker, and bowed very slightly. "Bureau of Titles, Concordance of Worlds. Thank you for receiving me."

There were speeches. I don't remember them. I stood at the back of the welcoming party with my hands behind my back and watched the crystal eye, and when the speeches were over Marsh caught my sleeve and brought me forward and said, "Commander, may I present Assessor Hollis Fen, of the Office of the Account, who'll be your liaison during your visit," and Thorne looked at me with both eyes at once for the first time.

"Assessor," he said.

"Commander."

"You're the one investigating the murder of the Keeper."

I didn't look at Marsh. I didn't look at the Speaker. "Yes, Commander."

"I was told about it on approach. I'm sorry." He didn't sound sorry. He didn't sound anything. He had a flat, even voice with no accent I could place, as if it had been planed smooth. "I'd like to begin in the vault this afternoon. Is that possible?"

It was possible. Everything was possible for him; the Speaker had made that clear. I walked him from the dock up through the spoke to the spine, while his luggage followed with two silent Concordance ratings in white, and I showed him where the handholds were and how to move in zero gravity, which he already knew, and he asked me questions in his planed-smooth voice. How many portions were held. How many Clear. How many struck in the last year. How long the Index had been kept on film and why. He listened to every answer without comment. At the Office hatch he stopped and looked up at the red letters over the vault door at the end of the passage.

"*What is written is owed*," he said. "Article Four of the Purser's Ordinances. Did you know that?"

"No, Commander."

"Charter law. The Ordinances were the Charter's code for ship's pursers. The Concordance inherited the Charter's legal code when it absorbed the old Charter worlds, and the Bureau inherited the Ordinances along with everything else." He looked at the letters with his crystal eye, and the eye made a tiny sound, a click, like a lens focusing. "Your Account claims title under them. That's why I'm here. Not to judge your society, Assessor; I want to be clear about that, because people always assume. I'm not here to decide whether the Liberty Debt is just. I'm here to decide whether the instrument by which your Houses claim to hold the debts of this system is what it says it is."

"And if it is?"

"Then it is," said Thorne. "Paper is paper."

His crystal eye flicked down. Not at my face — lower. At my chest, where under my shirt, on its long chain, my mother's key hung against my breastbone.

"You're carrying lattice," he said.

I'd forgotten it was there. You do, with things you've worn for twenty-nine years. I put my hand to it, through my shirt, without meaning to.

"A family thing," I said.

"Charter work," said Thorne. "Old. Very old." The eye clicked again. "Is it Office property?"

"It was my mother's."

"Ah," he said, and looked at me for a moment longer with both eyes, and then nodded and went on through the hatch, and never mentioned it again.

* * *

That evening, after I'd left him in the guest quarters the Speaker had given him in the Crown — a suite of rooms with real windows and a garden — I took the ferry down to Saltmarch and went looking for the Hold.

Saltmarch hangs from the underside of Tamar's ice shelf like a wasps' nest from the eaves of a roof. The top of it, near the shelf, is the Clear quarter, where Imke and Wren live: domes and terraces and real glass, lit from above by panels that imitate a sky nobody under the ice has ever seen. Below that are the working districts, the refineries and the belts and the stacks of ballast flats, tier on tier, getting narrower and darker as they go down. And at the bottom, where the city meets the black sea under the ice, is the Sump.

The proctors don't go into the Sump at night. The Office doesn't go into it at all, if it can help it. I'd been there twice in twenty years, both times with an escort of six, and both times I'd come out with fewer people than I went in with. I went in that night alone, in a ballast coat over my grey, with my pin in my pocket and my sidearm under my arm and no idea at all what I was doing except that I'd run out of other things to do.

It took four hours and most of the money in my pockets. A tea-seller who knew a woman who knew a man. A back room behind a fish-gutting shed that smelled so strongly of the sea under the ice that my eyes ran. A hood over my head, and two sets of hands on my arms, and a long walk down a passage that sloped and turned and sloped again, and a door, and a chair, and then the hood came off and I was sitting in a little room carved out of the ice with a single lamp, and across a table from me was Calla Dray.

I knew her face. Everyone in the Office knew her face; it was on every proctor's wall in Saltmarch. Thirty-four, dark, wiry, with cropped black hair and a long white scar from her left ear to the corner of her mouth, and two fingers missing from her left hand — the third and the fourth, gone at the knuckle — where the Deep Ice had taken them. She'd done four years down there, for her father's portion. She'd come back up and organised the Hold in the six years since. The Office had a price on her I'd have needed to be an Assessor for another century to earn.

She looked at me for a long time without speaking. Then she said, "Hollis Fen."

"Yes."

"Second Rank. Lifted out of Gallows Reach at twelve. Marsh's boy." She said it flatly, as if reading off a card. "Three hundred and eight strikes. Am I right? Three hundred and eight."

"Three hundred and nine," I said. "There was one this week."

"Haskell. The refinery boy. Fourteen months." She leaned forward. "You struck my brother, Assessor. Do you remember? You must; you remember them all, they say. Jory Dray. Gallows Reach thirty-one. Seven years ago. Two years for six hundred marks."

"I remember."

"He died in the eighth month. A lance blew out. They brought him up in a sack." She was very calm. "I want you to know that I know that. Before we talk. I want you to know that I know, and that you're sitting in my room, and that nobody in the world knows you're here."

"I know it," I said.

She spat. Not at me, quite. On the floor between us, deliberately, the way you'd mark a line.

"Ottilie Brask," she said.

"Yes."

"You think we killed her."

"No," I said. "I think someone wants me to think you did. I want to know why."

She looked at me with her dark eyes for a long moment. Then she sat back.

"She came to us," Calla said. "Two months ago. Through a laundress in the Crown who carries for us. Said she was a Keeper of the Office and she'd found something and she didn't know who else to trust. We thought it was a trap. Of course we did. We watched her for a month. Then we met her — once, down here, in the dark, the way you've met me." She shrugged. "She said she'd found something that would end the Account. Those were her words. Not *hurt*. Not *embarrass*. *End.* She said she needed a little more time to be sure, and then she'd bring it to us, and we'd know what to do with it. She was supposed to come down on the twenty o'clock ferry, three nights ago. We sent two of ours up on the same boat to bring her back safe."

"The ballast tickets."

"The ballast tickets. They got to the Office and found it full of proctors and the Index sealed. They heard she was dead. They ran. They're in the ice now; you won't find them, so don't try." She looked at me. "We didn't kill her, Assessor. We needed her alive more than anyone in the system."

"Do you know what she found?"

"No." She said it bitterly. "She wouldn't say. She said it was safer if only one person knew until she was certain." Calla's mouth twisted. "She was wrong about that."

I sat there in the cold little room with the lamp and the spit on the floor. After a while I said, "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you came down here alone," said Calla Dray. "Nobody from the Office has ever come into the Sump alone. Either you're stupid, or you're frightened of something worse than us. I'd like to know which." She stood up. "I'll give you one thing, Assessor. One. Not because I like you; I'd cut your throat for Jory and sleep well. Because she trusted you."

I stared at her. "She told you that?"

"She said if anything happened to her, there was an Assessor called Fen who might come looking. She said he was a good boy who'd done bad things, and that he'd know her handwriting." Calla's face did something complicated. "The laundress. Mags Tiller, in the Crown laundries, lower ring. She carried Ottilie's notes. She might have seen something. Ask her, if you can find her before the proctors do. That's all you get." She nodded to someone behind me. "Put his hood on. Take him up."

* * *

I got back to the Hulk on the last ferry, at two in the morning, with the hood-marks still red on my neck. My quarters are in the lower Crown, where junior House families and senior Office staff live in a kind of polite proximity — two rooms, a window onto the dark, a bed with real gravity under it. I let myself in. I took off my coat. I was halfway across the room toward the bed before I stopped.

Everything was where it should have been. That was what stopped me. My quarters are never exactly where they should be. I'm untidy; Imke used to say I lived like a man waiting to be evicted. There are always papers on the table and a shirt on the chair and my second boots kicked under the bed at an angle. The papers were on the table. The shirt was on the chair. The boots were under the bed. But the papers were squared to the edge of the table, and the shirt was folded over the chair-back, and the boots were side by side, toes out, like a soldier's.

Someone had been through every inch of the place. Someone careful, who'd put everything back exactly where it had been, and hadn't known that *exactly* was the one thing my rooms had never been.

I stood very still in the middle of the floor. Ottilie's paper was inside my shirt, against my skin. It had been there all day. I put my hand flat on it.

My handset chimed. The personal tone.

"Da?" Wren's voice was small and wide awake. "I'm sorry. I know it's late. Mam's asleep. I couldn't—"

"It's all right, love. What is it?"

"There was a man today. From the Office. He came to the flat after school, when Mam was at work." She swallowed. "He was very polite. He had a pin like yours. He said it was routine. He wanted to know about my portion — how much was left, and whether you were still paying it, and when my birthday was." A pause. "He asked when my birthday was twice, Da. He wrote it down. Why would they want to know when my birthday is? They know when my birthday is. It's in the Account."

I looked at my boots, side by side under the bed, toes out.

"What did he look like?" I said.

"Young. Nice-looking. A Crown voice. He said his name but I didn't—" She stopped. "Is it something bad? Did I do something?"

"No," I said. "No, love. You didn't do anything. It's routine." I made my voice level. It's the one thing they train you for. "Go back to bed. I'll come down and see you soon. I promise."

That was two.
