# Chapter 8: The Sale

*The Account · CR 929*

The Crown laundries are in the lowest deck of the ring, under the gardens, where the spin is strongest and the water falls hardest and nobody from the Houses has ever been. You go down to them by a service stair behind the House Patience kitchens, and the air gets warmer and wetter with every step until by the bottom it's like breathing soup. Then there's a long low hall full of steam and noise, and machines the size of ferry cabins churning the Houses' linen in soapy water drawn up from the cisterns in the spine, and two hundred ballast women in rubber aprons feeding them.

Mags Tiller was at the end machine. She saw me before I saw her. I knew because she stopped feeding sheets into the drum, and stood very still with a sheet in her red hands, and every woman on that side of the hall stopped too and looked at me, and then looked away, all together, the way a shoal of fish turns.

"Mrs Tiller."

"I don't know you."

"No. Calla Dray sent me."

She was a heavy woman of sixty or so, with forearms like hams and a face boiled pink by forty years of steam. She looked at me — at my grey coat, which I hadn't thought to change, and at the place on my lapel where my pin would be if I'd been wearing it — and I watched her decide whether to scream.

"You're the second," she said.

"The second what?"

"Assessor. This week. Asking." She fed the sheet into the drum without looking at it. Her hands were shaking. "The other one came Tuesday. Young. Nice manners. Crown voice. Asked had I ever carried anything for the Keeper. I said I carried sheets." She fed in another. "He said if I remembered anything else I should come and see him. He said his name. I didn't keep it."

Tuesday was the day after Ottilie died. The day before I'd gone into the dead accounts. Someone had been a day ahead of me the whole way.

"Did you carry anything for her, Mrs Tiller?"

She didn't answer. She fed sheets. After a while she said, very low, under the noise of the drums, "Calla really sent you?"

"Calla said Ottilie trusted me. She said I'd know the handwriting."

Mags Tiller looked at me sideways through the steam for a long moment. Then she reached into the pocket of her rubber apron and took out something small and folded and pressed it into my hand, fast, palm to palm, the way you pass a coin to a beggar so the Houses won't see.

"She gave me that the morning she died," she said. "To take down to Calla on my day off. And then she was dead and the proctors were all over and I didn't dare. I've had it in my apron since." She turned back to her machine. "Don't come back. Don't ever come back here. I've got grandchildren."

I didn't come back. I went up the service stair and out into the Crown gardens, where the light panels in the ceiling were doing a very good morning and House children were feeding fish in an ornamental pool, and I sat down on a bench under a fig tree with my back to the trunk and unfolded the paper.

Ottilie's blue-black ink. Ottilie's numbers. More of them than before — two sides, close-written, the last thing she ever wrote. I took out my handset, called up the Catechism, and began.

* * *

It took an hour. When I'd finished I sat under the fig tree for another hour and didn't move.

I'll give it to you as she wrote it. I've had a long time since to think about whether to soften it, and I've decided not to. She didn't.

*C. — Found it. In the Chief's correspondence. I index everything the Chief signs, it's my job, and he forgot. Conveyance of the Account of Liberty to the Ostrava Labor Trust, a Concordance contractor. Every un-Clear portion in the Account. One point six million. Price agreed with Speaker C. Drafted by M. in his own hand.*

*Concordance Bureau of Titles to verify the Houses' title to the Account. If verified, Account passes to Ostrava. Ostrava may then take satisfaction of the portions under Concordance labour law. Transport to Ostrava projects on worlds I cannot pronounce. Contract labour. Terms of satisfaction: the portion as written, with interest, at Ostrava rates. They will not be coming back.*

*Signing set for Burning Day, in the Hall. Before the Ash.*

*In return the Seven Houses receive Concordance peerage, citizenship and estates on Concordance worlds, and a share of the price. The Office's senior Assessors are promised citizenship. I was promised citizenship. I was asked to prepare the Account for verification.*

*That is how I found the other thing. You know what I mean. I have told you. I am nearly sure now.*

*Lots by district. Copied below. Gallows Reach is lot one. They are going to take the ice first.*

And then, below, in a tight column of numbers that took me twenty minutes on its own, she'd copied out the lot list — the order in which the portions of Tamar would be satisfied, district by district, with the number of people in each lot and the transport that would carry them. Gallows Reach, lot one, eleven thousand four hundred. The Deep Ice, lot two, all of it. The Saltmarch refinery districts, lots three to seven. The Sump, lot eight.

Lot nine was the Saltmarch Clear quarter. Not the Clear — the Clear were Clear, they had no portions to sell. The un-Clear living in it. The partials. The children of one Clear parent and one Cleared, with their small inherited shares of Liberty still outstanding, waiting for a sixteenth birthday or a last payment. Ottilie had copied out the names in lot nine in full, because there were only three hundred and six of them. I found Wren's on the second side, eleven lines from the bottom. *Fen, W. Saltmarch, Upper Terrace 4. Portion outstanding: 406 m.*

I read it, and then I read it again, and I found I was counting the lines below it, the way you'd count the steps down to a door you don't want to open. Eleven. There were eleven lines below her.

*M. in his own hand.*

There are a lot of people in the Office of the Account whose names begin with M. That's what I told myself, sitting under the fig tree. There's Morrow in Clearances and Maddox in the strike office and Merriam the Keeper of the Account, who'd been Ottilie's rival for twenty years. There's the Chief Assessor, who drafts every conveyance the Office makes, by right of his office, because that's what the Chief Assessor is for. It didn't mean anything. A man can put his hand to a thing he didn't choose. I'd done it three hundred and nine times.

I folded the paper and put it in my shirt with the other one. Then I went to find the Verifier.

* * *

Thorne was in his garden.

The Speaker had given him one of the best suites in the Crown, in House Constance's own section of the ring, and the best thing about it was a walled garden open to the light panels, with gravel paths and beds of Tamar ice-moss and a little fountain that ran with real water. When I came in through the gate he was sitting on a stone bench beside the fountain in his plain black uniform with a slate on his knee, reading, and he didn't look up.

"Assessor," he said. "You're not on my schedule until fourteen."

"No, Commander."

"Sit down." He touched the slate and it went dark. "You look as though you've been told something."

I didn't sit. I stood in front of him on the gravel with my hands in my coat pockets, and I could feel Ottilie's papers against my skin under my shirt, and I said, "Ostrava."

He looked up at me then, with both eyes. The crystal one clicked.

"Ah," he said.

"You knew."

"Of course I knew. I've read the conveyance. It's the reason I'm here." He gestured at the bench beside him. "Sit down, Assessor. I'm not going to have this conversation craning my neck."

I sat. The fountain ran. Somewhere beyond the garden wall, House children were laughing.

"Your Houses approached the Concordance six years ago," said Thorne, "when our first survey ship came through the Lane. They wished to negotiate the integration of the Absalom system into the Concordance on terms favourable to themselves. Integration takes many forms. The Concordance has absorbed two hundred and eleven systems; it has a procedure for most of them. One of the procedures concerns systems whose economies are founded on bonded labour — there are more of those than you'd imagine; the Charter was very busy, once. In such cases the Concordance's law allows the bonds to be assigned to a licensed labour trust, on verification of title, and the trust may then take satisfaction of them under our own labour code." He spoke as he always did, in that flat, planed voice, like a man reading out a timetable. "Ostrava is the largest such trust. It works on terraforming projects in the outer systems. The conditions are hard. The contracts are long. They are, under our law, entirely legal."

"They're going to ship a million and a half people off this system and work them to death."

"They are going to take satisfaction of a debt," said Thorne, "if the debt is valid. That's all I'm here to determine."

"And if it's valid, you'll certify it."

"Yes."

"Knowing what it's for."

"Knowing what it's for." He looked at me. "I verify title, Assessor. That is my office. What title is used for is not my office. If I began deciding which valid debts ought to be enforced and which ought not, I would not be a verifier any longer; I would be a judge, and the Concordance has a great many of those, and they're not very good, because they all think they're being merciful." He turned his slate over in his hands. "You think I'm a monster."

"I think you're a clerk."

"Yes," said Thorne. "That's worse, isn't it?" Something moved at the corner of his mouth that on another man might have been a smile. "But I'll tell you something about clerks, Assessor, since you are one too. A clerk has exactly one power, and it's the power to say *this is not what it says it is*. Nobody can make a clerk certify a false instrument. Not a Speaker, not an admiral, not the Concordance itself. If the Houses' instrument is false, I will not certify it. I will say so, in writing, under my seal, and the Bureau will stand behind me, and the conveyance will be void." He said it in exactly the same tone as everything else. "That is the only power I have. I use it without fear or favour."

*Without fear or favour.* I'd heard the phrase before. Every Assessor takes an oath in it. I'd sworn it at twenty-one in the Hall, in front of the Ash, with Lucan Marsh's hand on my shoulder.

"The signing's set for the Burning," I said.

"Your Speaker felt the symbolism was appropriate. The Burning freed your people from the Charter's Book; the Sale frees your Houses from the burden of the Account." Thorne's crystal eye moved — to the gate, to the wall, back to me. "I begin the founding page in three days. I proceed in order, Assessor. I've read the Account's structure and its current entries. Next I read its origin. Its first entry. The page on which the whole instrument rests." He paused. "If you have something to tell me about that page, now would be a good time."

I sat on the bench beside the fountain with my hand flat on my chest, on Ottilie's papers. *That is how I found the other thing. You know what I mean.*

I didn't know what she meant. Not all of it. I knew *composed*. I knew *silent*. I knew *ask the rings*. I didn't know what the rings would say, and I didn't know who'd killed her for asking, and I had a list in my shirt with my daughter's name on it eleven lines from the bottom.

"No, Commander," I said. "Nothing yet."

"Then I'll see you at fourteen," said Thorne, and turned his slate back on.

* * *

They tried to kill me that evening in the spoke.

The spokes are the lifts that run from the Crown in toward the Hulk's spine, eight of them, like the spokes of a wheel. You get in at the rim, where the spin gives you nearly a full gravity, and the car runs inward along the spoke toward the hub, and as it goes you get lighter and lighter, until by the time you reach the spine you're floating. It takes about four minutes. Most people stand at the rail and read their handsets. I'd ridden the spokes perhaps eight thousand times in twenty-nine years. I knew the feel of every one: the shudder at the half-way point on Spoke Three, the grinding sound in Spoke Six. I was in Spoke Five. It was seven in the evening. There was nobody else in the car.

It stopped between levels at about the forty per cent mark. Half a gravity. The lights flickered and held.

I knew. I'd like to say I'd been expecting it — after the boots, after Wren's call, after Mags Tiller's *you're the second* — but I hadn't, not like this, not in a lift I'd taken eight thousand times. What I knew was the count. The car had been running for one minute fifty-one seconds. It had never in my life stopped at one minute fifty-one, and the lights should have gone to emergency red if the stop was a fault, and they hadn't, and that meant somebody had stopped it on purpose from the maintenance panel, and that meant they were coming in.

They came in through the roof.

There's a maintenance hatch in the ceiling of every spoke car. It came open with a bang and a man dropped through it — not fast; at half a gravity nothing's fast; he came down through the hatch like a diver into a pool, feet first, slow and deliberate, with one hand on the hatch rim and the other holding a knife the length of my forearm. He wore a plain grey coat. He had a soft round face and very pale eyes, and he was smiling a little, the way some men smile at work.

I had my sidearm. I had it out of its holster, even. But in half a gravity, in a car two metres square, with a man coming down on you from above, a sidearm is very nearly useless; you can't get your arm up, and the recoil will throw you back against the wall, and if you miss, the round will go through the car's skin and into the spoke shaft and you'll be lucky if all it does is breach the car. He knew that. That was why he was smiling. He landed on me with his knees and his weight and drove me down against the floor of the car, and the sidearm went skittering away across the deck in long slow bounces, and the knife came down.

I caught his wrist. That was all. I caught it with both hands and held it and the point of the knife hung a hand's breadth above my left eye and quivered there, and he leaned on it, and I held it, and neither of us said anything at all.

I'm not a fighter. I want you to understand that. I'm an Assessor. I've struck three hundred and nine people to the ice and I've never in my life struck one with my hand; I have proctors for that. This man was a fighter. I could feel it in his wrist — the patience of it, the way he wasn't straining, just leaning, letting gravity do the work, letting me tire. In a full gravity he'd have had me in ten seconds. In half a gravity it was going to take a minute.

I counted. I don't know why. It's what I do. I lay on the floor of Spoke Five with a knife over my eye and counted his breathing, in, out, in, out, slow and even, a man at his ease; and on the twenty-third breath I noticed that every time he breathed in, his weight came up off me a fraction, a few hundred grams, the way it does in low gravity when your chest lifts.

On the twenty-fourth breath, as it came up, I let go of his wrist with my right hand and hit him in the throat.

Not hard. I didn't have the leverage for hard. But the knife came down when I let go, and I turned my head, and it went into the deck beside my ear, deep, and stuck there; and he was choking from the throat punch, and in half a gravity a man who's choking floats up off you a little whether he wants to or not. I got my knee between us. I pushed. He went up and back, slowly, turning, toward the far wall of the car, coughing, reaching for the knife; and I rolled the other way and found my sidearm under the bench where it had bounced, and turned onto my back with it in both hands, and he came off the far wall toward me with his arms out like a man diving into water, and I shot him in the chest.

The recoil threw me back against the bench. The round didn't go through the car's skin. It didn't go through him either. He hung in the middle of the car with his arms still out, looking surprised, and then very slowly he came down to the floor in the half gravity, as if he were lying down to sleep.

I sat on the deck of Spoke Five with my back against the bench and the sidearm in my lap and listened to the lift hum. After a while I found I was counting again. His breaths. They stopped at nine.

He had nothing in his pockets but a slip of film. No identity tag, no handset, no money. A slip of film, folded once, and on it in a neat clerk's hand, two lines.

*Fen, H. Lower Crown 7, 14.*
*Fen, W. Saltmarch, Upper Terrace 4.*

Under his collar, on a thin chain, he wore the small blue enamel shield of the Mercy Guard. They all wear it. It's issued at enlistment. Most of them never take it off.

I reported it. Of course I reported it. I sat in Spoke Five with a dead man until the proctors came, and I told them a man had attacked me with a knife in an attempted robbery and I had defended myself, which was true, and I didn't tell them about the slip of film, which was in my sock. They took the body away. The next morning the proctors' summary identified him as an unemployed ferry hand from the Saltmarch docks with a record of petty theft. There was no mention of the enamel shield. When I asked the proctor-captain about it, very politely, he looked at me for a moment with his face perfectly still and said there had been no shield, Assessor; I must be mistaken; it had been a very distressing experience.

* * *

The next morning I went to Clearances with four hundred and six marks.

I'd sold my second pair of boots, and my good coat, and a set of Charter-era brass instruments I'd bought in a Saltmarch junk shop at twenty and kept on my shelf for nineteen years because I liked the way they caught the light. I'd emptied my savings. I'd borrowed eighty marks from Bastian, who'd lent it to me at once without asking why, and who'd made me tea afterward and asked if I was all right, because I looked terrible. It came to four hundred and six exactly. I had it on a payment chip in my hand. I'd counted it eleven times.

The Clearances counter is in the forward Office, near the Hall, a long brass rail with grilles and clerks behind them, where people come up from Tamar on the ferry with their last payments and stand in line and hand them through and are made Clear. It's the happiest place in the Hulk. People cry there. I'd seen a woman of seventy dance there once, by herself, at the end of the line, with her arms out in the air.

There was no line. The grilles were closed, all but one. Behind the open one was a young clerk I didn't know, reading a slate.

"Clearance," I said. "Portion of Fen, W. Saltmarch Upper Terrace Four. Final payment." I put the chip on the counter.

He didn't take it. He looked at it, and at my pin, and then up at me, and his face did something careful.

"I'm sorry, Assessor," he said. "All Clearances are suspended."

"Since when?"

"Pending the Speaker's review." He touched his slate and turned it round so I could see the order on it, and the seal at the bottom, and the name above the seal. *By order of the Chief Assessor.* "No portion may be Cleared until the review is complete. Payments may be lodged against the portion but the portion remains open."

"When was this issued?"

He checked. "Six weeks ago, Assessor."

Six weeks ago. Before Ottilie died. Before any of it. Six weeks ago someone had sat down in the Speaker's rooms and worked out the price of one point six million portions, and someone else had understood that every portion Cleared before the Burning was a portion that couldn't be sold; and so for six weeks nobody on Tamar had been made free. I thought of all the people who'd stood in this line in those six weeks, with their last payments on chips in their hands, and been told *pending review*, and gone home. I thought of the woman dancing. I thought of Wren's voice going up at the end, the way it did when she was worried. *Will it be done? By my birthday?*

"Lodge it," I said.

"Assessor?"

"The payment. Lodge it against the portion."

He looked at me with something like pity. "It won't Clear her, sir. Not until the review."

"I know," I said. "Lodge it anyway. I want it written down."

He lodged it. The Account took it — four hundred and six marks, against the portion of Fen, W., outstanding four hundred and six — and grew it into the lattice under the clerk's seal, and the portion stood at nil and remained open, and my daughter was exactly as owned as she'd been the day before. I watched him do it. Then I went out into the forward Office and along the passage to the Hall.

* * *

The Hall of the Account is the old cargo bay of the Hulk, in the spine, near the bow — a great iron cavern a hundred metres long with ribs like a whale's, where the *Merciful Increase* once carried the stores of four thousand colonists. It's where the Founders burned the Book. There's a disc of black iron set into the deck at the centre of the Hall, two metres across, scorched and pitted, and the Houses say it's the very plate the crucible stood on; and on the disc, under a column of glass, is the Ash Case.

I went and stood in front of it. People do, in the Hulk, at odd hours. Nobody looks at them.

It's a box the size of a big book. Iron, black and blistered, its cover melted and run and set again, its hasp fused into a lump. There's a crack along one side. If you lean close you can see, through the crack, something dark and glassy underneath, like burnt sugar. That's the lattice, they say. That was the Book. Every debt the Charter ever wrote on us, grown into the crystal and burned to slag in the Founders' fire before all the people, three hundred and eighteen years ago, on the first Burning Day. *They burned the Book. And the ash was our freedom.*

They were hanging the banners for the Burning. Long strips of grey silk, ash-coloured, let down from the ribs of the Hall on cords, so that by the day itself the whole cavern would be hung with them like a forest of smoke. Ballast workmen on long poles, floating in the zero gravity, guiding the silk into place. One of them was singing, under his breath, the way ice miners sing on a long shift, and I caught a line of it as I stood there. Not the Houses' version. The other one.

*— heave and heave and we'll be free,
when the ash is paid, says he.*

Eight days.

I stood in front of the Ash Case and counted them. Eight days to the Burning. Seven to Wren's birthday. Two to the founding page. Three hundred and nine strikes. One man in a lift, nine breaths. Four hundred and six marks lodged against a portion that wouldn't close. Eleven lines below my daughter's name.

And at the bottom of it all, where I couldn't see it yet, the figure I hadn't reached. The one that every entry above it was adding up to, line by line, whether I liked it or not.
