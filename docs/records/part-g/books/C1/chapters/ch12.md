# Chapter 12: The Client

*The Salvage · CR 1317*

Every diver in the Gutter knows the vents.

Ringfall was built in the Houses' time for ferries, and the Houses built everything large: the dock's air ducts are a metre square, lined with steel, and they run behind every wall in the place like a second set of corridors. Children learn them first. There's no Gutter child over the age of eight who can't get from the concourse to the old dockmaster's office without once setting foot in a passage, and no Gutter adult who'll admit to remembering how. Yara remembers. She took Pim through them when he was six, the summer their mother died, because it was the only place in Ringfall he'd stop crying.

She goes in behind the noodle cart at the eleventh hour, in the dead time between shifts, through a grille she unscrews with a coin. Then it's twenty minutes on her elbows and knees in the dark, in the dust of three hundred years and the faint warm breath of the air pumps, past the backs of a hundred rooms, until she finds the one she wants and lies down flat over its grille and goes still.

The Company has taken the old dockmaster's suite for its offices. There's a carpet down below her, new, and a long table, and two chairs pulled close at one end of it with a pot of something on a warmer between them. Aurel Kade is in one chair. Sabeth Orrin is in the other.

"— the vault door," Kade is saying. "You're sure."

"Tull marked it. A yellow cross and the number five. The Warden's crew found the mark this morning, after the window closed." Sabeth pours. She has a careful way of doing it, Yara notices, both hands on the pot. "They went in the long way, through the Hall and the aft service passage. Clear all the way, apparently. Gorse's people didn't know it was there."

"And next pass she'll go through."

"Next pass she'll try. It's a Charter vault door. A metre of iron. She'll need two windows to cut it, maybe three, and she hasn't got three." Sabeth sets down the pot. "But she'll try. She's the kind that tries."

Kade drinks. In the light from the warmer, his face is calm and attentive and slightly tired, a man going over a schedule. "And when she's through."

"Whatever's in there comes to us. Unopened. Sealed if it's sealed." Sabeth's voice is just as calm. "We take it aboard the *Remedy* and you burn it in the incinerator, in front of me, and I go home."

"Good." Kade sets his cup down. "I want to be very clear about this, Sabeth, because the Chancery's people will ask me later and I want to be able to say I was. I don't know what's in that vault. I don't know if anything is. The family that sold the instrument to the estate always believed — it was a sort of family legend, I gather — that on the Night of the Hulk, an Assessor named Fen stole something from the Office that would have destroyed the Account if it had ever come out. Proof of some kind. Something sealed." He spreads his hands. "Probably it's nothing. Probably it's a ledger of somebody's gambling debts. But the Clerk is a stickler, and the Accords are very clear: a certified contrary record defeats the writ. Not reduces it. *Defeats* it. Four hundred thousand satisfactions, and the Company's whole investment, gone on one piece of sealed paper." He looks at her. "So if there's any chance at all, I want it in the incinerator before anyone else sees it. Fifty thousand marks is a very cheap price for peace of mind."

"And if she opens it first?"

"Then we'll have to discuss what she's seen." He says it pleasantly. "She has a brother on the medical schedule, I understand."

"Yes."

"Then I don't imagine we'll have to discuss it for long."

Yara lies over the grille and doesn't breathe.

"There's Saar too," says Sabeth after a moment. "In case she balks. We bought his book from Gorse on the second day. He knows what we want."

"Does he know we own him?"

"He knows." Sabeth sips her tea. "He took five thousand on account and asked for his sister's family to be moved down the schedule. I told him I'd see what I could do."

"Will you?"

"I'll see," says Sabeth Orrin. And then, after a pause, in a slightly different voice: "She saw me. On the dock, yesterday. Coming off the shuttle with you."

Kade laughs, softly. It's the first time Yara has heard him laugh. It's a nice laugh; it sounds like someone you'd want to have a drink with. "Good," he says. "Let her. People work harder when they know who's paying."

* * *

Mitya's can is at the far end of the Gutter, in the oldest row, where the cans were bolted on before anyone thought about spacing them, so that you have to go sideways along the hull between them and the hatches open almost onto each other. Yara hasn't been inside it for three years. She knocks with the side of her fist, hard, three times, and doesn't stop knocking until he opens it.

He's in his inner suit, with his hair wet, as if he's just washed. He looks at her face and doesn't say anything. He stands back and lets her in.

It's exactly as she remembers. That's the worst thing. The same bunk with the same blanket, grey and red, that she bought him at the Saltmarch market the first winter. The same scratch on the galley shelf where she dropped a cutter tip. The same pictures pinned on the wall above the bunk: his grandmother's flat with its window, a faded House crest he found in the ring and swore was his family's, a photograph of the two of them on the Ringfall dock six years ago with their helmets under their arms, laughing at something the photographer said. On the inside of his hatch there are his dive marks, in fives. Four hundred and twelve. Below them, in a different hand, smaller, there are hers — the dives they did together, before. She'd forgotten she ever scratched them there.

"How much?" she says.

He doesn't pretend not to understand. He sits down on the bunk with his hands between his knees.

"Five thousand," he says. "On account."

"For what?"

"To tell her where we'd got to. After each dive." He looks at the floor. "And to make sure that when we found it — if we found it — it came out sealed and went to her. Not to you. Not to anyone else."

"When?"

"The day after she came to you. She found me in the bar." He laughs, a little, without any sound in it. "I thought I was being clever. I thought, a third from you, and five from her, and I'd never have to dive again. Pay off Gorse. Get out." He looks up. "Then the next day Gorse called me in and told me he'd sold my book."

"To the Company."

"Nine hundred marks. All of it, everything I owe him, everything I ever owed him. Sold it to the Company for nine hundred marks, because the Company asked, and the Company's paying everybody this month." His hands are shaking. He presses them together. "So it doesn't matter what I'm paid, Yara. They own me. Whether I take their money or not. They can call my book any day they like and I go on the schedule, lot one, first shuttle. They didn't have to pay me at all. Sabeth just thought it was polite."

"And your sister."

"Petra's got three kids. All on the schedule. Lot nine. I asked her to move them down. She said she'd see." He closes his eyes. "I know what that means. I knew it when she said it. I asked anyway."

Yara stands in the middle of his can, in front of the pictures, and the anger is so big in her that she can't find the edges of it.

"You'd have let them burn it," she says. "Whatever it was. If it could stop all this. If it could stop *them*. You'd have handed it to her and watched her take it to that black ship and burn it, and taken your five thousand, and—"

"Yes."

"And Pim—"

"Pim's on their medical schedule, Yara. Pim's the one they'll *keep*." He's on his feet now. "Do you think I don't know that? Do you think I haven't lain here every night since she came to that bar and thought about it? The Company's got your brother's name on a list for the medbay on that ship, and you're going to go into the Hulk and come out with something that might, *might*, if the Clerk likes the look of it, stop the whole thing — or might be somebody's gambling ledger — and if you hand it to anyone but her, they'll take him off the medical schedule and put him on lot one with me. You know they will. Kade said it to her face in front of you, didn't he? *She has a brother.* I can see it on you. He said it."

She hits him.

She's never hit anyone outside a shell. She doesn't do it well. Her fist catches him on the cheekbone, more of a shove than a blow, and his head goes back and he takes a step and catches himself on the galley shelf, and the photograph of the two of them on the dock comes loose from its pin and drifts down in the can's light gravity and lands face down on the deck between them.

He doesn't hit back. He doesn't do anything. He stands with one hand on the shelf and the other on his face, and looks at her, and his eyes are wet.

"I'd have told you," he says.

"When?"

"When it didn't matter any more."

She looks at him. At the long Saltmarch face, the stubble, the eyes that are always laughing at something just behind you and aren't now. At the four hundred and twelve marks on the hatch and her own small ones underneath. At the photograph on the floor.

She doesn't pick it up. She goes out and leaves the hatch open behind her, and she hears him, as she goes sideways along the hull between the cans, making a sound she's never heard him make, like a man trying very hard not to.

* * *

Mireille Dunn finds her at the noodle cart an hour later.

Yara doesn't see her coming. She's sitting on the cart's one stool with a bowl she isn't eating, looking at the concourse screen, which is showing the *Lawful Remedy* again, and the first satisfaction lists, scrolling, name after name after name in neat Charter letters. Lot one: defaulters of registration. Lot two: the Gutter's eastern rows. Lot three. The noodle-cart man has stopped serving. He's watching the screen too. He's on lot six.

"You're Tull."

The Advocate is standing at her elbow in her too-big coat, with a satchel of slates over one shoulder. Up close she's smaller than she looked in the fish-market room, and older, and her eyes are very bright and very tired.

"Who's asking?"

"Mother Quell sent me. She said you'd found the vault." Dunn sits down on the edge of the cart's counter, uninvited, and the noodle-cart man doesn't object. "I'm not going to waste your time. I know what Orrin's paying you. I know who she is; I've known since the day the *Remedy* docked. I can't pay you anything like it. I can't pay you anything at all. The Assembly's treasury wouldn't buy you a new helmet."

"Then what do you want?"

"I want you to bring it to me." Dunn says it plainly. "Whatever's in that vault. If it's sealed — and everything the Office of the Account ever kept was sealed, it was what they did — bring it to me, not to her. I'll put it in front of the Clerk. That's all I can promise you. That he'll see it, and read it, and rule on it, under the Accords, in front of everyone."

"And if it's nothing?"

"Then it's nothing, and we've lost, and we'd have lost anyway." Dunn looks at the screen. "Rhee's a hard man. He's not a bad one. He'll do what the law says. And the law says a certified contrary record defeats the writ — not part of it, all of it. If what Fen took that night is sealed, and prior to the Account or superior to it, and says the Account is false—"

"Then nobody's taken."

"Then nobody's taken. Not one. Not the noodle man. Not your brother." Dunn turns back to her. "Quell told me about your box. The brass one from the annex."

Yara says nothing.

"It's an Office seal-reader. They're very rare now; most of them were melted for brass in the Dimming. If you find anything sealed in that vault, put the seal to the box before you hand it to anyone. It'll tell you if it's true. A Charter seal, a Council seal, anything grown under a key." Dunn stands. "That way you'll know what you've got before you decide who to give it to. I'd want to know, in your place."

She slings her satchel back over her shoulder. On the screen, the lists go on scrolling.

"Why do you care?" says Yara. "You're an advocate. You've got a flat in Saltmarch. Your line won't be on lot anything."

Mireille Dunn looks at her for a moment with her bright tired eyes. Then she pulls back the cuff of her too-big coat, and turns her left wrist over, and shows it. On the inside of it, faded nearly to nothing in the thin old skin, there's a mark. Not ash. Ink. A number, in the blue-black of very old tattoos, in the plain block letters of a style nobody has used for centuries: a letter, a figure, a dash, four more figures.

"My grandmother's grandmother had it done," says Dunn. "On Burning Day. It's a family thing. We used to be very proud of it. The number of our first one, the one they carried in the Crossing. It was in the Book." She lets the cuff fall. "Every line's on lot something, Miss Tull. Some of us just know where we're filed."

* * *

The Hulk comes down for perigee at the twentieth hour, and the whole Gutter goes to watch.

It's not usual. Most passes, nobody bothers. But there's been talk on the concourse all day — the Assembly engineers have been watching the Crown, what's left of it, and they think this is the pass it goes — and by the nineteenth hour the long observation gallery on Ringfall's outer face is packed shoulder to shoulder with divers and their families, pressed up against the thick old windows, looking down.

Yara and Pim get a place near the end. Pim's wrapped in two blankets and coughing. He insisted.

Below them, Tamar turns: white and grey and cracked like an old plate, with the dark line of the shelf over Saltmarch and the scattered lights of the ice towns. Beyond it, filling half the sky, is Absalom. You forget how big it is until you look. Rust and ochre and cream, banded, streaked, with storms in it the size of worlds, slowly turning; and against the face of it, very small, coming down out of the dark on its long ellipse toward the top of the cloud deck, the Hulk.

From here she's a dark sliver. Then, as she meets the air, a spark.

The spark grows. It's red at first, then orange, then white, a tiny fierce point of light sliding across the banded face of the giant with a tail behind it that lengthens and lengthens until it's a line of fire a hundred kilometres long. The gallery goes quiet. You can hear the air pumps. You can hear someone's child asking a question and being shushed.

Then the Crown goes.

It's not dramatic. That's what Yara will remember. From this far away it isn't a tearing or an explosion; it's just that the spark becomes two sparks. The bigger one keeps going along its line. The smaller one — a long thin arc of light, curved like a fingernail paring — separates from it very slowly, and drifts a little apart, and begins to burn on its own. It gets brighter. It breaks into three. The three break into more. And then there's a spray of tiny lights across the face of Absalom, like sparks thrown up from a fire when you drop a log on it, each one trailing its own small thread of flame, falling, falling, down toward the clouds, and going out one by one.

The gallery makes a sound. Not words. The sound the fish-market room made, when the Factor said *forty marks*.

"Constance," says Pim softly, beside her.

"What?"

"That was the Constance section. The big piece. I read it on the engineers' bulletin." He's watching the last sparks fall. "Patience and Temperance went last month. That was Constance and Prudence and the gardens. There's not much ring left now."

Yara looks down at the long bright line of the Hulk climbing away from perigee, smaller now, lighter, trailing its last threads of fire.

On the gallery's screen, the engineers' bulletin updates. *Mass loss significant. Structural integrity of spine uncertain. Passes remaining revised: four (estimated). Three (possible).*

"Four," says Pim.

"Four."

"Three possible."

"I can read, Pim."

He coughs. When he's done, he says, without looking at her, "Are you going to give it to them? If you find it?"

She doesn't answer.

"You should," says Pim. "I want you to know that. If it's that or me. You should." He pulls the blankets tighter. "I've thought about it. I'm on the medical list. They'll fix my lungs. And then I'll go wherever they send me and work, and maybe it'll be all right. People come back from things. Mam used to say so." He looks at her then. His face is grey from the cough and the cold of the gallery, and his eyes are very steady, and for a moment he looks exactly like their mother. "Don't not do it because of me. That's all. Whatever you decide. Not because of me."

* * *

Mitya is waiting at her hatch when they get back.

He's sitting on the step outside it, in his diver's jacket, with his helmet in his lap, the way Sabeth Orrin sat on the step inside it the first night. There's a bruise on his cheekbone where she hit him. He stands up when he sees them and doesn't come any closer.

Pim looks at him, and at Yara, and goes past them both into the can without a word and pulls the hatch half shut behind him.

"Four passes," says Mitya.

"I saw."

"Three, maybe." He turns his helmet over in his hands. "You can't do the door alone. You know you can't. It's a metre of Charter iron and you've got two windows if you're lucky. You need someone on the cutter while you brace. You need someone on your line." He looks up. "Let me cut."

"So you can hand it to her."

"So you can have it." He says it very quietly. "Whatever's in there. You get it. You decide. I won't touch it. I won't tell her anything you don't want me to tell her — I'll tell her whatever you say. I'll tell her it was empty. I'll tell her we didn't get through." His voice breaks, a little, and he steadies it. "They own my book, Yara. They can put me on lot one whenever they like. I can't do anything about that. But I can cut a door. Let me do the one thing I'm good for."

She looks at him for a long time on the step in the dim light of the Gutter bulbs. At the bruise she put on his face. At the helmet in his hands, scraped bright along one side from the slosh in the CONSTANCE corridor, the day she found the seal-reader. A lifetime ago. Ten days.

She thinks of Kade's pleasant voice through the grille. *People work harder when they know who's paying.* She thinks of Sabeth's careful hands on the pot. She thinks of Mireille Dunn's thin wrist, and the old blue number on it, and *some of us just know where we're filed*. She thinks of Pim on the gallery, wrapped in two blankets, looking like their mother. *Not because of me.*

She doesn't trust Mitya. She doesn't trust anyone. She's been diving the Hulk for twelve years and she's never once trusted the water, either, and she's still alive.

"Front of the window," she says. "Like always."

He closes his eyes. "Like always."

"You cut. I brace. Whatever's in there, I take it. You don't touch it. You don't look at it. And when we come up, you go straight to your can and you stay there and you don't go near the bar or the dockmaster's office or anywhere else until I tell you." She waits until he opens his eyes and looks at her. "And if you try anything — anything, Mitya — I'll cut your line in the Hall and leave you for Fen."

Something moves at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. Something older.

"Fen's in there somewhere," he says softly, "counting."

"Then he can count you."

She goes in and shuts the hatch. Through it, after a long moment, she hears him go — slowly, sideways along the hull between the cans, back toward the oldest row. And from somewhere far off on the other side of the Gutter, faint through the plating, the Ash bell rings the hour on the old band, seven notes falling; and on the galley shelf, in the dark, the brass box clicks once in answer, and its dull glass eye, for the space of a breath, glows amber.

Four passes.
