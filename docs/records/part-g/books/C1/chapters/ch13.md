# Chapter 13: The Master's Authority

*The Crossing · CR 611*

Three days before the Fork, the captain sent for her at midnight.

It was not the steward who brought the message. It was Lindqvist himself, on the comm in her cabin, in a voice she had never heard him use: slow, careful, with the edges of the words worn soft. *Purser. Would you come to the day room. If it's not too late.* She dressed and went up the spoke to the forward hub and along the short passage past the dark Lantern, where the night watch sat in their bowl of instruments with the red coal of Pell on their faces, and knocked at the door with the brass plate on it, and he said *come*.

He was sitting at his grandfather's table with the decanter in front of him and a glass in his hand, and the decanter was two-thirds empty.

"Sit down, Annick."

He had never used her name before. She sat.

"I've been trying to think when I last called a purser by their first name," said Harrow Lindqvist. "I think it was Aurelio. I think it was the night we left Callis on the third voyage, and he'd found a stowaway in the hold, a girl of about twelve, and he came up here and asked me what I wanted him to write, and I said *write whatever you want, Aurelio, I don't care*, and he wrote her into the Book as crew. Cabin girl. Paid her a wage out of his own pocket for two years." He drank. "I'd forgotten that. I remembered it tonight."

"What happened to her?"

"She jumped ship at Gethen. Married a quarryman." He looked at the glass. "I watched them put that woman out of the lock, you know. Osei. I was on the Lantern. I could have stopped it. The Factor can space a mutineer under Seventeen but the Master can stay any execution aboard his ship for seven days, pending review. Article Eighteen. You'll know it."

"I know it."

"I didn't. Stay it. I sat in my chair and watched it on the screen." He set the glass down very precisely in the ring it had left on the wood. "I haven't slept since. I lie there and I see her standing in that lock with her hands tied, talking to them. I couldn't hear what she said. The screen had no sound. I keep wondering what she said."

Annick told him.

He listened without moving. When she had finished he sat looking at the table, his thumb moving slowly round the rim of the glass.

"*Don't let them make you choose*," he said at last. "Well. She was right about that. That's all they've got." He looked up. "I'm not taking them to Shale."

She had known it was coming. She found she was holding the edge of the table.

"I'm sixty," said Lindqvist. "I've carried bonded for the Charter for thirty-four years. I told you that, in this room, the day you were made purser. I told you I'd never asked where they ended up and I wasn't going to pretend to be the sort of man who would." He laughed, a little, without much in it. "It turns out I am, after all. Too late to be any use. But there it is." He leaned forward. "I want to take her to Absalom. I'll land them on the ice with what's in the holds and every tool aboard and the ship's stores and the ship herself, if they want her for a station, and I'll give them every one of my crew who'll stay. The rest I'll take back up the Lane in the shuttles, as far as the shuttles go. And when the Receivers' court finds me, a year from now or five, I'll answer for it."

"The crew won't be paid."

"No. That's the price. I'll answer for that too."

"Quarry will stop you."

"Yes." He looked at her steadily, and she saw that under the whisky he was entirely clear. "That's why I need you."

He told her. It was simple, and it was exactly what she had been afraid of. When Quarry had recorded the Receivers' order in the Book's annex at Pell, under the Factor's authority over Company property, he had also recorded a restriction on the Master: any departure from the directed course required the Factor's countersign. The Lantern's locks had taken it. Lindqvist could not make the jump from the Fork to the Absalom branch without Quarry's code alongside his own. But a Master's authority was whatever the Book recorded. If the Purser sealed a new entry — Harrow Lindqvist, Master, without restriction — the locks would drop the countersign and take his code alone.

"Forty seconds," he said. "You told the officers that once, at a briefing, years ago. Forty seconds for the locks to re-key. That's all I need. At the Fork, when we come out of fold, the coil has to cool for six hours before it can jump again, but the navigational solution for the next jump can be laid in during the cool. I lay in Absalom. I don't tell anyone. At the end of the six hours, when the Factor comes down here with his order for you to seal, you seal me instead. Forty seconds later I jump. And by the time Quarry understands what's happened we're two days down the Absalom branch and there's nothing he can do but sit in his cabin and write a complaint."

"His proctors control the Lantern deck."

"His proctors control the Lantern's *doors*. They don't fly her." Lindqvist's mouth moved. "I've been flying ships for thirty-four years, Annick. I can make a jump with a proctor's carbine at my neck. I've done it before. Pirates, off Gethen, when I was second officer."

"And when he arrests you? Under Seventeen. A Master departing from the owners' orders—"

"Then he arrests me. Two days down the Absalom branch. And he can space me if he likes, and Nadia can fly them the rest of the way." He said the First Officer's name easily, with affection. "She's better than I ever was. She'll get them there."

Annick looked at him across the table. At the grey stubble and the worn cuffs and the wet clear eyes. At the hands round the glass, which were very steady.

"You'd have me seal it," she said, "and not tell anyone."

"I'd have you seal it and not tell anyone." He smiled for the first time. "Aurelio would have done it. You know he would. That's why he's dead." He said it quite simply, as if it were a fact about the weather. "I don't know who did it, Annick. I don't know how. But I've known since the morning you found him. I've sat in this room every night since and looked at Edwin Quarry across this table and wondered." He picked up the glass again. "Think about it. That's all I ask. You've got three days."

* * *

She did not go back to her cabin. She went to the Lantern, because she could not think of anywhere else to go, and found Nadia Szell on the watch.

The First Officer was alone in the command ring. The night watch had been cut to two since Callis — Quarry's economy — and the other officer, young Brandt, was asleep at the navigation station with his head on his arms. Szell sat in the captain's fold seat with her boots up on the rail and the whole sky above her in the dome: the thin stars of the Long Lane, the dull red coal of Pell. She didn't turn round.

"Purser," she said. "You've been with the captain."

"Yes."

"He's been drinking."

"Yes."

"He wants to run for Absalom and hand himself in to the Receivers' court in a year with his cap in his hand." Szell turned her head, then, and looked at Annick with her dark considering eyes. "And he wants you to seal him free of the Factor so he can do it. Forty seconds. Am I close?"

Annick said nothing.

"He told me," said Szell. "Last week. He tells me everything; he's told me everything for nine years. He's a good man. I told you once that good men are expensive." She swung her boots down off the rail. "Sit down. There's something I want to tell you, and I want to say it once and not have it go any further than this dome."

Annick sat in the navigator's second chair, beside the sleeping Brandt. Szell leaned toward her across the command ring.

"I'm Ruck's pilot," she said.

It was very quiet in the Lantern. The instruments ticked. Somewhere in the hull the coil, cooling, gave its slow far-off metallic creak.

"You've known there was someone," said Szell. "He'll have told you that. *A friend on the bridge.* He likes that phrase; he thinks it's discreet. It's me." She waited a moment for Annick to say something, and when Annick did not, she went on, low and fast and absolutely clear. "At the Fork, when we come out of fold, the Factor comes down to the strongroom with his order. Ruck starts the rising in the Hold at the same moment. The proctors go to the Hold, because that's where the noise is. I take the Lantern with Orme and four of the others — they're mine, they've been mine since Callis. You seal me as Master. Forty seconds, the locks re-key, and the ship's mine. Ruck's people hold the spine. I hold the bridge. The Factor's locked in your strongroom with his order in his hand. We cool, and we jump for Absalom, and we don't stop."

"And the captain?"

"The captain sits in his day room and drinks, and when it's over I'll bring him a glass." Szell's face did not change. "He wants to save them by giving himself up to a court that will hang him. I want to save them by not."

"Why you?"

"Because I can fly her," said Szell. "And because Absalom has a patent waiting for whoever's holding the Book when she arrives." She said it without any embarrassment at all. "You know what's in that Book better than I do, Purser. Not just debts. The Charter's patent for the Absalom system — the title to every world in it, granted to the Company at Callis, recorded in the Book at departure, to be vested in the colony at Landfall. Quarry wants to sell it to Shale with the debts. Lindqvist wants to drop it on the ice and walk away. I want to keep it. Someone will hold that world. Better someone who can keep four thousand people alive on a moon made of ice than a dead company or a mine." She sat back. "That's all. I've told you more than I've told Ruck. I thought you'd rather have it straight."

Annick looked at her. In the dim red light of Pell coming through the dome, the First Officer's face was calm and tired and extraordinarily intelligent, and there was nothing in it Annick could read at all.

*Watch the First Officer, Purser.*

"Benn," said Annick.

Szell's eyebrows moved, very slightly. "What about him?"

"He refused to seal the assignment. He sat in the day room for five hours and said no. And three days later he was dead."

"Of his lungs."

"Of his lungs," Annick agreed.

Nadia Szell looked at her for a long moment. Then she said, quite gently, "I liked Benn. He taught me to play cards, my first voyage. He cheated." She stood up, and stretched, and looked up at the stars in the dome. "Three days, Purser. You'll have to choose one of us. The captain, or me, or the Factor. You can't seal us all." She glanced down. "Choose the one who can fly."

* * *

She went down to the strongroom in the second watch with Haddad and a length of cable.

They had been waiting for this for two weeks. The concentrator's service log could be read only by an engineer's reader, which Haddad could not ask for; but every piece of ship's equipment reported, eventually, to the Book's annex, the great slow record of the ship's own life that grew in the lattice alongside the voyage. Annick had found the protocol in the clerks' manual: a maintenance record could be pulled into the annex directly from the device, if you had a purser's key and the right cable. Haddad had found the cable in a locker in the infirmary, behind a box of splints, where somebody had left it on the third voyage.

He held the concentrator while she opened the Book. She set the cable's end into a socket on the inside of the case that she had never used before, beside the hasp, and the other end into the concentrator's casing, and put her thumb to the bead. The page-glass flickered. Then it filled with columns she did not recognise — dates, codes, figures — and at the top, in Charter letters, *MAINTENANCE: RESPIRATORY UNIT 14-B. ASSIGNED: BENN, A.*

They read it together, in silence, with their heads nearly touching over the glass.

The casing had been opened four times in eleven months. Three were routine: filter changes, logged by the infirmary orderly, with his code. The fourth was two entries, close together, on the last night of Aurelio Benn's life.

*01:12 — Casing opened. Output valve: manual override engaged. Operator: —*

*04:40 — Casing opened. Output valve: manual override released. Operator: —*

The operator field was blank in both.

"Erased," said Haddad. His voice was very quiet. "It doesn't come blank. The casing won't open without a code. Somebody put one in and then went back afterward and scrubbed it."

"Who can scrub a maintenance code?"

He had already looked it up. He had looked it up two weeks ago, she understood, and been waiting to say it. "Three people," he said. "Officer-level maintenance access. The Master. The First Officer. The Chief Engineer."

"Varga."

"Ilse was on the coil the whole of that night. It's logged. Eleven hours. I checked." He did not look at her. "That leaves two."

They looked at the blank fields in the page-glass. Neither of them said anything.

"It isn't proof," said Haddad at last.

"No."

"A tribunal would say a Factor could get an officer's code. Bribe it, steal it. He's got twenty-four proctors and he's been through every cabin on this ship. Or that the log's corrupt. Or that the old man engaged the override himself, in his sleep, the way I said, and somebody else — the orderly — wiped the record afterward to cover a mistake." He pulled the cable gently from the casing. "It isn't proof, Annick. It's two lines with nothing in them."

"It's two lines with nothing in them," she agreed, "where there should be a name."

She closed the Book. Haddad wrapped the concentrator back in its towel, the way he always did, carefully, as if it were something alive.

"The captain loved him," he said at the door. "Thirty years. I've seen them together. If it was the captain, I don't understand anything at all."

She did not answer. He went. And she sat for a long time afterward in the dark strongroom with her hand on the Book's iron lid, and in her head, in the shadow ledger she had never written anywhere, she wrote two names side by side in a column by themselves and drew no line under either.

* * *

Tobiah Ruck was in the hollow among the cradles, alone, with his hand on Lise Saal's window.

"Szell," said Annick.

He didn't turn. "She told you."

"She told me. In the Lantern. Tonight." Annick caught the strut at the edge of the hollow and held it. Her breath smoked in the blue cold. "Your friend on the bridge is the First Officer. Who wants the patent for herself. Who'll be Master of this ship and every world at the end of the Lane the moment I seal her."

"Yes."

"And you knew."

"Of course I knew." He turned then. In the cradle lamps his broken face was gentle and tired. "Who else was it going to be? Lindqvist? He'd have handed us to the Receivers' court with an apology. Brandt? Brandt can't find his own boots. She's the only one aboard who can fly her through the Fork and down the branch with the proctors trying to stop her, Annick. She's the only one." He let go of the window. "Seal Szell. She can fly."

"Somebody with an officer's code killed Benn."

He was very still.

"I pulled the log," said Annick. "Haddad and I. The concentrator. Someone opened it at one in the morning and closed the valve and came back at twenty to five and opened it again, and erased their code both times, and only three people on this ship can do that. One of them was on the coil all night. That leaves the captain. And her."

Tobiah said nothing. A cradle pump sighed somewhere in the racks, and then another.

"Might be the captain," he said at last.

"Might be."

"Might be the Factor bought a code. Might be one of his proctors. Might be the old man's heart after all and the log's wrong." He spread his great hands. "You don't know. Haddad doesn't know. A log with two blank lines in it."

"Would it matter to you?" said Annick. "If it were her?"

The question hung in the blue air between them. Somewhere in the racks a cradle pump sighed.

"Yes," said Tobiah Ruck. "It would matter." He looked at her steadily. "And I'd still need her to fly."

She had known he would say it. She had known it before she came down. She found she was holding the strut so hard her hand hurt.

"And at Absalom," she said. "When she's Master. When she holds the patent. What does she get?"

"A ship to fly. That's all she wants. She told me."

"She told me she wants the world."

"Then she can have the world." His voice was very soft. "Let her have it, Annick. A moon made of ice and a gas giant. She's welcome. I want *them*." He put his hand flat on the cradle beside him — not Lise Saal's, another, a man's face through the frost — and then on the next, and the next, slowly, along the row, the way a man touches the heads of sleeping children going down a ward. "I want them off this ship and on the ice with nobody's number on their wrist. That's all I've wanted since Callis. If Nadia Szell wants to call herself Master of Absalom while we do it, she can call herself whatever she likes."

Annick did not answer. She could not think of anything to say that she had not already said, in this hollow, in this cold, a dozen times.

"I know what Quarry asked you," said Tobiah.

It was very quiet.

"In the brig. The night they took Dace." He did not look at her. He was looking at the frosted face under his hand. "I know what he asked. Everyone in the Wake knows what he asked. He's asked it on every voyage he's ever sailed; it's how he works." He paused. "I'm not going to ask what you said."

She could not breathe. Four in. Hold. Four out. She tried, and it would not come.

"I'm not going to ask," said Tobiah again. "I want you to know that. Not now. Not after. Marit said she wasn't angry, and I'm not either. We've three days, and I need you in that strongroom with your key, and I'm not going to ask you anything at all."

He turned away from her, back to the cradles. After a moment he began, very softly, to sing.

She went down through the struts toward the hatch. Halfway down she passed Dace, coming up, with a lamp and a tool roll, on her way to a cradle that needed tending; and Dace saw her and stopped on the strut below, and looked up at her, and for a moment Annick thought her sister would speak.

Dace did not speak. She looked at Annick for perhaps three seconds with her dark unreadable eyes. Then she turned her head away, deliberately, and went on up past her into the racks, and did not look back.

* * *

The ship's voice spoke at the change of the next watch.

*All hands. All hands. Coil temperature nominal. Fold to the Fork in thirty minutes. Secure for fold.*

Annick was in the strongroom. She had been there for six hours. She had not slept, and she had not eaten, and she had opened the Book eleven times and closed it again without writing anything, and she was sitting now with her back to the cold bulkhead and her knees drawn up and the key in her hand, turning it over and over.

Three of them. Three people who each wanted her to write one line.

The captain, drunk and clear and sixty years old, who wanted to save them by giving himself up to the court that would hang him. *Aurelio would have done it. That's why he's dead.*

The First Officer, who could fly, who wanted the world, who had liked Benn and played cards with him and whose code might be the one that had been erased from a blank line. *Choose the one who can fly.*

The Factor, who had bought her sister's life with Marit's, at a good price, and who would come down to this room at the Fork with his order and his soft hands and expect her to seal it. *Watch the First Officer, Purser.*

And Tobiah, who was not asking her anything at all. Who had known, and had sat with Lise Saal, and had said *I'm not going to ask what you said*, and meant it, and had turned away and begun to sing.

She looked at the brass plaque on the wall. Twenty-two articles in a cramped legal hand. She did not need to read them. *Article Fourteen. The Master's authority is that which the Book records.* Whatever she wrote, the ship would obey. That was the whole of it. Every other power aboard — the Factor's proctors, the captain's thirty-four years, the First Officer's officers, Tobiah's three hundred — came down, at the end, to one line in a block of crystal and the thumb of whoever held the key.

She had never in her life had a power like it. She had never wanted one.

*Fold in ten minutes. All hands secure.*

She got up. She strapped the Book into its cradle with the fold straps, the way the regulations required, and checked them twice. She strapped herself into the purser's fold seat beside it, on the wall under the plaque, and put the key back inside her collar on its cord, where she could feel it lying cold against her breastbone.

Through the bulkhead to her left, very faintly, she could hear the water of Tank Four shifting as the ship came about for the jump. A slow deep sound, like something turning over in the dark.

*Fold in one minute.*

She closed her eyes. She found she was counting — not her breath; the names in her head, the shadow ledger, from the top: every person she had entered dead in eleven years. Emeric Saal. Aurelio Benn. Marit Osei. She got to the end of them. She began again.

The coil spun up. She felt it before she heard it, a deep rising hum that came through the deck and the bulkhead and the seat into her spine, and then her teeth; and the lamps flickered; and the strongroom seemed to stretch, very slightly, in a direction there was no word for, the way it always did, the way she had felt it at every jump for eleven years and never got used to.

Then the *Merciful Increase* went into fold for the Fork, and for a long moment nothing at all was written anywhere.
