# Chapter 9: First Satisfaction

*The Salvage · CR 1317*

The Common Assembly of Tamar meets in a room that used to be a fish market.

Everyone knows it. The Assembly would rather they didn't. There's a carved stone over the door that says ASSEMBLY HALL in the old Charter letters, and benches in a horseshoe, and a dais with a long table and the Warden's chair, and a gallery up under the roof for the public; but the floor slopes to a drain in the middle, and there are hooks in the ceiling beams where the carcasses of ice-sharks used to hang, and on warm days, when the city's heat comes up from the refinery districts and the ice of the walls sweats, the whole room smells faintly of brine and gutting. Yara's mother used to say it was the most honest room on Tamar.

The gallery is full this morning. It's never full. Yara has come down from Ringfall on the early ferry because Pim wanted to come, and Pim wanted to come because he's read about the Assembly in the lending library and never seen it, and because, he said, standing in the can's hatch with his coat on and his face set, *somebody should watch*. So they're wedged into the back row of the gallery between a refinery woman and two old divers, and Pim is leaning over the rail with his slate in his hand, writing things down.

The Factor of the Paschal Recovery Company stands at the foot of the dais with his hands clasped behind him, and he's the most reasonable man Yara has ever heard.

He is perhaps forty-five. Tall, with a straight back and greying hair cut short, in a suit of dark grey cloth so plain it must have cost more than her shell. He has the bearing of a naval officer and the voice of a priest: low, warm, unhurried, a voice that seems to be taking you into its confidence. His name is Aurel Kade. He's been speaking for twenty minutes, and he hasn't once raised that voice, or used a word Yara doesn't understand, or said anything that isn't, as far as she can tell, perfectly true.

"No one wishes this," he says. "I want to be very clear about that, here, in your Assembly, before your Warden and your people. The Company takes no pleasure in it. I take no pleasure in it. I've read your histories on the voyage out. I know what the Account was. I know what it meant, here, for three hundred years." He looks up at the gallery, as if he can see each one of them. "But the obligation exists. It was written. It was sealed by the authority that governed this system. It was never discharged. It passed, by sale and inheritance and the ordinary operation of law, into the estate of a trading house on Vey that failed eleven years ago; and the Chancery of Vey, in liquidating that estate, offered it at auction, and the Company bought it, in good faith, at a fair price." He spreads his hands. "We didn't create this debt. We only hold it. And under the Accords of Settled Space, which govern every system in this region of the galaxy, including — since your Warden signed them forty years ago, when the Lane reopened — this one, a debt that is held must be honoured."

"Three hundred and eighty-eight years," says a voice from the benches.

It's a woman. Small, sixty, sharp-faced, with white hair scraped back and a coat too big for her, sitting at the end of the front bench with a stack of slates in her lap. Yara knows her from the bulletins: Mireille Dunn, Advocate of the Assembly, who's been the Assembly's lawyer for twenty years and has never, as far as anyone can remember, won anything.

"Three hundred and eighty-eight years," says Dunn again, standing. "That's how long your obligation has been sitting in a drawer, Factor. Nobody on Tamar has paid a mark on the Account since the Night of the Hulk. There's nobody alive who remembers it. There's nobody alive whose *grandmother* remembers it. And you come out of the Lane with a copy of a copy of a ledger nobody's seen since the Dimming and tell us the people of this system owe you their children."

"I tell you they owe what is written," says Kade gently. "With interest. As the instrument provides."

"The instrument was a fraud from the start. Everyone knows that. The Book was burned. Burned before all the people. Every debt the Charter ever wrote on us went into that fire, and the Houses dug up the ash and called it Liberty and sold it back to us for three hundred years. Ask anyone in this room."

"I don't doubt it," says Kade. "But your Book was the Charter's instrument, Advocate. The Account was the Houses'. The burning of one doesn't discharge the other — indeed, as I understand your own Church's teaching, the Account was raised *after* the Burning, precisely because the Book was gone." He inclines his head to her, as if she's made a fair point that he's sorry to have to answer. "I've no doubt the Houses were tyrants. Most founders are. But a tyrant's seal is still a seal. If the instrument was a fraud, show me. Show the Clerk."

Every head in the room turns to the end of the long table on the dais.

The Clerk-Notary of the Chancery of Vey has been sitting there all morning without a word. He's a narrow man of no particular age, thirty or fifty, with a long pale face and pale hair cut very short, in a high-collared robe of grey that falls straight to the floor, and on his chest, on a chain, a bead of dull crystal the size of a thumbnail. He has a slate in front of him and a stylus in his hand and he's been writing, steadily, through everything, without looking up. His name, the bulletins say, is Ansel Rhee. Without him, the bulletins say, the writ is only paper; the Company can register, and count, and post notices, but it cannot take a single person in satisfaction until the Clerk has certified it. The Chancery sends a Clerk with every writ, to see that it's executed as written. Not more. Not less.

He looks up now. He has pale eyes, almost colourless.

"The writ," he says, "stands upon an ancient instrument under seal." His voice is dry and very clear and has no warmth in it at all. "The Chancery has examined the seal and finds it to be what it purports to be: the seal of the issuing authority of the Account of Absalom. The Chancery makes no finding as to the justice of the instrument. The Chancery is not competent to make such a finding." He sets down the stylus. "An instrument under seal may be defeated only by a certified contrary record of equal or greater standing. That is: by another instrument, under seal, of prior date or superior authority, which discharges the obligations of the first or impeaches its validity. Absent such a record, the Clerk is bound to certify satisfaction of the writ as written." He looks at Dunn. "Have you such a record, Advocate?"

The fish-market room is very quiet.

"No," says Mireille Dunn.

"Then the writ stands." The Clerk picks up his stylus. "First satisfaction will commence at the eighth hour tomorrow. Forty thousand persons of the liable lines, of working age and sound health, in order of the Company's schedule, to be held at the Company's charge pending transport. Persons in default of registration will be taken first." He writes something. "The Clerk is obliged to the Warden for his cooperation."

Up on the dais, in his chair, Corin Asch shifts his heavy body and says nothing. He's been saying nothing all morning. Yara watches him not say it.

"Tell them the fee," says Dunn.

The Factor turns to her politely. "I'm sorry?"

"The fee. Tell the Assembly what the Company is paying the Wardenship for its cooperation. Per head." Dunn holds up one of her slates. "I have the schedule. I'd rather you read it."

There's a pause. Then Aurel Kade smiles, very slightly, and for the first time Yara sees something under the warmth of him — something flat and patient and cold, like the water at the bottom of the Hulk.

"Forty marks," he says. "Per satisfaction. For the costs of administration. It's quite standard."

The gallery makes a sound. Not words. Just a sound, the way the Hulk makes a sound when something deep in her gives way.

Beside Yara, Pim writes it down.

* * *

They register that afternoon, because there's no choice, because if they don't they'll be in default by morning and the defaulters go first.

The line on the Ringfall concourse is shorter now. Most of the Gutter registered in the first two days. There are perhaps forty people ahead of them, and the bailiffs at the table under the black canopy work fast, and nobody talks. When it's their turn, Yara holds out her left wrist, and a bailiff in a narrow visorless helmet takes it in a gloved hand — not roughly; they're never rough — and presses it to a plate on the machine, and something stings, and a light goes green.

The bailiff reads the screen. His voice comes out of a grille in the helmet, flat and pleasant. "Tull, Yara. Diver, Ringfall. Line of TULL, Gallows Reach twelve, portion of record. Liable." A pause. "Outstanding at last entry: one thousand one hundred and four marks. Accrued at the instrument rate: four million, two hundred and sixty thousand, eight hundred and twelve marks." Another pause. "Scheduled for satisfaction: lot fourteen. You'll be notified. Next."

She doesn't move. "Four million."

"Accrued at the instrument rate," says the bailiff. "Next."

"For eleven hundred marks."

"The figure's on your registration, ma'am. You can request a copy at the Company office. Next."

Pim holds out his wrist. He's very pale. The machine stings him and he flinches and coughs, and the bailiff waits for him to finish coughing, patiently, and then reads the screen.

"Tull, Pim. Minor, Ringfall. Line of TULL. Liable." A pause, a little longer than before. "Medical flag. Respiratory: rime-lung, chronic, treatable." The helmet tilts slightly, as if the person inside it were looking at Pim more closely. "Scheduled for satisfaction pending medical assessment. Medical satisfactions are processed aboard the *Lawful Remedy*. You'll be notified."

"What does that mean?" says Yara.

"It means he'll be assessed," says the bailiff. "The Company has medical facilities aboard. Treatable conditions are treated before transport." The pleasant voice doesn't change. "He's an asset, ma'am. The Company looks after its assets. Next."

They walk home along the concourse without talking. Past the writs on the walls, past the line, past the place where Tobin Rusk's wife stood making her sound. She's not there now. Someone has scratched a skull on the plating beside the nearest writ, small and neat, and under it *T.R.*

In the can, Pim sits on the bottom bunk and looks at the inside of his wrist, where the machine stung him. There's a tiny red mark, already fading.

"They'll fix my lungs," he says.

Yara doesn't answer.

"That's funny, isn't it? You've been trying for three years. Eight thousand for the passage. And they'll do it for free." He laughs, and it turns into a cough, and he coughs for a long time. When he's done he wipes his mouth and says, in a different voice, "Before they take me. So I'll last longer. So I'm worth more."

"They're not taking you."

"They're taking forty thousand people, Yara. In order of the Company's schedule. I wrote it down." He holds up his slate. "Somebody should."

She takes the slate out of his hand and puts it on the shelf and sits down beside him. They sit there. After a while, because she doesn't know what else to do, she reaches up to the galley shelf and takes down the brass box from the drowned annex and puts it in his lap.

"Quell says it's a seal-reader," she says.

He looks at it. Then he looks up at her, and she can see the thing in his face that's always there under the cough, under the fear, the thing that makes him read everything in the lending library twice. "What did she say?"

"She said the old Office used them to read the wrist chips, and to read seals. Proper Charter seals. She said if you put a seal to it, it would tell you if the seal was true. And it talks on the old band. To the bell." Yara touches the coil of wire on its side. "She asked where I got it. I told her. She went very quiet. Then she said, *Keep it dry.*"

Pim turns it over in his hands. He finds a catch on the underside that she never noticed, and presses it, and a panel slides back to show a row of tiny brass contacts and a socket. He looks at it for a long time.

"It's got a cell," he says. "Look. A cold-cell. They last forever." He presses something else, and the dull glass eye on the front of the box glows, very faintly, amber, like a coal; and then goes out. "It's not dead," says Pim softly. "It's just asleep."

* * *

Five passes. The Hulk at the front of the window glows like a forge.

They can't go back the way they went before. The ice wall between the Office and the vault has melted back perhaps five metres since last time, and the tangle of cabinets that came down on them has frozen into what's left of it, so that the passage is blocked by steel as well as ice. But Quell's drawing shows a second way. From the Hall of the Account — the great drowned cavern near the bow, where the Houses held their ceremonies — a service passage runs aft along the spine on the far side of the Old Hold, and comes back round to the vault level from the other direction. Quell never took it. It was solid ice in her time. *Might be clear now*, she said, with her finger on the line. *Might not. If it's clear, it'll be the only clear thing in there.*

They go in through the Mouth and along the ring to the forward spoke and down it into the spine, toward the bow, into water so cold their lamps fog at the edges.

The Hall is the biggest room Yara has ever been in.

It's bigger than the Old Hold. It's bigger than the Ash Church. It goes on and on in the dark ahead of them, a long iron cavern with ribs arching overhead like the bones of some enormous animal, and it's full of water, and it's full of rags. They hang from the ribs on cords, hundreds of them, long rotted strips of something that was once silk and once grey, and in the water they sway — slowly, all together, the way the cradle lids swayed in the Old Hold — like kelp in a current, like smoke. The lamps go into them and get lost. It's like swimming into a forest that's burning very slowly in the dark.

"Burning banners," says Mitya, low, over the band. "For the Burning Day. They hung them every year, Pim said."

"I know what they are."

At the centre of the Hall, where the rags are thickest, there's a disc of black iron set into the deck, two metres across, scorched and pitted, and a broken column of glass standing up out of the middle of it, empty, its top shattered off. Yara swims down to it without meaning to. She puts her gauntlet on the iron. It's colder than the water.

"This is where they burned it," she says.

"It's in the Church now."

"I know. But this is where."

She doesn't know why it matters to her. It doesn't. She takes her hand off the iron and swims up again, and that's when the line goes slack.

Not slack the way it goes when Mitya moves toward her. Slack all at once, completely, the coil on her hip suddenly loose and light. She turns and pulls it in, hand over hand, and it comes and comes with no weight on it at all, and then the end comes out of the dark between the rags, and it's been cut clean.

"Mitya."

"I see them."

Three lamps. Yellow shells. They come out of the forest of rags between Yara and the forward hatch, the way they came in — cutting her off from the spoke, from the Mouth, from air. One of them has a cutter in his hand, the arc still hissing, the water boiling white around its tip.

"Back off," says a voice on the open band. A man's voice, thick, Gutter accent. "Back off out of it, Tull. This is Gorse's. Warden's licence. You're on it without a permit."

"I'm on Gorse's permit."

"Not for this you're not. Gorse says you've been lent out. Gorse says that loan don't cover the spine. Back off and go home and nobody's hurt."

She knows the voice. Kell Amory. He's dived for Gorse for fifteen years. He came to her mother's ash.

"You cut my line, Kell."

"You'd have done the same."

She wouldn't. She doesn't say so. She hangs in the water among the rags with the cut end of her line in her hand and the empty column below her, and she can hear her own breath, loud and slow in the helmet, and she counts it. Her air gauge says three hours twenty. The spoke is two hundred metres behind them, through Kell and his crew. Without a line, in this dark, among these rags, she might find it and she might not.

"Yara," says Mitya on the suit band, very quietly. "Left."

There's a fourth lamp, coming in from the left, low, along the deck. Slowly. While Kell talks.

She doesn't think. She'll think about it later, for a long time, in the can at night. Now she just moves — down and left, fast, kicking off the iron disc with both boots, straight at the fourth lamp, with her own cutter coming off her back and into her hand and her thumb finding the trigger. The diver behind the lamp sees her coming and tries to turn, and in the water you can't turn fast, nobody can, and she's on him before he's half round, and she puts the cutter's arc against the seal at the back of his helmet and holds it there.

Not long. Two seconds. The water boils white between them. The seal goes. She feels it go, a jolt through the cutter, and a gout of silver bubbles erupts from the back of his helmet and streams away up through the rags, and he's thrashing, both hands at the back of his neck, and the bubbles keep coming.

She lets go and kicks back.

"*Ferro!*" Kell's voice on the open band, high. "Ferro, seal it, seal it—"

The man is spinning slowly in the water, clawing at his helmet, the bubbles pouring out of him in a long silver rope. He'll have a patch kit. Every diver has a patch kit. If he finds it in the next minute he'll live. If his crew stop and help him, they'll have to stop doing anything else.

They stop. All three yellow lamps converge on him, fast, swinging, and for a moment the four of them are a knot of light and limbs and silver bubbles in the middle of the Hall, and the rags swirl round them, and nobody is between Yara and anything.

"Go," she says to Mitya. "*Go.*"

They go. Not back toward the spoke — the yellow lamps are too near it — but aft, down the length of the Hall, through the rags, toward the far end where Quell's drawing shows the service passage. Mitya first, then Yara, kicking hard. Behind them Kell is shouting on the open band, a long stream of words she stops hearing after the first few. Her heart is going like the Hulk at perigee.

The passage is there. A square black opening in the aft wall, half hidden behind a curtain of rotted silk. Mitya pulls the silk aside and they go through, and on the other side the water is still and clear and empty, and their lamps go a long way down a straight Charter corridor with hazard stripes on the walls, red and yellow, red and yellow, and no ice in it at all.

* * *

It's clear the whole way.

They follow it for two hundred metres, aft and then round, past hatches marked STORES and HOLD AFT and a long stretch of blank wall where the Old Hold is on the other side, until the corridor turns again and they find themselves in a passage Yara recognises: the one from the Office, the one that ended in the ice wall. They're on the other side of it now. Behind them, ten metres back, the ice is a cloudy grey face full of frozen debris, and the tangle of cabinets that came down at them is a dark shape inside it, like something seen through fog. Ahead of them, at the end of the passage, there's a door.

It's iron. It fills the passage from wall to wall and floor to ceiling, black and smooth and enormous, with a wheel at its centre the size of a sled and a heavy frame of riveted plate around it, and above the frame, on the iron of the wall, there are letters. They were painted once. Red, Yara thinks, from the flakes that are left. Most of them have gone in the wet. What's left says:

WHAT IS WRIT — — — OWED

She stares at it.

"Vault," says Mitya, behind her. He sounds very tired. "That's it. That's the vault."

There are marks on the door. Old ones — a row of shallow scorches across the iron at chest height, where someone, a long time ago, cut at it with a thermal lance and gave up. Quell's crew, forty years ago. A thumb's depth in. Yara puts her gauntlet on them.

"Air," says Mitya.

"I know."

"Two hours ten. And no line. And Kell's crew between us and the spoke."

"I know, Mitya."

She takes her hand off the door. She looks at it for a long moment — the wheel, the frame, the ruined letters — and then she takes the marker spray from her harness and puts a small yellow cross on the wall beside the frame, the divers' mark for *here*, and under it the number five.

"Next pass," she says.

They go out the long way, round through the service corridor and the Hall and its rags, slowly, with their lamps down, listening. There's no sign of Kell's crew. There's a patch on the deck near the iron disc where the water is cloudy with something dark, and a little cluster of silver bubbles still trapped against one of the ribs overhead, trembling. That's all.

* * *

They come up into the Ringfall shuttle dock at the end of the window with forty minutes of air left, and the dock is full of black.

The *Lawful Remedy*'s shuttle is berthed at the far end, where the big ferries used to come in during the Houses' time: a long sleek black wedge with the key-and-sword crest on its flank, its ramp down, a line of bailiffs on either side of it. And coming down the ramp, between the bailiffs, are two people.

The first is the Factor. Aurel Kade, in his plain grey suit, walking with his hands clasped behind his back and his head bent a little toward the person beside him, as if listening carefully to something she's saying.

The second is Sabeth Orrin.

She's in the same good grey wool, the same soft boots. Her hair is pinned up. She's talking to Kade in a low voice, and he's nodding, and at the foot of the ramp he stops and puts his hand on her shoulder — lightly, briefly, the way you'd touch a colleague you trust — and says something, and she smiles.

Yara stands at the other end of the dock in her mother's unpainted shell, with her helmet under her arm and the cut end of her line still coiled on her hip and the smell of the Hulk coming off her in waves, and watches them.

"Yara," says Mitya, behind her, very quietly.

She doesn't turn round. She's watching Sabeth Orrin's face. And so she sees the moment, at the foot of the ramp, when Sabeth's eyes travel along the dock, across the divers and the cargo and the bailiffs, and find her.

Sabeth doesn't look away. She doesn't look surprised. She looks at Yara across the length of the shuttle dock for perhaps two seconds, with her plain broad face perfectly still; and then she turns back to the Factor and says something else, and they walk away together, toward the lift.

Five passes.
