# Chapter 18: The Empty Box

*The Salvage · CR 1317*

The inside of the *Lawful Remedy* is white and quiet and smells of nothing at all.

Yara has never been anywhere that smelled of nothing. Ringfall smells of scorched metal and fish sauce and people; the Hulk smells, through the shell's filters, of rust and cold; even the Ash Church smells faintly of lamp oil and old ice. The *Remedy* smells the way the inside of a new shell smells for the first hour, before you've sweated in it: clean, filtered, empty. The corridors are wide and softly lit and lined with a pale material that gives a little under her hand. There's no sound but the air. The bailiff who brought her up on the shuttle walks a step ahead of her, unhurried, his long black coat whispering, and doesn't speak.

They pass a long window. Not to the outside — to a room. It runs the length of the corridor, thirty metres, and on the other side of the glass there are bunks, in tiers, six high, very neat, and on the bunks are people in grey. Hundreds. Sitting, lying, talking low. A woman braiding a girl's hair. A man with one arm, lying on his back with his eyes open, looking at the bunk above him. Nobody's crying. Nobody's doing anything. They look like people waiting for a ferry that's late.

"Satisfaction hold," says the bailiff, without turning his head. "Lot one. Very comfortable. Through here, please."

* * *

Pim is in the medbay.

It's a white room with a white bed, and he's sitting up in it in a white gown with a clear mask over his nose and mouth, and a thin tube from the mask to a machine beside the bed that hums. On the machine's screen there's a picture of two shapes like grey trees, slowly turning, and inside the trees, like weather, patches of darker grey that are slowly — very slowly, as she watches — getting paler.

"Yara." His voice through the mask is muffled. And strong. She notices that before anything. "They let you come."

"They asked me."

"Look." He points at the screen. "That's me. My lungs. That's the scarring. It's cleaning it out, a bit at a time. Doctor Hallam says six more days and it'll be all gone." He takes a breath — a long one, deep, all the way down, the kind of breath she hasn't heard him take since he was ten — and lets it out, and laughs. "It doesn't hurt. I can breathe, Yara. Listen. I can just *breathe*."

She sits down on the edge of the bed. She takes his hand. It's warm. It's never warm.

"Mitya," says Pim, and stops. "They told me. The Factor came and told me himself. He said it was very brave." He looks at her over the mask. "Was it?"

"Yes."

"He said Mitya asked them to move Petra's family down the schedule. And they did it. As a mark of respect." His eyes are very steady. "Did he? Ask them?"

"Yes."

Pim nods slowly. He's quiet for a while. Then he says, "Did he say anything? At the end?"

"He said he was sorry about the copper. The bar he lost. In the slosh." She has to stop. "He said you'd know."

Pim looks at her for a long moment. Then he closes his eyes, and turns his face away toward the humming machine and the grey trees on its screen, and his hand in hers holds on very hard.

* * *

The Factor's office is the only room on the ship with a window to the outside.

Through it, Tamar is turning, white and grey and cracked like an old plate; and beyond Tamar, filling the lower half of the glass, rust and cream, banded, enormous, Absalom. Somewhere against the face of it, too small to see from here, the Hulk is climbing toward apogee for the second-to-last time.

Aurel Kade stands at the window with his hands behind his back.

"My condolences," he says, turning. "Mr Saar was a brave man. I understand he died trying to save you." He gestures to a chair. "Please."

She doesn't sit. Sabeth Orrin is standing by the door, very still, in her good grey wool, with her hands folded. She doesn't look at Yara. She looks at a point on the wall about a metre to the left.

"You've seen your brother," says Kade. "He's doing very well. Doctor Hallam is very pleased with him. Six days and his lungs will be as clean as mine." He smiles a little. "That's not a threat, Miss Tull. I want to be clear. That's simply the case. The Company treats its assets. It's in the writ."

"And after six days?"

"After six days his medical satisfaction is complete, and he's scheduled for transport with the next lot. The Company has contracts on eleven worlds. He's young; he'll be placed somewhere with good terms. Light industry, perhaps. Clerical. He reads, I'm told." Kade sits on the edge of his desk. "Or."

He lets it hang. She's heard Sabeth do it. She wonders, distantly, if they learned it from each other or from someone else.

"Or you bring me what's in the vault," says Aurel Kade. "Unopened. Whatever it is. And when you do, your brother's treatment is completed at the Company's expense, and the Company executes a deed of release for the line of TULL — the whole line, Miss Tull, the whole four million, discharged — and you and your brother are given passage to Vey on the next ship out, with Mrs Orrin's fifty thousand marks to start you." He spreads his hands. "Vey's a good world. Clean air. You'd never have to dive again."

She looks at him. At the plain grey suit and the straight back and the calm attentive tired face. At Sabeth by the door, looking at the wall.

"You know I found the door," she says.

"I know Mr Saar cut nine of its twelve bolts before he died. Mr Amory of the Warden's crew was very forthcoming." Kade's face doesn't change. "I know there's one pass left before the Assembly's engineers expect the Hulk to break up, and two at the very most. I know that you're the only diver alive who knows the clear way in. And I know that whatever's on the other side of that door has been waiting there for nearly four hundred years, and might be nothing at all — a dead man's papers, a ledger, some gambling debts — and that I'm offering you your brother's life and your own freedom for it, sight unseen." He tilts his head. "You see, I'm not asking you to do anything wrong. I'm asking you to bring me something you haven't seen."

"And burn it."

"And let me decide what to do with it. Yes." He stands up. "You don't have to answer now. You have until the end of the next window. If you come back from it without the cache, or if what's in the vault turns up anywhere but in this room — before the Assembly, before the Clerk, on the band — then I'm afraid your brother's schedule proceeds as written, and I'll have no choice in the matter. Nor will he." He walks her to the door. "I'm sorry, Miss Tull. Truly. It's a very hard thing to be asked."

In the corridor outside, a man is coming the other way. Narrow, pale, in a high-collared grey robe that falls straight to the floor, with a bead of dull crystal on a chain on his chest and a slate in his hand. Ansel Rhee, the Clerk-Notary of the Chancery of Vey, walking slowly, writing as he walks.

He looks up as she passes. His pale eyes rest on her face for a moment, without any expression at all. Then he nods, once, very slightly — the way you'd nod to someone whose name you'd written down — and goes on along the white corridor, writing.

* * *

Mireille Dunn and Mother Quell are waiting for her at the can.

They're sitting on the step outside the hatch, side by side, the small sharp advocate in her too-big coat and the broad old priest in her grey robe and her diver's harness belt, and they stand up when they see her coming along the hull.

"No," says Yara.

"You haven't heard what we—"

"I know what you're going to say. You're going to say it's everyone. If it's what Kade's afraid of, it's everyone. Four hundred thousand. The noodle man. Petra's kids. Mitya's dead for it, so I owe it to him." She stops at the hatch. "Pim's on that ship. He can breathe. I sat on his bed and held his hand and it was warm."

Dunn says nothing. Quell looks at her with the one good eye.

"Your mother," says Quell.

"*Don't.*"

The old woman is quiet for a moment. Then she nods.

"All right," she says. "I won't." She reaches into the pocket of her robe and takes out something — a short coil of old cable, braided copper in a cracked grey sheath, with a brass fitting at each end — and holds it out. "Then take this. For your box."

Yara doesn't take it. "What is it?"

"A lead. Off the bell. There's a socket on the side of your seal-reader, under the coil — I saw it when you came to the church. This fits it. The other end goes into any suit's band jack." Quell is still holding it out. "If you're ever in there and you need to talk to me — to anyone — put your box on the band through your suit, and the bell will hear it. The array sends what you give it. I told you." Her good eye is very steady. "That's all. I'm not asking you for anything. I'm only giving you a way to be heard, if you want one. Your mother would have—" She stops herself. "Take it."

Yara takes it. She doesn't know why. She puts it in the pocket of her jacket, and she goes into the can, and she shuts the hatch on both of them.

* * *

Two passes. She goes in alone.

The Hulk at the front of the window is barely the Hulk any more. Yara sees it from the sled, coming in, and for a moment she doesn't recognise it. The long iron body has twisted on itself along its whole length — not much, a few degrees, but enough that the lines of it are wrong, like a face seen in a bent mirror. The stump of the ring is gone entirely. The leading face is a black glassy scab from bow to stern. And the plumes from its trailing side aren't plumes any more but a single continuous cloud, white, glittering, streaming away behind it into the dark like the tail of a comet, so that the whole Hulk seems to be moving inside its own fog.

The Mouth is still there. She goes in.

Down the ring to the forward spoke. Down the spoke to the spine, with the water so warm now she can feel it through the shell like a bath. Through the Hall, where the rotted banners have mostly come down and lie in drifts on the deck like grey leaves, and the empty column on its scorched disc has tipped over and lies on its side. Down the long service passage aft, past STORES and HOLD AFT, slowly, with her lamp on the walls; and at the corner, the skull. Small and neat. *M.S.*

She turns the corner.

He's where she left him. Under the beam, on his back, with his lamp still burning at its strange angle — the cell on the lamp will last a year, divers say; it's the last thing to go — and his faceplate turned up toward the ceiling. She doesn't look through it. She doesn't need to. She knows what's there. She hangs in the water beside him for a while with her hand on his shell.

"Hello," she says, on the suit band, to nobody.

Then she finds his cutter. It's under his left hip, where he thought it was, wedged between his shell and the deck. She has to work it out a centimetre at a time, and when it comes free it comes all at once and she nearly loses it into the passage, and she holds it against her chest for a moment before she can do anything else.

Three dogs. Alone. With nobody to brace.

She braces herself. She wedges her boots against the beam — the beam that killed him — and her back against the frame of the door, and she sets the cutter's head against the seam with both hands and her whole body behind it, and she cuts. It's slower like this. The thrust keeps pushing her off the iron and she keeps having to stop and set herself again. The first dog takes forty minutes. The second takes thirty-five. Between them the Hulk shudders twice, long deep lurches that come through the iron into her teeth, and both times she stops and waits with her eyes shut and her hands on the cutter, and both times it passes.

The third dog goes at an hour and fifty-one minutes.

She feels it through the cutter. A lurch, a give. And then, slowly, without her touching it, the vault door begins to move. Not much. A hand's breadth. The Hulk's tumble does it: the great iron slab, freed of its bolts for the first time in nearly four centuries, swinging very slowly on its hinges as the whole ship turns around it, the seam opening in front of her like a mouth.

Dark water beyond. And cold. Colder than the passage. Colder than anything in the Hulk.

She puts her shoulder to the door and pushes, and it swings, and she goes in.

* * *

The vault is a box of black iron ten metres on a side, and it's full of ice.

Not all of it. The perigees have reached in here too, through the door and the walls, and melted the outer skin of it, so that she swims in through a layer of water a metre deep into a room that's half frozen still — the far wall and the floor and one corner locked in old grey ice, cloudy and fissured, with the near side clear. Her lamp goes into the ice and gets lost. Her breath in the helmet is very loud.

In the middle of the floor, rising out of the ice, there's a cradle.

That's what it is. She knows it at once, though she's never seen one: a padded iron bracket on a pedestal, the kind you'd make to hold something precious absolutely still. It's small. Built for a thing the size of a big book. Around it, frozen into the ice at its base, there are steel bands — four of them, thick as her wrist, snapped and twisted and splayed out across the ice like the petals of a flower — as if something much larger than the cradle had been strapped down on top of it once, and torn loose, and taken.

Empty.

On the wall beside the cradle, a shelf. Iron, like the walls. Three boxes strapped to it in a row, each the size of the cradle's missing thing, each with a lead seal across its hasp and a number stamped into its cover. She swims closer with the lamp. I. II. IV.

There's a gap between the second and the fourth.

On the other wall, above a crust of ice, a brass plaque. Green with verdigris, crusted with something white, covered in tiny writing she can't read through the crust. Old Charter letters, in a cramped hand, in columns. She puts her gauntlet on it. Brass. Good heavy brass, a metre wide. Eleven the kilo, Gorse would say. She can't move it; it's bolted into the iron. She turns on her helmet camera and takes it in, slowly, top to bottom, column by column, out of habit — the way you photograph anything you can't carry, so you can come back for it, or sell the picture to someone who will.

Beneath the plaque, set into the wall, a little steel safe a hand's breadth square.

Its door is open.

She hangs in front of it for a long time. It's very small. It has a keyhole and no handle. Its door stands open on its hinge, and inside it there's nothing. Nothing at all. A little steel box with ice in the bottom of it and her lamp shining in.

And floating above the ice in the corner of the vault, tethered by one arm to the iron pedestal of the cradle with a belt somebody looped through a handhold nearly four hundred years ago, there's a man.

He's in grey. A long grey coat, torn, and under it a grey suit. He's turned away from her, face down to the ice, his white hair drifting round his head in the water. He's been frozen and he's thawed and he's been in the water a long time, and she doesn't want to see his face, and she doesn't look. But round his neck, on top of the coat, there's a chain. Heavy brass links, and on them a medallion the size of a palm. On the medallion, a pair of scales.

An Assessor's coat. An Office chain.

She knows the song. Everyone in the Gutter knows the song. *Fen was an Assessor, grey was his coat.*

"Oh," says Yara softly, on the suit band, to nobody. "So this is where you got to."

She looks at him for a long time. At the grey coat and the chain and the belt looped through the handhold of the empty cradle. At the empty safe in the wall and the snapped steel bands on the ice and the gap on the shelf between two and four. *Fen's cache.* Something Fen stole from the Office on the Night of the Hulk. Something the people who lost it would have killed to keep. *If he'd taken it out of the Hulk, someone would have used it.*

He hadn't taken it out. He'd brought it in here. Here, to the vault, to the strongroom of the old Charter ship — and whatever it was, it was gone. Taken off the cradle, or out of the safe, by somebody, sometime, in four hundred years. Torn loose and carried away. And the Turncoat had stayed. Tied himself to the pedestal of the empty cradle with his own belt, so he wouldn't drift, and stayed here in the dark with nothing, counting, until he froze.

*Fen's in there somewhere, counting.*

She doesn't search his pockets. She doesn't touch him. She can't. She turns away from him to the shelf, and puts her gauntlets on the box marked IV, and works at its strap until the strap gives. It's heavy — not in the water, but she can feel the mass of it, the way it doesn't want to move — and she clips it to her harness with two carabiners and feels it settle against her hip, iron and lead, a box the size of a big book with a seal across its hasp.

Kade wants something sealed.

At the door she stops and looks back once. The lamp makes a small moving room of light in the black water. The empty cradle. The open safe. The plaque with its columns of tiny writing under the crust. And the grey man in the corner, tied to nothing, drifting very slightly on his belt as the Hulk turns round him, with his face to the ice.

She doesn't make a mark. She doesn't know what she'd write.

* * *

She comes up into the Gutter at the end of the window with twenty minutes of air and an iron box on her hip, and nobody looks at her.

In the can, she unclips it and sets it on the galley shelf, beside the brass seal-reader. They look like they belong together. The same sunburst-and-scales on both lids, though the box's is so worn she can barely see it. She sits on the bottom bunk and looks at them.

Then, because she has nothing else to do, she takes the seal-reader down, and turns it over, and finds the catch that Pim found, and presses it. The panel slides back. She holds the dull glass eye of the reader against the lead seal on the box's hasp.

Nothing. Then, very faintly, the eye glows amber, the way it glows when the bell rings. A small sound, a click. And across the glass, so faint she has to cup her hand round it to read, a line of tiny Charter letters, scrolling.

*M.I. VOYAGE IV. CLOSED UNDER SEAL CR 597. ALL OBLIGATIONS DISCHARGED OR TRANSFERRED. SEAL TRUE.*

She reads it three times.

A closed book. A ship's ledger from a voyage more than seven hundred years ago, its debts all paid, its people all landed and dead on worlds she's never heard of. The seal true. Of course it's true. Who would bother to forge a dead book?

She sets the reader down. She sits on the bunk in the dim can with her hands between her knees and looks at the iron box on the shelf and the brass eye glowing faintly beside it, and then going out.

Two passes. One window left, if the engineers are right, and maybe not even that. Pim in a white bed with a mask on his face, breathing all the way down. Mitya under a beam with his lamp burning. A grey man in an empty vault, tied to nothing. Four hundred thousand names on the concourse screen, scrolling. And on the shelf, the only thing she's brought out of the Hulk in two weeks that anyone might pay for, a box of debts that were settled seven hundred years ago.

On the inside of the hatch, under her mother's two hundred and nine, there are two short lines in fresh scratches. She looks at them. She doesn't add a third.

Somewhere far off on the other side of the Gutter, the Ash bell rings the hour, seven notes falling. The seal-reader on the shelf clicks once in answer and glows, and goes dark.

She sits in the dark and doesn't count anything at all.
