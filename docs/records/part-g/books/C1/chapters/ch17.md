# Chapter 17: Burning Day

*The Account · CR 929*

I went up to the Hulk on the Burning in a clean grey coat that wasn't mine.

Mags found it in the laundry. An Assessor's long coat, sent down from the Office the week before for cleaning, with a burn on one cuff where its owner had leaned on a lamp, and a name stitched inside the collar that I didn't know. It fitted me badly across the shoulders. I put my own pin on it — I'd had it in my trouser pocket since the night in the Sump, and nobody had thought to take it off me, because nobody had thought I'd want it — and Mags looked at me and said I looked like an Assessor, God help me, and that was the idea.

The pilgrim ferries started at five in the morning. Every one of them full: forty seats and two hundred people standing, packed in so close that you didn't need to hold the rails. Gallows Reach families, and refinery families, and people from the Sump in their best coats with their children on their shoulders; old women with bowls of ash wrapped in cloth on their laps, to mark the wrists of the ones who couldn't afford the Office's; ice miners still grey from the shift, who'd come straight from the galleries without washing. It's the one day in the year the ballast of Tamar goes up to the Hulk in its thousands, to stand in the Old Hold among the cradles of the Crossing and remember. The Houses encourage it. It's good for the Mercy.

Nobody looked at me. On the Burning, an Assessor's grey is just a colour. I stood among them in the hot crush with my hand inside the borrowed coat on the papers against my chest, and across the cabin, wedged against the forward bulkhead with a cloth bundle in her arms, Calla Dray stood looking at nothing, and didn't look at me either.

There were forty-one of hers on that ferry. I'd counted them as they boarded. I'd stopped counting at the dock, because there'd been forty-one on the last ferry too, and there would be on the next.

* * *

Bastian was in the Old Hold.

I'd known he would be. Every junior Assessor in the Office works the Burning — marshalling the crowds in the Old Hold, keeping the lines moving past the ash bowls, making sure nobody climbs the cradle racks or scratches their name on a shell where the Speaker will see it on her walk-through. It's a junior's job. I'd done it myself for nine years. And Bastian Oduya had worked the Burning since he was twenty, and was proud of it, and had told me so, more than once, over tea.

The Old Hold on the Burning is a thing I can't describe to you properly, and I've tried. A cylinder two hundred metres long and sixty across, racked from end to end with empty cradles — white shells the shape of coffins, their lids standing open, their windows dark, rising in tiers on a lattice of black struts in every direction until they're lost in a blue dimness the lamps can't reach. In the Founders' time they were full. Three thousand six hundred of them. The Houses keep them as they were, cleaned and polished, every lid open, every window clear; and on the Burning the people of Tamar come up in their thousands and float among them in the blue cold, and touch them, and look through the windows at nothing, and mark their wrists with ash. It's very quiet. Even with ten thousand people in it, it's very quiet. People whisper in the Old Hold the way they whisper in a room where someone is sleeping.

He was at the third ash station, on the aft rack, in his grey coat with a slate in one hand, directing the line. I came up behind him through the crowd. Teodor Rask and two cistern men came up on either side. I put my hand on his shoulder, the way Marsh used to put his on mine, and said, "Bastian."

He turned. He saw my face. And I watched him understand it — all of it, in about a second and a half — and go white, and open his mouth, and not say anything.

"Come with me," I said. "Quietly. Please."

He came.

There's a cradle bay on the aft rack, three tiers up, where a section of the lattice was damaged in a fire a century ago and never repaired, and the Office roped it off: a hollow among the cradles, out of sight of the main lines. We took him there. The cistern men held his arms — not hard; he wasn't fighting — and Teodor stood at the edge of the hollow and watched the crowd below, and I floated in front of Bastian Oduya among the open lids with my back to an empty cradle and looked at him.

"Ten past ten," I said. "You went into the Index at ten past ten. You came out at half past eleven with your gloves in your hand, holding them by the cuffs. Mr Rask saw you."

Bastian looked at Teodor's soft white hands. Then at me.

"I didn't—" he said.

"Don't."

He stopped. His mouth worked. He was twenty-four, and he looked about fifteen, and there were tears standing in his eyes — not falling; in zero gravity they don't fall, they collect, and his eyes were brimming with them, swimming, so that he had to blink and blink to see me at all.

"He said she was going to give the Hold a weapon," he said. It came out all at once, high and fast, as if it had been waiting behind his teeth for nine days. "The Chief. He called me in. He said Keeper Brask had found something in the archives that would start a war — that the Hold would use it to rise, and thousands would die, and the Concordance would come in with guns. He said she was old and she didn't understand what she was doing. He said I only had to get her papers back. Just the papers. He said she trusted me; she'd let me in." He swallowed. "She did. She was waiting in aisle nine. She had her glasses off and her gloves on. She thought I was — I don't know who she thought I was. She looked so surprised."

"And?"

"She wouldn't give them to me. She said she'd already sent them. She laughed. She said, *You're too late, dear, and you'll never find them, and I'm very sorry the Chief has made you do this.*" He was shaking. "And I — I took her by the arms. Just to frighten her. That's all he said, Sir, *frighten her*. And she said *Bastian, let go*, very quietly, like you'd say it to a child, and I—"

He stopped. He couldn't go on. The cistern men held his arms and looked at the cradles.

"He told me what to do after," said Bastian at last, very low. "The paint. The words. I'd never seen them on a wall. I'd only seen them on the feed. He told me to put the pen in your rooms when the proctors searched. He said — he said if you were arrested, your girl would be taken into custody, and she'd be safe there. He said if you weren't, the Speaker's guard would deal with it themselves. He said—" He looked at me with his brimming eyes. "He said it was the only way to keep her safe. Sir. I believed him. I went to see her, to — to make sure she was real. To see her birthday was right. I don't know why. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

"The man in the spoke," I said.

"I didn't know about that. I swear I didn't. Not till after." He was crying properly now, if you can call it crying in zero gravity: the tears coming away from his eyes in little trembling globes that drifted off among the cradles. "I lent you the money. Do you remember? For her portion. I gave you eighty. I wanted — I wanted—"

"I remember," I said.

I'd taken out my handset at the start. I'd had it in my hand, in the coat pocket, with the recorder running, since *ten past ten*. I took it out now and held it up so he could see it, and I watched him look at it and understand that, too.

"Will you swear to it?" I said. "Before the Verifier. Under your name."

He stared at me. Then, slowly, he nodded. "Yes. Yes. I want to. I want to, Sir, I've wanted to every day—"

"Today," I said. "In the Hall. When I tell you."

"Yes."

I believed him. I want you to know that. I looked at his face, the face of a boy from a steward's family in House Temperance who'd wanted all his life to be useful, and I believed him absolutely, and I nodded to the cistern men, and they let go of his arms.

He went.

Not fast. He didn't run; you can't run in zero gravity. He simply pushed off from the cradle behind him, very hard, with both feet, and went out of the hollow between Teodor and the nearest strut, head first, like a diver, down into the crowd below. Ten thousand people in the blue dimness. Grey coats and ballast coats and children on shoulders and old women with bowls of ash. I saw him for perhaps two seconds, a grey shape going down through the lattice among the open lids. Then he was gone, and there was only the crowd, whispering.

"Let him go," I said to Teodor, who'd turned to go after him.

"He'll tell them."

"He'll tell no one. He's going to find somewhere very small to hide and stay there for the rest of his life." I put the handset back in my pocket. I found my hand was steady. "I've got it. I've got it under his voice. That'll have to do."

* * *

I should have gone straight to the Hall then. Calla's people were waiting. Thorne was waiting, though he didn't know it. The signing was in an hour.

I went to Marsh.

I've told you why I went the first time, and I couldn't make it make sense. I can't now either. Except that this time I knew. I'd known since the fig tree, and I'd known since the spoke, and I'd heard it in Bastian's voice ten minutes before with my own handset recording. I didn't go to Marsh to be told it wasn't true. I went because I'd been taking things to him for twenty-nine years, and I wanted to take him this. I wanted to put it on his desk in front of him like a page of sums and watch him look at it.

His office door was open. He was alone.

He was misting the juniper. He floated by the glass sphere in his grey coat with the Chief's chain of brass links round his neck, holding the little brass bulb, and he was squeezing it, gently, a puff at a time, so that a fine mist drifted into the sphere through its valve and settled on the twisted branches that went every way at once. He didn't turn round when I came in.

"I thought you might come," he said.

I shut the door behind me. I had my sidearm. I didn't take it out.

"Bastian," I said.

"Yes." He misted. "He told me you'd taken him. Not in words; he hasn't got the courage for words. He sent me a message on my handset five minutes ago. It said *sorry*. That was all." He set the bulb down on its magnetic shelf. "Poor boy. He'll have gone to ground somewhere. I hope he stays there."

"You told him to kill her."

"I told him to get her papers back." Marsh turned round. He looked old, and tired, and entirely calm. "No. That's a lie, and I'm too old to tell you lies, Hollis; you were always too good at hearing them. I knew what he'd do. I've always known what people will do. It's the only gift I've ever had. I saw it in you in the dirt outside that schoolroom in Gallows Reach — that you'd do anything at all to be lifted, and that you'd spend the rest of your life paying for it, and that you'd be very, very good at your job." He smiled a little. "I was right about you. I was right about Bastian. I'm sorry."

"She was your friend."

"She was more than that, once. A long time ago." He looked at the juniper. "She'd have started a war. You know she would. The Hold with her papers in its hand, on the Burning, with the Concordance squadron sitting in orbit. Thousands dead. Tens of thousands. And at the end of it the Concordance would come down and take the system anyway — by force, under a military governor, and the people of Tamar would be shipped off to Ostrava with nothing, and the Houses with them, and nobody would get anything at all." He turned back to me. "The Sale is the soft way, Hollis. The only soft way there is. The Houses take the price and the peerage and go, and the Concordance takes the system without a shot, and the people go to Ostrava under contract — hard contracts, yes, long ones, but contracts, with terms, with an end. It's not good. I'm not telling you it's good. I'm telling you it's the least bad thing on the table. Ottilie couldn't see that. She saw the founding page and nothing else."

"The founding page is a forgery."

"Yes," said Marsh. "Of course it is."

He said it the way you'd say the time.

"Every Chief Assessor is told," he said. "On the day of his appointment, in the Speaker's rooms, privately. I was told nineteen years ago by Speaker Constance's father. The Account was written in the ninth year after Landfall, in Tamar water, under the Council's seal, because the people had begun to ask what they owed and why, and the Founders needed something written down to show them. They wrote what they wanted to be true and sealed it, and then it was." He spread his hands. "Merriam doesn't know. Most of the Keepers don't. Ottilie found it on her own, by asking the rings. She always was the best of us."

"And you've known for nineteen years."

"I've known for nineteen years." He drifted to his desk and put one hand on the brown teapot, as if for warmth. "Every ledger is a forgery, Hollis. Every one. That's what Ottilie never understood, and what you've never let yourself understand, though you've known it since you were twelve. Someone decides what happened — who owes, who's owed, what it cost — and writes it in a book and seals it, and then it's true. Not because it happened. Because it's written. The Charter's Book was a forgery too: a debt for a passage nobody asked to take, at a price nobody could pay. The Account is a forgery of a forgery. Whatever comes after will be the same. The only question there's ever been is who holds the pen."

I stood by the door with my hand on the sidearm in my coat and didn't say anything. I'd come to put something on his desk in front of him and watch him look at it. He'd looked at it. He'd looked at it nineteen years ago.

"There's one other thing," said Lucan Marsh. "Then I'll let you go and do whatever it is you've come up here to do. She found something else. In the old Charter manifests — the voyage papers of the *Merciful Increase*, in the dead stacks, that nobody's read since Landfall." He looked at me. "A ship's purser carries a key. Did you know that? Two keys, on a Charter ship. The manifests list them. Key number one, the master, broken at the death of the old purser during the Crossing and committed with his body, it says. And key number four-four-seven, the assistant's. The last line beside it says only *in the Purser's keeping*." He paused. "Never recovered."

I didn't move.

"Ottilie remembered a boy of twelve," said Marsh softly, "frightened of everything in the Hulk, showing her an old iron key on a chain his mother had given him. She remembered he'd said it didn't open anything. She remembered the number on the shank, because she remembered every number she ever saw; she was like you that way." He held out his hand. "May I?"

I took it out. I don't know why. I took the chain from round my neck and held the key up in the lamplight between us, the long iron key with its notched bit and its dull bead in the bow, that had hung against my breastbone for twenty-nine years; and I turned it, and looked at the shank, where generations of thumbs had worn the iron smooth; and there, so faint I'd never once in my life thought to look for it, were four small stamped figures.

0447.

*D-0447.* The card in her shoe. The dead account she'd hidden her notes behind. Not a portion number. A message. To a boy who'd shown her his mother's key.

"She thought it might open the purser's box," said Marsh. "The little safe in the vault, under the plaque. The one nobody's opened in three hundred years. She thought — I don't know what she thought. She was a romantic. Some dead woman's letters, I expect. A will. A bottle of something." He was still holding out his hand. "Give it to me, Hollis."

"No."

"Give it to me." Very gently. "It doesn't matter now. It never did. Whatever's in that box is three hundred years too late for anyone."

"No," I said.

He looked at me for a moment longer. Then he sighed, and let his hand fall, and touched something on his desk.

The door opened behind me. There were four of them — Mercy Guard, in dress blue for the Burning, with carbines. They'd been waiting in the passage. Of course they had.

"I'm sorry," said Lucan Marsh. "I'm going to let you watch. I think you should. Ottilie used to say that to you, didn't she? When you were a boy. *You should know what you're serving.*" He nodded to the guards. "His sidearm, please. And the key. Gently. It was his mother's."

They took the sidearm. They took the key. One of them held it out to Marsh on its chain, and he took it, and looked at it in his palm for a long moment, and put it in the pocket of his coat.

They didn't find the papers, or the wafer, or the handset. They didn't look. They were Mercy Guard, not proctors; nobody had told them to.

* * *

The Hall of the Account on the Burning is the most beautiful thing in the system, and I'd seen it every year of my life, and I'd never seen it from where I saw it then: on the forward gallery, under the Lantern stair, with my hands cuffed in front of me and two Mercy Guardsmen holding my arms.

Below me the great iron cavern was full. Twenty thousand people, the Office said afterward, though I don't know how they counted. The ballast of Tamar floating shoulder to shoulder in the zero gravity, in their best coats, with their children; filling the Hall from the deck to the ribs, from the bow hatch to the stern. And among them and over them, let down from the ribs on cords, the banners. Long strips of grey silk, ash-coloured, hundreds of them, swaying very slightly in the current of the ventilation, so that the whole Hall seemed to be full of slow grey smoke.

At the centre, on its disc of scorched black iron, the Ash Case in its column of glass.

Before it, a dais. On the dais, a table of black wood, and at the table, the heads of the Seven Houses in their plain unbleached linen. Severine Constance at the centre, silver-haired, grey-eyed, perfectly still. Beside her, in white, a tall woman I didn't know, with the stylised mountain of the Ostrava Labor Trust on her collar. On the far side, apart, in plain black with his document case open in front of him, Isaak Thorne. Behind the Houses, in a row, the Office in grey: Merriam touching her chain, the Keepers, the senior Assessors. And Lucan Marsh, at the end of the row, with his hands folded in front of him and the key in his pocket.

The Speaker rose and read the Mercy.

I won't give you all of it. You know it, or your grandparents did. It's the long form of the Catechism, the version the Houses read on the Burning: how the Increase carried us in the dark, and the Purser would have sold us, and the Founders saved us, and burned the Book before all the people on this very spot, and asked only for our Liberty in return. Severine Constance read it beautifully. She had a low, clear, carrying voice, and she didn't hurry, and twenty thousand people floated in the grey smoke of the banners and said the responses with her, the way we'd said them in the schoolroom, in the same singsong, with the same little hitch before the hard answers.

*What was her name?*

*Her name was Annick Vell, and it is written in ash.*

I looked for Wren.

The House wards were in the side gallery, opposite me, across the Hall: the children of the Houses and their clients, and the guests, in white, in a long row behind a brass rail. Sixty of them. I went along them face by face. I knew I'd know her. I'd have known her through ice.

She was eleventh from the end. In a white dress that wasn't hers, too big at the shoulders, with her hair pinned up the way the Crown pins it, and her hands on the rail. She wasn't saying the responses. She was looking at the crowd — searching it, the way I'd searched the wards, face by face — and as I watched, her eyes came up from the crowd to the forward gallery, and along it, and found me.

She saw the cuffs. I saw her see them.

I didn't do anything. I couldn't. I looked at her across the whole width of the Hall of the Account, across twenty thousand people and the banners and the Ash, and I moved my lips without any sound at all. One word.

*Door.*

I don't know if she saw it. I don't know if she'd read the towel. She looked at me for a long moment with her mother's face and mine. Then she turned her head — very slowly, as if she were only looking at something along the gallery — and looked at the small service door at the end of the wards' rail, eleven places away. And she took her hands off the rail, and began, very slowly, a little at a time, as if she were only shifting to see better, to move toward it.

*And the Book was burned.*

The Speaker sat. Thorne stood.

He didn't say much. He never did. He opened his document case and took out a slate and read from it in his planed-smooth timetable voice: that the Bureau of Titles of the Concordance of Worlds had been asked to verify the title of the Houses of Absalom to the instrument known as the Account of Liberty, for the purposes of its conveyance to the Ostrava Labor Trust under the Concordance labour code. That the Bureau had examined the instrument. That the Bureau would now state its finding.

He looked up. His crystal eye went across the Hall — swept it, end to end, the way it had swept the dock the day he arrived — and stopped, for no more than a second, on the forward gallery. On me.

I don't know what he would have said. I've thought about it a great deal. I've thought about it more than about almost anything else.

He never said it. Because somewhere in the crowd below, on the deck near the bow hatch, a man shouted.

I didn't see him. I heard him: one voice, hoarse, a miner's voice, from the middle of twenty thousand. Three words. *We were cargo!* — not ice-script; you can't shout in ice-script; but you could hear the galleries in it — and then a single flat crack, very loud under the iron ribs, and the voice stopped.

A Mercy Guardsman on the bow gallery had fired into the crowd.

For one second, perhaps two, nothing happened at all. Twenty thousand people floated in the grey smoke of the banners in perfect silence, and the Speaker sat frozen at her table, and Thorne stood with his slate in his hand and his crystal eye on the bow, and I heard, from a long way off, a child begin to cry.

Then the Hall roared.

It came from everywhere at once. Not a shout; a sound like the Hulk itself makes in the deep night, a vast iron groan, rising. The crowd moved. All of it, at once, the way water moves when a hatch blows — toward the bow, toward the shot, away from it, every direction — and in the movement I saw them: the cloth bundles opening, the pipes and the coil-gang cutters and the ugly home-made guns coming out of them, forty-one on every ferry, Calla's people, rising out of the crowd all over the Hall with their faces set. The Mercy Guard on the galleries fired again. Something exploded near the stern hatch, a flat hard thud that I felt in my teeth. The banners were swinging wildly now, tearing loose from their cords and drifting, grey silk turning slowly in the air over the crowd like smoke from a fire that hadn't started yet.

The guards on my arms let go of me and unslung their carbines.

I looked for Wren. The wards' gallery was a confusion of white dresses, children screaming, House servants pulling at arms. I couldn't see her. I couldn't see the door.

On the dais, Lucan Marsh had turned, very calmly, and was moving away from the table toward the aft hatch — the one that leads down the spine past the Index toward the Office and the vault — with one hand in his coat pocket.

And then the lights went out.

All of them. Every lamp in the Hall, every panel, every screen. It was so sudden and so complete that for a moment there was no sound at all, only the dark; and then, in the dark, twenty thousand people began to scream.
