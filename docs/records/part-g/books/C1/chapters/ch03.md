# Chapter 3: Perigee

*The Salvage · CR 1317*

The Hulk is still hot when they go in.

It is always still hot. That is the joke the divers make, and like most jokes in the Gutter it is mostly true. Every three and a half days the Hulk swings down out of the dark on its long, failing ellipse and drags its belly through the top of Absalom's atmosphere, two hundred kilometres above the cloud deck, and the friction lights it up like a coal. From Tamar you can watch it happen — a red spark crossing the rust-coloured face of the giant, trailing a thin bright tail — and then it climbs away again, out toward apogee, cooling as it goes. The cold window is the day and a half near the top of the climb, when the outer hull has radiated enough of its heat that a shell can touch it without cooking the diver inside. Most crews wait for the middle of the window. Yara Tull goes in at the front of it, when the plates still glow faintly in infrared and the meltwater inside is warm enough to move, because that is when nobody else is there.

"Seven," says Mitya, over the suit band. "Did you see the bulletin? Assembly engineers say seven passes. Then she comes apart."

"They said nine last month."

"Last month she still had her Crown."

The sled drifts in on cold gas. Ahead of them the Hulk turns slowly end over end against the stars, black and enormous, eleven hundred metres of scorched iron with half its ring torn away. Whatever she was — and she was a great many things, Yara's mother used to say, a ship and a station and a seat of government and a prison and a church — she is now mostly a shape. Her leading face, the side that meets the air first at perigee, has been ablated smooth and glassy, the old hull plates fused into a single dark scab. Her trailing face is a ruin of torn superstructure, broken vanes, the stumps of docking arms, and from a hundred cracks in it, as she warms and cools and warms again, she bleeds thin white plumes of vapour that catch the sunlight and glitter and are gone. She looks, Yara thinks every time, like something dying of a fever. Which she is.

"Mouth's clear," says Mitya. "No lights."

"Good."

The Mouth is what the divers call the breach in the trailing hull near the old docks, a tear forty metres across where something hit her a long time ago and nobody ever patched it. Yara brings the sled in under its lip, matches the slow tumble with three short bursts, and clamps on. The vibration comes up through the clamps into her shell: a deep, irregular shuddering, as if the whole Hulk were breathing.

"She's loud today," says Mitya.

"She's always loud after perigee."

"She's louder." He unclips from the sled and pushes off toward the hull, a bulky shape in a patched orange shell with a cutter on his back and a coil of line on his hip. "Fen's restless."

"Fen's dead."

"Fen's in there somewhere, counting." He says it in the singsong all divers use, the words worn smooth with saying, and then he laughs, because he always does, and she can hear in the laugh that today, for once, he means it a little.

* * *

Inside, the water is everywhere.

It has taken three hundred years of slow freezing for the Hulk's interior to become what it is now, and thirty years of falling for it to start becoming something else. For most of the Long Dimming she was a sealed iron bottle full of ice — every flooded compartment, every burst pipe, every tank and conduit and drowned room frozen solid in the dark, with her air leaked away and her heat long gone. When her station-keeping engines finally failed and she slid off her old balance point between Tamar and the giant and began to fall, the ice began to wake. Every perigee heats her a little more. Every perigee, a little more of the ice turns to water, and the water does what water does in a hull that is falling freely through space: it clings. It forms skins on every surface. It fills the sealed rooms completely and trembles there, held in by doors that were shut three centuries ago. In the open corridors it drifts in globes the size of a fist or the size of a man, wobbling, merging, splitting, catching the beams of the divers' lamps and throwing them back in slow shivering patterns across the walls.

Yara loves it. She has never said so to anyone. It is the most beautiful thing she knows.

They go in through the outer ring, along a corridor whose walls are lined with water like the inside of a throat. Their lamps make moving rooms of light in the dark. At the first junction there is a hatch they rigged eleven days ago, a crude airlock of welded plate and a salvaged door seal that lets them into the flooded section beyond without draining it out into the void, and they go through one at a time, and on the other side the world becomes liquid.

Diving the Hulk is not like swimming. There is no up and no down; there is only the water, and the walls somewhere in it, and the line on your hip that goes back the way you came. The water is cold and grey with silt and full of things. Paper — real paper, from the Houses' time, drifting in pulped grey clouds. Fragments of furniture. Wiring. A child's shoe. At one turning, a portrait in a gilt frame, face down, slowly rotating. Yara pushes it aside with one gauntlet and it turns over as it goes, and for a moment her lamp catches the face of a woman in a high collar, painted very finely, with an expression of perfect contempt, and then it is gone into the dark behind her.

The walls in this part of the ring are panelled in some pale stone that has gone green in the wet, and carved into the panels, at intervals, in tall elegant letters, are words. CONSTANCE. CONSTANCE. CONSTANCE. The old House names. They are all through the ring. Divers use them for navigation: you go left at PRUDENCE and through the arch at TEMPERANCE and if you find yourself at CLEMENCY you've gone too far and you'll want to watch for the vent shaft. Nobody remembers what the Houses were, exactly. They were rich, and they lived up here in the turning ring with gardens and real stone while everybody else lived in the ice, and then they were gone. Some of the families in Saltmarch with good flats still claim them. Nobody believes them.

"Here," says Mitya.

The salvage tonight is copper. There is a junction room off the CONSTANCE corridor where the ring's old power bus came through, and the bus bars are solid copper as thick as a wrist, and copper is fetching forty-two the kilo at Gorse's scale this month because the refineries in Saltmarch are rebuilding their furnace coils. They found the room on the last dive. They have nine hours of window and four hours of air.

They work. It is slow work, and hard, and in the water every movement costs twice what it would in air. Mitya cuts. Yara braces the bar, and when it comes free she wrestles it into the bag on her back, and they move to the next. The cutter's arc boils the water around it into a cloud of hissing bubbles that tremble away into the dark, and every few minutes the Hulk groans around them — a long low iron sound that comes through the water and into the shell and into Yara's teeth — and they both stop, and wait, and when it passes they go on.

Between the third bar and the fourth, while Mitya is changing the cutter's tip, Yara drifts to the end of the junction room and finds a door.

It is not much of a door. Narrow, a service hatch, its seal long gone, so that it opens with a push into a little room beyond: a dozen square metres, flooded like everything else, with a desk bolted to the floor and a rack of empty shelves and a strongbox in one corner with its lid torn off. An office. There are a lot of little offices in the ring. Most of them have been stripped already, years ago, by crews who dived the Hulk when it was still mostly ice and you went in with a thermal lance and came out with whatever you could carry. This one has not been touched. Its hatch was hidden behind the bus housings. On its back wall, in brass letters fixed to the stone, are the words OFFICE OF THE ACCOUNT — RING ANNEX, and beneath them, in smaller letters, a motto she can't read for the green crust over it.

The desk has one drawer. It is stuck. She braces her boots against the desk and pulls, and the drawer comes out all at once in a cloud of silt, and something heavy tumbles slowly out of it into the beam of her lamp.

She catches it. It's a box. Brass, the size of a big book, with a lens of dull glass set into its face like an eye and a coil of fine wire wrapped around one side, and on its lid an emblem she half recognises: a sunburst, and under it a pair of scales. Under the emblem, stamped into the metal, are the same words as the wall. OFFICE OF THE ACCOUNT.

"What's that?" Mitya is in the doorway.

"Don't know. Brass."

"Brass is eleven the kilo."

"It's heavy brass."

"It's Office junk." He drifts closer and turns his lamp on it. "That's a seal-reader. My grandad had a picture of one. They used them to read the old wrist chips — your portion, your number, all that. Before the Dimming." He taps the coil with one finger. "That's a Charter-band aerial. They talked to each other on the old band. It's worth nothing, Yara. Nobody's used the Charter band in two hundred years except the Ash bell."

"It's worth eleven the kilo."

"It's worth eleven the kilo," he agrees, and laughs, and goes back to the copper.

She keeps it. She couldn't say why. She clips it to her harness, where it hangs against her hip like a second heart, heavy and cold, and she goes back to work.

* * *

On the way out, the Hulk tries to kill them.

It is not personal. The Hulk tries to kill everyone eventually. They are three bars up and two hours from the end of their air, working their way back along the line toward the airlock, when the long low groan comes again through the water, louder than before, and keeps coming, and the walls of the CONSTANCE corridor begin very slowly to move.

"Slosh," says Yara.

It happens when the Hulk's tumble shifts — when some unseen mass of ice deep in her breaks loose, or a compartment fails, and her spin wobbles, and all the free water in her responds at once. In the open corridors it's a current. In the flooded sections it's a wall. She sees it coming, a grey thickening of the silt at the far end of the corridor, a rolling cloud of paper and debris advancing on them like weather, and she has time to grab the nearest handhold — an old brass rail, worn smooth — and shout Mitya's name.

The current hits. It is like being taken by a hand. Her body is pulled out straight from the rail, her bag of copper dragging at her shoulders, the seal-reader slamming against her hip, the silt so thick that her lamp shows her nothing but a blind grey glow. Something big tumbles past her in it — the portrait, she thinks absurdly, the woman with the contemptuous face — and strikes her helmet and is gone. Her gauntlets are locked on the rail. She can feel the line on her hip go taut and then go slack, taut and slack, as Mitya is thrown about at the end of it.

Then it passes. The water slows, and settles, and the silt begins slowly to clear, and she is hanging from the rail with her arms on fire and her heart going like a pump.

"Mitya."

Nothing.

"*Mitya.*"

"Here." His voice is thin. "Here. I'm here. I'm caught on something. Hold on."

He is twenty metres down the corridor, wedged against a doorframe where the current threw him, his line wrapped twice around a broken stanchion. His shell is scraped bright along one side. He is laughing, in a high, unsteady way she doesn't like. "Did you see that? Did you see it? The whole ring moved. That was the whole ring."

"Are you hurt?"

"My pride." He unwinds the line. "And my bar. I lost a bar."

"We've got three."

"We had four." He pushes off from the doorframe and comes up the corridor toward her, slowly, and stops beside her at the rail. Through the faceplates, in the lamp light, she can see his face — the long Saltmarch face, the stubble, the eyes that are always laughing at something just behind you. They are not laughing now. "She's coming apart, Yara," he says quietly. "Seven passes. You can feel it."

"I know."

They go out by the spine.

It's not the fastest way, but the slosh has filled the ring corridor behind them with debris, and the spine route is clear. The spine is the old core of the Hulk, the long weightless shaft that runs from her bow to her stern, and it is the part of her that is most a ship. The Houses and the Office and the Dimming and the falling have changed her ring past recognising, but the spine is still Charter work: wide square corridors with hazard stripes on the walls in faded red and yellow, hatches with heavy dogged wheels, and everywhere the neat stencilled labels of a ship that once carried people across the dark. HOLD ACCESS. PURSER'S DECK. BALLAST 3. BALLAST 4.

The fourth ballast tank is sealed. Every diver knows it. It sits against the spine in the dark near the old Hold, and its access hatch is a great round iron door, dogged shut and welded at the rim with a bead of slag three centuries old, and riveted to the middle of it is a plate in the Office's hand: CONDEMNED — BY ORDER OF THE OFFICE OF THE ACCOUNT. Below the Office's plate someone has painted, much more recently and much more crudely, a skull, and beside the skull two names and a year. BRAM. OLE. '16.

Bram Osgood and Ole Varrick. They cut the weld on the Widow last spring, on a bet, at the end of a long cold window, and went in. They were good divers. Ole's line came out four days later on its own, cut clean. Nobody went in after them. Something about the Widow — the way she's built, the way the current runs in her when the ice thaws — makes her a trap. Gorse welded the hatch shut again himself and painted the skull. Divers say she's full of ice still, at the bottom, and the ice moves.

As they pass her, Yara hears it. She doesn't mean to stop. She stops. Through the iron, through the water in the corridor and the water in the tank, comes a slow, dull knocking — irregular, faint, like something heavy turning over and over in its sleep.

"Ice," says Mitya.

"I know it's ice."

"Come on. Air."

She goes. She doesn't look back. But she can feel it behind her for a long time, the knocking, coming up the line, until the airlock closes on it.

* * *

Gorse weighs the copper at forty-one the kilo, not forty-two, because the bars have been cut short and he says short bars are harder to sell. Nobody argues. You don't argue with Gorse on his own scale. He is a vast soft man who has not been inside a shell in twenty years, with a beard dyed the colour of rust and three rings on each hand, and he runs the biggest salvage concession on the Hulk under licence from the Warden of Tamar, and every crew that dives the Hulk dives it on his permit or not at all. He takes sixty per cent. He takes it first, before the weight is even off the scale, and then he takes what you owe him, and then he gives you what's left in Saltmarch scrip, which is printed on plastic film and is worth slightly less every week.

"And that," he says, tapping the seal-reader where it sits on the scale beside the copper.

"I'm keeping that."

"Brass. Eleven the kilo."

"I'm keeping it."

Gorse looks at her for a moment with his small wet eyes. Then he shrugs, an immense slow movement, like the Hulk shifting. "Keep it. Your mother kept junk too." He gives her a slip of film. "Mitya stays. We need to talk about his book."

Mitya's book is the ledger of what he owes Gorse, which is a great deal. Yara does not look at him as she goes. He does not look at her.

The Gutter is what everyone calls the cluster of habitation cans clamped to the underside of Ringfall dock, in low orbit over Tamar. Ringfall was built in the Houses' time to service ferries to the Hulk, and when the Hulk was abandoned the dock was too, and the divers moved in — first into its empty offices and stores, then into the cans they bolted to its hull when the offices filled up. There are perhaps four thousand people in the Gutter now. Divers, mostly, and their families, and the people who sell to divers: welders, shell-fitters, scrip-changers, the noodle cart on the main concourse, three bars, a clinic that is open on alternate days. It smells of scorched metal and fish sauce and too many people breathing the same air. Its corridors are lit by strings of salvaged bulbs. On its walls, layered over each other, are a hundred years of divers' marks: names, dates, tallies of dives, the House names copied out of the Hulk for luck, and everywhere the little scratched skull that means someone didn't come back.

There's a crowd at the concourse screen.

Yara notices it because there's never a crowd at the concourse screen. It shows the Warden's bulletins, the perigee schedule, the copper price. Today there are two hundred people packed in front of it, and nobody is talking, and on the screen is a ship.

It's not a ferry. It's not anything she's seen before. Long and narrow, black, with a hard bright sheen like oil on water, and along its flank in tall white letters a name: LAWFUL REMEDY. Under the name, smaller, a crest — a key crossed with a sword, inside a circle of words too small to read. It hangs in Tamar orbit against the stars, very still, very clean.

"What is it?" she asks a woman beside her.

The woman doesn't look round. "Bailiffs," she says.

The Warden's face replaces the ship. Corin Asch is sixty, heavy-jowled, with the high colour of a man who drinks; he has been Warden of Tamar for twenty-two years, which is longer than any Warden in living memory, and he has the trick of looking at a camera as though it has disappointed him personally. He reads from a slate.

"— from the High Chancery of Vey, under the Accords of Settled Space, a writ of execution in favour of the Paschal Recovery Company, assignee of record of the instrument known as the Account of Absalom." He pauses. He seems to be reading it for the first time. "The Recovery Company holds a certified copy of the said instrument, under the seal of its original issuers, and asserts that the obligations recorded therein were never discharged and have accrued interest under the terms of the instrument since the date of its last entry." He looks up. "The Wardenship has examined the writ and finds it in order. Registration of liable persons will commence on Tamar and Ringfall tomorrow at the eighth hour. All residents are required to present themselves. Further notices will follow."

The screen goes back to the ship.

For a moment, nobody says anything. Then someone laughs — a man near the front, an old diver Yara half knows, with one arm. "The Account?" he says. "The *Account*?" He looks round at them, grinning, inviting them to share it. "That's a story. That's a Burning story. They burned it. My grandmother told me. They burned it on the ice and danced on the ash."

"They burned the Book," says someone else. "Not the Account."

"Same thing."

"It's not the same thing."

Then everyone is talking at once.

Yara doesn't stay. She knows the stories as well as anyone — the Book and the Burning, the Purser who would have sold them, the Houses and their Liberty, the Account, Fen the Turncoat on the Night of the Hulk — and she knows that stories are what people tell each other when nobody can remember what happened. None of it has anything to do with copper. She pushes out of the crowd and down the concourse toward home, and as she goes she hears, faint and clear above the noise, the Ash bell ringing the hour.

It isn't a bell. It's a chime on the old Charter band, broadcast from the Ash Church's tower on the ice above Saltmarch, where someone two hundred years ago rigged a salvaged array to sound the hours for the faithful. Every radio in the system picks it up. Seven notes, falling. On Ringfall they come through the concourse speakers, and the divers' suit bands, and the cheap receivers in the noodle cart; and for once, tonight, everyone in the Gutter stops talking to listen to them, as if they might mean something.

At her hip, the brass box she took from the drowned office gives a single faint answering click.

She looks down at it. Nothing. Old brass, dull glass, a coil of wire. She keeps walking.

* * *

Pim is coughing when she gets in.

Their can is one of the old ones, eight metres by three, with two bunks and a galley and a curtain across the end where the toilet is. Their mother bought it the year Pim was born. It still has her diving marks on the inside of the hatch: two hundred and nine dives, scratched in fives, and then nothing, because the two hundred and tenth dive was the one she didn't come back from. Yara has never added hers below. Pim says she should. She says she will when she gets to two hundred and ten.

He's on the top bunk with a blanket round his shoulders and a slate on his knees, coughing into his sleeve, a long rough barking that goes on and on and leaves him grey. Rime-lung. Fourteen years of breathing Gutter air with ice-dust in it, the doctors at the alternate-day clinic say, though a doctor on Vey would probably say something else. On Vey they could fix it. On Vey there are clinics with machines that clean the scarring out of a lung like scrubbing a pot. The passage to Vey alone is eight thousand marks. The treatment is more than that. She's been counting it for three years.

"You're late," he says, when he can.

"Copper was heavy."

"You're bleeding."

She looks. There's a smear of blood on the inside of her wrist where the shell's seal chafed when the slosh hit. "Not mine," she says, which is a lie, and he knows it, and lets it go, which is one of the things she loves him for.

"Did you see?" He holds up the slate. On it, small and grainy, is the black ship. "Lawful Remedy. It's from Vey. It came through the lane yesterday and nobody said anything until today. Yara, they're saying it's the *Account*."

"I saw."

"But the Account was a — it was the Houses' thing. The Liberty Debt. That was three hundred years ago." Pim reads everything. He's read everything in the Gutter's little lending library twice, and everything the Ash Church will let him borrow, and he collects old words the way other boys collect salvage: songs and sayings and the names of things that don't exist any more. "How can anyone owe something from three hundred years ago?"

"Ask the bailiffs."

"I'm asking you."

She unclips the seal-reader and puts it on the galley shelf and starts peeling off the inner suit. "I don't know, Pim. Interest, I suppose. Someone always owes something." She looks at him. "Did you take your drops?"

"Yes." He's looking past her, at the door. "Yara. There's someone here."

She turns.

There's a woman sitting on the step at the end of the galley, in the curtain's shadow, where Yara didn't see her when she came in. She's perhaps fifty, with a plain broad face and greying hair pinned up, and she's dressed the way nobody in the Gutter dresses: in good grey wool that fits her, and soft boots, and a coat with a collar. She has her hands folded on her knees. She is entirely still, the way people are still when they've been waiting a long time and are used to it.

"I'm sorry," she says. "Your brother let me in. I told him I'd wait. My name is Sabeth Orrin."

Yara doesn't move. "What do you want?"

"To hire you."

"I dive for Gorse."

"I know. I've spoken to Gorse. He's agreed to lend you to me, for a fee." Sabeth Orrin smiles slightly. "He was very reasonable about it. I find people usually are, once they understand how much money is involved."

"How much?"

"Fifty thousand marks." The woman says it the way you'd say the time. "Ten now, forty on delivery. Vey scrip, not Saltmarch. Paid wherever you like."

On the top bunk, Pim has stopped coughing. Yara can feel him holding his breath.

Fifty thousand marks is Vey and the clinic and the machines that scrub the lungs clean, and passage back, and Gorse's book paid off, and the can, and the rest of their lives. Fifty thousand marks is more money than Yara's mother made in two hundred and nine dives. It's the kind of figure that only means one thing in the Gutter. Somebody wants something out of the Hulk very badly, and they want a diver who'll die getting it.

"For what?" she says.

Sabeth Orrin leans forward, out of the shadow, into the light of the galley lamp.

"You know the stories," she says. "Everybody does. The Night of the Hulk. The Houses and the Hold. Fen the Turncoat." She says the name carefully, as if it were a thing that might break. "On the Night of the Hulk, an Assessor of the Office of the Account named Hollis Fen stole something from the Office. Something the people who lost it would have killed to keep. It was never found."

"Fen's cache," says Pim from the bunk, very softly.

"Fen's cache," Sabeth agrees, without looking round. "The divers have a name for everything, don't they? Yes. That."

"It's a story," says Yara. "There are a hundred stories about the Hulk. Fen's cache, the Houses' gold, the Purser's ghost. Fen sold the Hold to the Concordance and ran. Everyone knows that. If he stole something he took it with him."

"Perhaps." Sabeth takes a folded film out of her coat and holds it out. "Or perhaps he didn't get the chance. If he'd taken it out of the Hulk, someone would have used it by now. Nobody has. So I think it's still there." She waits, holding out the film, until Yara takes it. "Traitors steal useful things, Miss Tull. That's what makes them traitors. And I need it found before the Hulk comes apart. Seven passes, the Assembly says. I'd like it in my hands in six."

Yara unfolds the film. It's a plan — old, copied and recopied, the lines gone soft and fuzzy: the spine of the Hulk, deck by deck, with the Office of the Account marked in red along the middle of it, and the old Index gallery, and the strongroom. Someone has annotated it, in a neat small hand, with the depths of ice and water as they were mapped thirty years ago.

"Why me?" she says.

"Because you go in at the front of the window when no one else is there," says Sabeth Orrin. "And because you need the money more than anyone else Gorse showed me." She stands up. She is shorter than Yara expected. "I'll come back tomorrow for your answer. Don't tell anyone what I've asked. Don't tell Gorse what it's for. He thinks I want copper." She nods to Pim, politely, as if he were an adult. "Goodnight."

She goes. The hatch closes behind her.

For a while neither of them says anything. Pim coughs, quietly, into his sleeve. The can hums around them — the air pump, the heater, the faint vibration of Ringfall's hull. Through the wall, from the can next door, someone is singing a diver's song, slow and out of tune, the way people sing when they've been drinking since the bulletin:

*Heave the iron, heave it slow,
down the Widow, down we go —*

"Are you going to do it?" says Pim.

Yara looks at the plan. She looks at the brass box on the galley shelf, with its dull glass eye. She looks at the inside of the hatch, at their mother's marks: two hundred and nine, in fives, and nothing under them.

"I'll think about it," she says.

She has already decided. They both know it. She folds the plan very small and puts it inside her shirt, against her skin, where she can feel its edges; and that night, lying on the bottom bunk listening to Pim breathe, she counts the passes the way her mother taught her to count dives. Seven. Seven passes, and then the Hulk comes apart, and whatever is in it goes down into Absalom forever and is never seen again.

Seven. She lies awake counting until the Ash bell rings the next hour, seven falling notes on the old band, and at the galley shelf, in the dark, the brass box clicks once more, softly, as if it were answering.
