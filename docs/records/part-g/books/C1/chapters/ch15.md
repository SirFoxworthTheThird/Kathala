# Chapter 15: What Is Writ

*The Salvage · CR 1317*

"You don't cut a door," says Mitya. "You cut what holds it."

They're hanging in the water in front of the vault with their lamps on the iron. Three passes. The Hulk at the front of this window was so hot they had to wait an hour on the sled for the Mouth to stop glowing, and even now, deep in the spine, the water is warmer than Yara has ever felt it — not warm, nothing in the Hulk is ever warm, but a degree or two off the cold, enough that she can feel the difference through the shell like a breath on her neck. The ice wall behind them in the Office passage has gone back another eight metres since last time. The cabinets that came down at them are standing clear of it now, a tangle of torn steel. The thing in the coat is gone. She doesn't know where.

The door is what it was. A metre of black Charter iron, filling the passage. The great wheel at its centre. The ruined red letters above it.

WHAT IS WRIT — — — OWED

"Look," says Mitya. He takes her lamp hand in his gauntlet and moves it, slowly, along the seam where the door meets its frame. "See those? Under the frame lip."

She sees them. Every half-metre or so, round the whole edge of the door, there's a dark square in the frame: the end of a bolt, as thick as her wrist, driven out from the door into the iron of the wall.

"Dogs," says Mitya. "The wheel drives them. Twelve, I'd guess. Turn the wheel and they pull back and the door swings. It's a Charter door; it's built to hold against vacuum and fire and a ship breaking up round it. Quell's people went at the face of it with lances, the poor bastards. You'll never cut a metre of iron with a lance. But the dogs are only iron as thick as your arm." He lets go of her hand. "Cut the dogs and the door's just a door."

"How long?"

"Each one?" He considers it, floating there, his head tilted in the helmet the way it always tilts when he's pricing a job. "Twenty minutes. Maybe twenty-five. They're good iron."

"Twelve dogs."

"Four hours. Five. If the cutter holds. If nobody bothers us." He unclips the cutter from his back. "We've got six hours of window and five of air."

She looks at him. Through the faceplate, in the lamp, his face is calm and absorbed and very nearly happy, the way it always is when there's something difficult in front of him that he knows how to do. There's still a faint shadow on his cheekbone where she hit him.

"Then cut," she says.

* * *

He cuts.

She braces. That's the work: she holds the cutter's head steady against the seam while he guides the arc, because in the water the cutter's thrust will push it off the iron if nobody holds it, and she keeps the line clear, and she watches the passage behind them. The arc boils the water white. The bubbles stream up past her faceplate in a hissing silver curtain and gather against the ceiling of the passage in a trembling sheet. The iron glows red, then orange, then a fierce small white, and drops of slag break away from it and cool instantly into black beads and drift.

The first dog takes twenty-two minutes. When it goes, she feels it through the cutter — a sudden give, a lurch — and Mitya laughs on the suit band, softly, and says *one*.

The second takes twenty. The third, twenty-six; it's thicker, or the iron's harder there. Between the fourth and the fifth he changes the cutter's tip, and she watches the passage while he does it, and it's empty. Her lamp goes a long way down it in the still water, all the way to the cabinets and the ice, and there's nothing there but the slow drift of slag beads and old cards.

"Where's Kell?" she says.

"Home, if he's got sense."

"He hasn't."

"No." Mitya fits the new tip. "Ferro'll have told him to stay off. Ferro lost an ear, you know. Frostbite, round the back where the seal went. Kell says he's going to have you for it." He sets the cutter back against the seam. "Brace."

She braces.

They don't talk much. They never have, in the water; there's no need. She knows from the angle of his elbow when he's going to shift the arc and from the sound of his breath when the iron's fighting him. Six years. Three of them in the same bunk. She'd forgotten what it was like, working with someone you don't have to watch.

At the sixth dog, he says, without looking up from the arc, "I told her about the cross."

She doesn't move. She keeps the cutter head steady on the iron.

"The yellow cross. And the five. I told her the morning after, when she came to the bar." His breath is very even. "I didn't tell her about the passage. The long way, round through the Hall. Gorse's people found the cross because they were looking for it — they'd have found it anyway, it's on the main way in. But they didn't know how we'd got to it. They still don't. I didn't tell her."

"Why not?"

"I don't know." The arc hisses. "Because it was ours, I suppose."

The sixth dog goes. *Six*, he says. He moves along the seam to the seventh.

She watches the back of his helmet in the lamp light, the scraped bright patch on the shell where the slosh threw him against the doorframe. She thinks of the four hundred and twelve marks on his hatch and her small ones underneath.

"Mitya."

"Mm."

"Brace yourself," she says, and takes the cutter head in both hands.

* * *

At the ninth dog, the Hulk moves.

It isn't like the slosh. It isn't like the groan in the Office that brought the cabinets down. It starts below hearing, a vibration that comes up through the iron of the door and the frame and the walls of the passage and into her boots and her gauntlets and her teeth, so deep and so steady that for a moment she thinks it's the cutter. Then the cutter stops — Mitya's taken his thumb off it — and the vibration doesn't.

"She's turning," says Mitya. His voice is very quiet. "Feel it? She's turning over."

The tumble. When the Crown went at the last perigee, the Hulk lost a third of her mass on one side, and since then the engineers' bulletins have been saying the same thing in longer and longer words: her spin is changing. Her slow end-over-end roll is becoming something else, a wobble, a precession. Every hour, a little more. And somewhere in her, now, something has given way to it — some beam or bulkhead that held her shape for seven hundred years has decided it's done — and the whole long iron body of her is beginning, very slowly, to twist.

"Keep cutting," says Yara.

"Yara—"

"Three to go. Keep cutting."

He cuts. The vibration doesn't stop. It rises and falls, rises and falls, like the breathing she used to feel through the sled clamps in the first passes, only bigger now, much bigger, and with a sound in it that she doesn't like at all: a long, low, grinding moan, iron on iron, coming from somewhere down the spine toward the stern.

The ninth dog goes. *Nine.*

That's when the lamps come round the corner.

Four of them. Yellow. Not from the Office passage, where she's been watching. From the other way: from the service corridor, the long way round through the Hall, the way that was theirs.

"Mitya."

"I see them." He's already turning, the cutter coming round in his hands. "How—"

"Doesn't matter."

It doesn't. They come down the corridor fast, kicking hard, two abreast, and the one in front has something in his hands that isn't a cutter: a long-barrelled thing with a fat canister on its side that she recognises after a moment as a hull-spiker, the kind welders use to nail patches over breaches. It fires steel bolts the length of a finger. In air it'd go through a shell like paper. In water it's slower, but not much.

"*Tull.*" Kell Amory's voice on the open band, thick and hoarse. "You don't move. Saar, you don't move. Put the cutter down. Back off from that door."

"Kell," says Mitya, very reasonably. "We're nearly through. Nine of twelve. Why don't we—"

The spiker coughs. Yara sees the bolt — a silver streak in the lamp light, trailing a thread of bubbles — and it goes past Mitya's helmet a hand's breadth from the faceplate and strikes the vault door behind him with a sound like a bell, and stays there, quivering, buried to half its length in the iron.

"Next one's in your seal," says Kell. "Like Ferro's. Back off."

Mitya backs off. Slowly, with the cutter held out to one side, away from his body. Yara backs off with him. They hang in the water a few metres from the door with the four yellow lamps on them, and the vibration comes up through the water into her shell, and the grinding moan from the stern goes on and on.

"Gorse wants what's in there," says Kell. "So does the Warden. There's a bounty on it now, did you know? Warden's paying a hundred thousand. Higher than your Vey woman." He laughs, harshly. "You did the hard work, Tull. I'll give you that. Nine dogs. We'll do the other three."

"She's turning, Kell," says Yara. "Feel it. She's coming apart."

"She's been coming apart for thirty years."

"Not like this."

And as she says it, the Hulk moves again.

This time it's not a vibration. It's a blow — a single huge shuddering lurch that comes through everything at once, as if the whole spine has been struck by something the size of a world. The water in the passage jerks sideways. Yara's slammed against the wall, her helmet ringing. The lamps swing wildly. Someone's screaming on the open band. And from down the service corridor, the way Kell's crew came, there's a sound she's never heard in twelve years of diving the Hulk: a long tearing shriek of metal, high and rising, going on and on, as if the ship herself were crying out.

Then the ceiling comes down.

Not all of it. A section of the service corridor's roof, three metres back from the vault door, where an old Charter beam crosses it. The beam tears loose at one end in a cloud of rust and silt and swings down through the water like a door slamming, slow and enormous and absolutely unstoppable, and everything under it goes dark.

* * *

When the silt clears, Kell's crew are gone.

She can see their lamps a long way off, back down the service corridor, going fast. One of them is trailing a thread of silver bubbles. They don't look back. She doesn't blame them. She'd have gone too.

She finds Mitya under the beam.

It's lying diagonally across the passage now, one end still fixed to the wall and the other wedged against the floor beside the vault door, and Mitya's between it and the floor. Not crushed. Pinned. It's caught him across the thighs and pressed him down against the deck, and the cutter's gone, and his lamp is shining up at the ceiling at a strange angle, and when she gets to him and puts her faceplate against his she can see his face.

He's very calm. That's the first thing. He looks at her through the glass with his long Saltmarch face, and he isn't laughing, and he isn't frightened. He looks the way he looked at the door. As if there's something difficult in front of him that he knows how to do.

"Hello," he says.

"Hold still."

"I'm holding." His voice on the suit band is thin. "Can't do much else."

She gets her shoulder under the beam. She braces her boots against the deck and pushes, and nothing happens. She pushes again. In the water she has no weight to put into it, nothing but her legs, and the beam is a beam from the spine of a ship eleven hundred metres long, and it doesn't so much as tremble.

"Yara."

"I'll get the jack. There's a jack on the sled—"

"The sled's an hour back. Yara." He reaches up with one gauntlet and puts it on her arm. "Look at my gauge."

She looks. The air line from his tank to his helmet runs over his left shoulder and down under his arm, and where it goes under his arm it goes under the beam, and the beam has pinched it nearly flat. The gauge on his chest reads eleven minutes. As she watches, it goes to ten.

"I can cut it," she says. "I can cut the beam. Where's the cutter—"

"Under me, I think. Or down the passage. It went when she hit." He tightens his hand on her arm. "Ten minutes, love. You can't cut a beam that size in ten minutes. You couldn't cut it in an hour." He waits for her to understand it. "And you've got your own air to think about. How much?"

She doesn't look.

"How much, Yara?"

"Two hours."

"An hour back to the sled," says Mitya. "If the corridor's still there. If she doesn't turn over again." He's quiet for a moment. The vibration comes up through the deck into both of them. "You have to go."

"No."

"Yes. You know it. I can see you knowing it." He smiles then, a little. It's not much of a smile, through the glass, in the bad light. "Don't make me watch you work it out. I hate it when you count."

She stays. She stays with her faceplate against his and her hand on his gauntlet on her arm, and she doesn't count anything. The gauge goes to nine, and to eight.

"I'd have told you," he says. "About Sabeth. I said that, didn't I? In the can."

"You said it."

"I meant it." His breath is starting to come short. She can hear it on the band, quick and shallow, the way the bonded used to breathe in the old stories, saving air. "And the passage. I didn't tell her the passage. I want you to know. They must have followed us. Kell must have — after the cross — he must have waited in the Hall—"

"I know."

"It was ours."

"I know, Mitya."

"Tell Petra." He stops. He breathes. "Tell Petra I tried. With the schedule. Tell her I asked." Another breath. Seven minutes. "And tell Pim—"

"What?"

He thinks about it. She can see him thinking about it, through the glass, with his eyes on hers. Then he laughs, a short breathless sound.

"Tell him the copper," he says. "The bar I lost. In the slosh. Tell him I'm sorry about the copper. He'll know." His hand tightens on her arm. "He was counting it for the clinic. He told me once. Forty-one the kilo."

She doesn't trust her voice. She nods, inside the helmet, so that he can see it.

"Now go," says Dmitri Saar. "Before you can't."

She goes.

That's the thing she'll never be able to tell anyone afterward, not Pim, not Quell, not anyone: that she went. That she took her hand off his arm, and pushed off from the deck beside him, and turned her lamp toward the service corridor, and went, while he still had six minutes of air and his eyes open, because he was right and she knew it. She went down the corridor kicking hard, and she didn't look back, and she didn't count.

At the corner, she stops. She can't help it. She turns her lamp back down the passage toward the vault.

He's still there, under the beam. His lamp is still shining up at the ceiling at its strange angle. Beside him the vault door stands in the dark with nine of its twelve dogs cut and Kell's spiker bolt still buried in the iron where it struck, and above it, in what's left of the red paint, the letters.

WHAT IS WRIT — — — OWED

He lifts one hand. Slowly. A small wave, the way you'd wave to someone getting on a ferry.

She takes the marker spray off her harness and puts it against the wall of the corridor beside her, at the corner, and draws a skull. Small and neat, the way they do it in the Gutter. Under it, she writes *M.S.*

Then she goes.

* * *

The sled is where they left it. The corridor holds. The Mouth is glowing faintly when she comes out of it, and the Hulk is turning beneath her as she clamps on — she can see it now, from outside, the new wrongness of it, the long body twisting very slightly against itself like a towel being wrung — and she runs the sled back to Ringfall with forty minutes of air and her hands shaking so badly she can barely hold the stick.

The shuttle dock is full.

Not of bailiffs. Of people. Hundreds of them, standing in rows on the dock floor between lines of black-coated bailiffs, with bags, with children, with nothing. Lot one. The defaulters of registration, and the people from the Gutter's eastern rows, and anyone else on the first satisfaction schedule whose name came up today. The *Lawful Remedy*'s shuttle is at the far berth with its ramp down, and a bailiff at the foot of the ramp with a slate is reading names, one at a time, in a pleasant voice, and the people whose names are read go up the ramp.

Nobody is fighting. That's what Yara will remember. Nobody is screaming. They're just going. A man with a bag over his shoulder. A woman holding a little girl by the hand. An old diver Yara knows by sight, with one arm, the one who laughed in front of the concourse screen the first day — *The Account? That's a story* — going up the ramp with his head down.

She doesn't stop. She goes along the side of the dock past them in her mother's unpainted shell with her helmet under her arm and the smell of the Hulk coming off her, and nobody looks at her, and she doesn't look at anybody, and she goes up the stair into the Gutter and along the concourse past the writs and down the row to her can.

The hatch is open.

She stands in front of it for a long time. Then she goes in.

The can is very neat. Someone has made the beds. Pim's blanket is folded at the foot of the top bunk, and his slate is on the shelf beside it, squared to the edge, and the brass seal-reader is on the galley shelf where she left it, and everything is exactly where it should be except Pim.

On the bottom bunk, on her pillow, there's a sheet of film.

It's printed in the Company's neat Charter letters, with the key-and-sword at the top.

*Pim Tull, minor, of the line of TULL, has today been admitted aboard the* Lawful Remedy *for medical assessment, as scheduled. He is comfortable and in the care of the Company's physicians. His condition is treatable.*

Under that, in a different hand — a real hand, a pen, a pleasant even script —

*The Factor would welcome a conversation at your earliest convenience. — A.K.*

She sits down on the bunk with the film in her hands. She sits there for a long time. Through the plating, very faint, she can hear the bailiff on the dock reading names. She can hear the air pump and the heater and the faint far vibration of Ringfall's hull. And under all of it, so low that she isn't sure she's hearing it at all, something else: a deep slow shudder, coming from very far away, from out in the dark beyond Tamar, where the Hulk is climbing toward apogee with its long body twisting and a man lying under a beam in front of a door with nine of its twelve dogs cut.

On the inside of the hatch, her mother's marks. Two hundred and nine, in fives.

She gets up. She finds the stylus Pim uses for his slate. She goes to the hatch and kneels in front of it, under her mother's marks, and for the first time in twelve years she begins to scratch her own. Not all of them. She doesn't know how many there are; she's never counted. Just one. One short line, under the two hundred and nine, cut deep into the paint.

Then a second line beside it, for the one she's going to make next.

Three passes.
