# Chapter 11: Rings in the Lattice

*The Account · CR 929*

The vault smelled the way I remembered. Old iron, old air, and something underneath like cloves.

I hadn't been inside it for twenty-eight years. I'd walked past its door perhaps ten thousand times, and touched the frame, and read the red letters, and never once gone through. You don't, if you're an Assessor. The vault is for the Keepers of the Account and the Chief Assessor and, once a quarter, the Speaker, and for the master lattice itself, which doesn't go anywhere. But the Speaker had told the Office that the Verifier was to see whatever he asked to see, and the Verifier had asked to see the founding page, and the Verifier's liaison went where the Verifier went. So at nine in the morning, six days before the Burning, I floated through the black iron door behind Isaak Thorne and two silent Concordance ratings in white, and the proctors closed it behind us, and the cold came up round me like water.

It hadn't changed. I don't know why I'd thought it would. The same dark iron walls, the same dim lamps in their cages. The same brass plaque on the wall, covered in tiny writing I'd never read. The same little steel safe beneath it with its keyhole and no handle. The same shelf, with three iron boxes strapped to it — VOYAGE I, VOYAGE II, VOYAGE IV — and the gap between the second and the fourth where the third should have been.

And in the middle of it all, on its cradle, the Account.

It's bigger than you'd think. A cube of crystal a metre on a side, the colour of smoke, so dense that the lamps go into its surface a little way and stop. It sits on the old Charter cradle in the middle of the floor — the padded iron bracket built for the Book, sized for something a tenth as big — and it's strapped down with steel bands so that it can't drift, and it looks, as it looked when I was thirteen, like a great grey animal crouched on a stool too small for it.

Every portion on Tamar is written in there. Every child born owing. Every quarter paid and every strike and every Clearance, three hundred and eighteen years of them, grown into the lattice layer on layer. My strikes. My mother's portion and her mother's. Ewan Haskell. Jory Dray. Wren.

"Keeper," said Thorne.

The Keeper of the Account was Constantia Merriam: a tall, heavy, nervous woman of sixty with a House Patience accent and a habit of touching the chain of her office as if to make sure it was still there. She'd been Ottilie's rival for twenty years. They'd hated each other the way only two people who care about the same thing can, and Merriam had wept at the funeral louder than anyone. She floated at the reading console beside the cradle — a panel of page-glass on an arm, connected to the Account by a bundle of fine cables — and she looked at the Verifier as though he were a fire she'd been told to stand next to.

"The founding page, Commander," she said. "As you requested."

She touched the console. The page-glass woke, and filled, and there it was.

I'd seen it before. Everyone has. It's printed in the front of every schoolroom copy of the Catechism, in facsimile: the first entry of the Account of Liberty, the founding text of the Houses, the page on which everything rests. I could have recited it. I read it anyway, there on the glass, because I'd never seen the real one.

*This Account is raised upon the last state of the Book of the* Merciful Increase, *Voyage the Fifth, at Landfall upon the world Absalom, as sealed by the Purser of that Book under the Ordinances, by which entry the patent of Absalom and all obligations of the hold were vested in the Council of the Founders in trust for the people of the hold; and whereas the said obligations were by the Founders' mercy remitted, saving only the Liberty owed by every person freed for the cost of that freedom, the Council hereby records that Liberty in this Account, to be held and kept by the Houses of the Founders until it be paid. And the Book was burned.*

Below it, a seal. Not a Charter sunburst; something else. A circle of seven small stars, and inside them a pair of open hands. The Council's seal. The Houses still use it.

"The voice," said Thorne.

Merriam hesitated. "Commander?"

"Every Charter instrument keeps the voice of its sealing purser. Article Twenty-two of the Ordinances. I'd like to hear the voice of the founding entry."

"The founding page is silent, Commander." Merriam touched her chain. "That's — it's the tradition. The Founders are not named. Their voices — they wished their entries to be silent. In humility." She looked at me for some reason, as if I might help. "It's in the Mercy."

"The founding page," said Thorne, "claims to be raised upon an entry sealed by the Purser. A purser's entry has a voice. A copy of a purser's entry carries the voice with it; that is what makes it a copy. A silent copy of a purser's entry is not a copy. It is a description." He didn't look at her. He was looking at the Account. "Thank you, Keeper. I'll read the lattice directly now, if you'd be so kind as to stand back."

She stood back. The two ratings took up positions at either side of the cradle. And Isaak Thorne floated forward until his face was a hand's breadth from the surface of the crystal, and put both hands flat on the steel bands, and leaned in, and looked.

I'd never seen it done. I don't suppose anyone in the system had. His right eye closed. His left — the crystal one, the faceted gold lens — moved forward a little in its socket, which I hadn't known it could, until it was almost touching the Account; and it began to make sounds. Clicks. A rapid, fine, whirring tick, like a clock with its back off, and then a pause, and then the ticking again, slower. I watched the lens turn. It turned the way a jeweller turns a stone under the glass, a fraction at a time, catching the light.

It took eleven minutes. I counted. Nobody spoke. Merriam touched her chain thirty-four times.

When he leaned back from the crystal, his right eye opened, and he looked at nothing for a moment, the way you do when you've been reading for a long time and the room comes back.

"The seal," he said, "is not a purser's seal."

Merriam made a small sound.

"A purser's key carries a lattice signature," said Thorne, in his flat timetable voice. "Every Charter key ever issued. It's grown into the bead of the key at the Charter foundry, and it marks every entry sealed with that key, the way a signet marks wax. I've read perhaps four thousand of them. This is not one. This is a corporate seal — a council seal, a company seal. It's a perfectly valid seal for a council's own instruments. It cannot seal a purser's copy, because it's not a purser's key." He turned slightly toward the Keeper. "Did the Houses never ask?"

"The — the Founders' seal is the Founders' seal, Commander. It's never been—"

"No. I see." He turned back to the crystal. "Second. The growth layers."

He held up one hand, flat, and with the other made a gesture over it, layering, as if stacking invisible cards.

"A lattice grows in a bath," he said. "A solution of mineral salts in water. Every entry is a layer; every layer takes its substance from the bath it's grown in. The water leaves a mark. Its isotopes — the ratio of heavy hydrogen to light, the oxygen — are fixed in the crystal as it grows, the way a tree's rings remember the weather of the year they grew." He looked at me then, quite deliberately, with both eyes. "The founding layer of this Account was grown in water with the isotopic signature of Tamar ice. Not Callis water. Not ship's water. Tamar."

I didn't say anything. I couldn't.

"At Landfall," said Thorne, "there was no Tamar water. There was nothing on Tamar at all; the Founders had not yet set foot on it. The only water in this system was the water the *Merciful Increase* carried in her ballast, from Callis, eleven jumps away." He set his hands back on the steel bands. "Third. The layers above the founding layer show the drift you'd expect in a bath used continuously for some years — the salts settle, the ratios shift, very slowly, very regularly. I can count back along that drift. I make it about nine years. Possibly ten. The founding layer of this Account was grown approximately nine years after Landfall, in Tamar water, under a council seal, without a voice." He paused. "It is not a copy of anything sealed at Landfall. It describes an entry it does not contain."

The vault was very quiet. Somewhere far off, through the iron, I could hear the station's ventilation, and under it, fainter, the slow deep sound the Hulk always makes, like a sleeper breathing.

"I note it," said Thorne. "I don't conclude." He turned to the Keeper, who was white to the lips. "Thank you, Keeper. That will be all for today. I'll resume the remaining entries tomorrow, in order."

* * *

He waited until we were in the passage outside, with the vault door shut behind us and the proctors twenty metres off, before he spoke again.

"You knew," he said.

"I suspected."

"The Keeper who was murdered. She found it."

"Some of it."

"Yes." He floated beside me with one hand on a rail, looking at the red letters over the door. WHAT IS WRITTEN IS OWED. "It's interesting, Assessor. I want you to understand that I find it genuinely interesting. Your Houses' founding instrument is a forgery. Not a clever one. A council of frightened people, nine years after Landfall, needing a paper for something they'd already been doing, wrote down what they wished had happened and sealed it with the only seal they had." His crystal eye clicked. "It's interesting. It's not sufficient."

I stared at him. "Not *sufficient*?"

"No."

"You just said it was a forgery."

"I said it was not a copy of an entry sealed at Landfall. Those aren't the same thing." He turned to face me. "Listen carefully, because I'll only explain this once and then you'll be on your own with it. The Bureau has a rule for late-made instruments. Many founding papers are late. People land on a world and spend nine years trying not to die, and then someone thinks to write down how it all began. That doesn't make the writing false. If the Council in year nine wrote down truly what the Purser sealed at Landfall, then the Account is a late copy of a true entry, and the obligation stands, and I verify it — with a note in the margin, deploring the irregularity." He paused. "To refuse verification, I need one of two things. Either a sealed contrary record — the original entry itself, or a sealed testimony of what it said — showing that the Account misdescribes it. Which, the Book being ash, nobody has. Or a lodging."

"A lodging."

"A sworn impeachment. An officer of the issuing office — an Assessor, a Keeper, someone with standing in the institution that keeps the instrument — swears under oath, before me, that the instrument is false and that the issuing office knows it to be false. Then the doubt is no longer mine. It belongs to the institution. And an institution that doubts its own founding paper cannot ask the Bureau to verify it." He looked at me steadily. "The Keeper might have lodged. I think she was preparing to. That may be why she isn't here."

"I'm an officer of the issuing office."

"You are." He didn't smile. "You'd need to be prepared to swear it. Before me, formally, under your name. And you'd need to be the kind of witness whose oath the Bureau can accept — someone uncompromised. Not a man with an interest. Not a man under a cloud." He reached into his coat and took out a small flat case, and from the case something the size of a coin: a wafer of crystal, pale gold like his eye, sealed in clear resin. "My reading. Under my seal. For your records, Assessor. It isn't evidence of anything by itself. It's what I saw." He held it out. "I give it to you because you're my liaison and I'm obliged to share my working with the issuing office. That's all."

I took it. It was cold. I put it in the inside pocket of my coat, against Ottilie's papers.

"The signing's in six days," I said.

"Yes," said Isaak Thorne. "I proceed in order." He turned toward the spoke. "I'll see you tomorrow at nine, Assessor. Unless I don't."

* * *

I should have gone straight to the Sump. I know that. I've had a long time to know it.

I went to Lucan Marsh.

I want to tell you why, and I find I can't, not in a way that makes sense. I'd known since the fig tree that *M.* drafted the conveyance. I'd known since the boots under my bed that someone in the Office was a day ahead of me. I'd killed a Mercy Guardsman in Spoke Five with my name in his sock, and the proctor-captain had looked at me with his face perfectly still and told me I was mistaken. I knew all of it. And I went to Marsh, because he'd crouched down beside me in the dirt in Gallows Reach when I was twelve, in his good coat, and checked my sums, and asked me my name. Because for twenty-nine years whenever I'd found something I didn't understand, I'd taken it to him. Because I thought — I did think this, I'm ashamed to tell you, I thought it all the way along the spine — that if I put it in front of him he'd look at it the way he'd looked at my sums, and frown, and then look up at me and say *Well, Hollis, what are we going to do about this?* and it would be all right.

His office door was open. He wasn't there.

The juniper was there, in its sphere, going every way at once. The brown teapot was on the desk with its magnetic base, still warm. And Bastian Oduya was there, standing by the desk with a stack of slates in his arms, and when he saw me in the doorway his face went the colour it had gone in aisle nine, the morning we found her.

"Sir," he said. "Assessor Fen. I didn't — the Chief's not — he's with the Speaker."

"I'll wait."

"Sir, I don't think—" He stopped. He looked past me, into the passage. He looked back at me. And I saw it in his face, very clearly, the way you see the figure on the tally before you've read it: that he was sorry. That he was very, very sorry about something, and that the something had already happened.

"Bastian," I said. "What have you done?"

The proctors came round the corner of the passage behind me.

There were six of them, in black, with stingers, and the proctor-captain from Spoke Five at their head, and they weren't hurrying. They came along the passage hand over hand, the way everyone moves in the spine, and they stopped a little way off in a loose half-circle, and the captain said, quite formally, as if reading it off a card:

"Assessor Hollis Fen. You're under arrest for the murder of Keeper Ottilie Brask."

I didn't say anything. I'd like to tell you I said something clever. I didn't. I hung in the doorway of the Chief Assessor's office with my hand on the frame and stared at him.

"Her pen was found in your quarters this morning," said the captain. "In a search ordered by the Chief Assessor, after information received. A steel-nibbed pen of Saltmarch make, with her initials on the barrel and her blood on the nib." He moved a little closer. "And a quantity of blue-black ink of the kind she used, on the cuff of your second-best coat. The one you sold in Saltmarch two days ago. The dealer kept it. He was very helpful."

"I didn't—"

"You'll have every chance to explain, Assessor. To the Chief." He nodded to the proctors on either side of him. "Hands where I can see them, please."

They came forward. And behind me, in the office, Bastian said in a small high voice, "Sir, I'm sorry, he said it was the only way to keep her safe—"

"Keep *who* safe?"

But I knew. I knew before the proctor-captain said it, almost kindly, with his hand out for my wrist.

"Your daughter's in protective custody, Assessor. In the Crown. Since an hour ago. For her own safety." He looked at me with his still face. "There's been a fire in Lower Crown Seven. Your quarters. Very sad. The Chief was concerned that whoever's behind all this might go after the girl next. Given her father's — situation." His fingers closed on my wrist. "She's quite safe. You can see her once you've given the Chief his account."

I hit him.

I don't remember deciding to. One moment his hand was on my wrist and the next it wasn't, and he was going backward along the passage with blood coming out of his nose in a slow red string of beads, and I'd pushed off the doorframe with both feet and gone straight through the gap where he'd been. A stinger cracked past my ear. Someone grabbed my coat. I twisted out of it — out of the coat, all of it, the sleeves stripping off my arms — and left it in his hands, and I was through them, and going down the passage toward the main shaft, hand over hand over hand, faster than I've ever moved in the spine in my life.

The coat. The coat had Thorne's wafer in it.

I'd moved it. I'd moved it on the way to Marsh's office, without thinking, the way you move a thing you're afraid of losing — out of the coat and into my shirt with Ottilie's papers, against my skin. I'd done it at the bend by the Index, with my back to the passage. I'd felt foolish doing it. I felt it there now, as I went, the hard little disc of it pressed against my chest under the shirt, beside the key on its chain.

Behind me the captain was shouting. Ahead of me was the main shaft, the great square tunnel that runs the length of the spine, with the Office opening off it on one side and the old Charter decks on the other, and I came out into it and didn't stop, didn't think, just turned aft and pushed off the wall and *dived* — down the shaft, away from the Office, away from the bow and the Hall and the Crown and everything I'd ever known in the Hulk, toward the stern, toward the dark.

* * *

The spine of the Hulk is eleven hundred metres long, and most of it is empty.

People forget that. They think of the Hulk as the Office and the Hall and the Crown, because that's where people live. But the Founders built the station onto the old ship from the bow backward, and they never got very far, and aft of the Office, past the Old Hold, past the stores, the spine is still mostly what it was three hundred years ago: Charter corridors with hazard stripes on the walls, Charter hatches, Charter stencils. Sealed decks that no one's opened since Landfall. Machinery nobody understands. Cold, because nobody heats it. Dark, because nobody lights it. The proctors patrol as far as the Old Hold and not a metre further, because there's nothing back there to steal.

I went past the Old Hold. I went past the great double hatch that leads into it, open as it always is, with the empty cradle racks going up and up inside into the blue memorial dimness where, every Burning Day, the people of Tamar come up on the ferries to stand among the cradles of their ancestors and remember they were cargo. I went past a stretch of blank wall. I went round a corner into a part of the spine I'd never seen, where the light panels stopped and my handset's little glow was the only light, and the stencils on the walls said things like BALLAST 3 and BALLAST ACCESS and HOLD AFT. I didn't stop until I couldn't hear anything behind me but my own breath.

Then I stopped, because I'd come to the end of a passage, and there was nowhere left to go.

It was a short dead-end corridor off the main shaft, three metres wide, with a great round iron hatch at the end of it. The hatch was dogged shut, its wheel rusted fast, and its rim had been welded all the way round with a thick dull bead of slag, very old. In the middle of it, on a riveted plate, in the Office's own lettering, were the words: CONDEMNED — BY ORDER OF THE OFFICE OF THE ACCOUNT. Below that, smaller: *Fourth Cistern. Closed CR 718. Contaminated.*

I remembered, distantly, the way you remember something from a schoolroom: a fever in the water, two hundred years ago. A whole cistern of the station's water gone bad. Hundreds dead in the Crown before they found it. They'd sealed the cistern and left it full, because draining it would have meant pumping the fever through the whole station. The Fourth Cistern had been sitting back here in the dark ever since, full of poison, waiting for nothing.

I hung there in front of it with my hand on the cold iron and my handset making a small pool of light on the plate, and I found that I was shaking. Not from the cold. From everything. From the vault, and Thorne's crystal eye, and Merriam touching her chain, and Bastian's face, and *her pen was found in your quarters*, and *protective custody*, and *there's been a fire*. From the fact that I'd hit a proctor-captain in the face and run, which no Assessor in the history of the Office had ever done, and that there was nowhere in the system I could go now where they wouldn't find me. From Wren. From Wren in the Crown, in some House ward's room with a window and a guard on the door, five days before her sixteenth birthday, being told her father was a murderer, being told she was safe.

I put my forehead against the hatch.

Behind it, through the iron, faintly, I could hear water. A slow, dull, irregular knocking — the water in the cistern moving, the way water moves in a tank in a station that never quite stops turning, sloshing very gently against the walls. A sound like something turning over in its sleep.

I don't know how long I stayed there. Long enough for my handset's glow to dim and go out. Long enough to start counting the knocks, out of habit, and lose count, and start again.

Then there was a light at the end of the passage. A small one, yellow, swinging. A lamp. And behind it a shape, coming slowly toward me along the dead-end corridor, hand over hand on the old Charter rails: a woman, short and heavy, in a rubber apron, with forearms like hams.

"You're a fool," said Mags Tiller, holding up her lamp to look at me. "Calla said you were. She said to bring you anyway." She looked at the hatch, at the plate, at me with my forehead on it. "Come on. Before they think to look back here. Nobody ever does, but there's a first time." She held out one big red hand. "And stop that. It's only water. It's been knocking like that for two hundred years."
