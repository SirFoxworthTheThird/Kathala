# Chapter 7: Air Debt

*The Crossing · CR 611*

Breathing was billable.

It had been billable all her life, so Annick had stopped noticing it, the way you stop noticing the hum of a ship. In the stacks of Callis there had been meters on the walls of every corridor, little brass dials with red needles that crept up through the day as the air went bad, and the Company billed each stack for its share of the scrubbing; and when she was very small her mother used to make a game of it, on the long nights when the needles were in the red, of seeing who could breathe the slowest. Four in. Hold. Four out. Annick had won every time. She had not understood until she was nine that it was not a game.

On the *Merciful Increase*, the meters were on the bonded. Each one wore a band on the left wrist, just above the number, with a sensor that counted every litre of air its wearer drew and sent the count to the Book, where it was multiplied by the voyage rate and added to that person's debt at the end of every shift. A big man working hard breathed perhaps four thousand litres a shift. A small woman sitting still breathed perhaps fifteen hundred. The difference, over a three-month Wake, was about two hundred marks. Over a lifetime of Wakes it was a great deal more. The bonded knew the figures better than Annick did.

So they rationed. Everybody did. You learned it in your first Wake or someone taught you: breathe shallow, move slowly, sleep as much as you can, because a sleeping body draws less. The coil gang breathed through their teeth. The cradle techs in the Hold, where the cold made you want to gasp, wrapped their faces in scarves and breathed through the wool. And every rotation, sooner or later, someone took it too far.

It was Ama Brook this time. A wiry, middle-aged woman from the galley crew, with grey in her braids, who collapsed at the slab vats at the end of the second watch on the fourteenth day after the courier and lay on the deck with her lips blue and her eyes rolled back, while the rest of the galley stood round her and nobody touched her, in case it counted against them somehow.

Annick was in the mess when it happened, eating, because she had found that if she ate in the mess the bonded talked in front of her, a little, and she was learning things. She got to Ama first. She did not know why. She knelt on the deck in the ring's light gravity and turned the woman on her side and held her jaw open, the way Haddad had shown the crew in a drill, and looked at the meter on her wrist. It read eleven hundred and four litres for the shift. Ama Brook had been at the vats for nine hours, stirring vats of protein paste with a paddle as long as she was, and she had breathed eleven hundred litres. She had been holding her breath.

"Get the doctor," said Annick, and nobody moved, and she looked up at the circle of faces round her and said, in the voice she used at the moment of the bill, "*Now*," and two of them went.

Haddad came. Ama Brook woke up in the infirmary an hour later with an oxygen mask on her face — free oxygen, medical, which by the Ordinances could not be billed — and looked at it with horror and tried to pull it off, and Haddad held her hands down gently and said something to her in a low voice until she stopped.

"They all do it," he said to Annick afterward, in the passage. "Every rotation. I see three or four a Wake. Mild hypoxia, headaches, fainting. Two years ago a man on the coil gang died of it. Not directly. He was lightheaded and he missed a handhold." He rubbed his face. "There's a line in the clerks' manual about it, did you know? *The voyage rate for air is fixed to encourage economy of respiration among the bonded.* Encourage economy. I've always admired that."

"I know the line."

"Of course you do." He looked at her with his tired, careful face. "You've been in the Hold a great deal lately, Purser."

She held his gaze. "I'm reconciling the cradle manifests."

"Yes," said Haddad. "That's what you told Sergeant Kolar too. She asked me about it." He hesitated. "I'm not asking you anything. I'd like you to know I'm not. But if I were reconciling cradle manifests at two in the morning, I'd want to know that the sergeant had noticed."

* * *

She had been giving them the rosters.

It was the first thing they had asked for, and it had been easy, which frightened her more than anything else about it. The purser's office kept the payroll. The payroll required the duty rosters. The duty rosters showed where every member of the crew was at every hour of every watch, including the twenty-four proctors of the Factor's guard, who were paid by the Charter like everyone else and whose posts and shifts and leave were recorded in the Book's annex alongside the bonded's air. Annick had only to open the annex and read. She did not copy anything. She did not write anything down. She read it, as she read everything, quickly, with her mouth shut, and then she went down to the Hold in the middle of the ship's night and told Dace, and Dace told whoever Dace told.

The second thing they had asked for was harder.

"The Lantern's locks," said Marit Osei. "How they work. Exactly."

They were in the hollow among the cradles, three tiers in, with the blue lamplight on their faces and the frost on the windows all round them. It was the fourth time Annick had come. It had begun to feel like a habit, which frightened her too.

"You know how they work. Article Fourteen."

"I know what the Ordinance says. I want to know what happens. When you seal a new Master. What do the locks do, and how long do they take, and what happens to the old Master's authority while they do it?"

So she told them. She told them that when the Purser sealed a change of Master in the Book, the Book sent the entry to the Lantern along a hard line that ran through the spine, and the Lantern's locks — the command ring, the coil controls, the navigational array, the weapons lockers on the bridge deck — dropped the old Master's codes and took the new one's, and that the whole process took about forty seconds, during which no one at all was Master and the ship would answer to nobody. She told them that the line could not be cut without the Lantern knowing. She told them that a Master's authority could be sealed only to a named person who was physically aboard, and that the Book would check the name against the crew manifest and refuse anyone not on it.

"Anyone not on the *crew* manifest," said Marit slowly.

"Yes."

"Not the bonded manifest."

"No. A Master has to be crew." Annick looked at Tobiah, who was floating a little way off with one great hand resting on the window of a cradle, as he often did, as if to keep its sleeper company. "That's why you need a friend on the bridge."

Tobiah didn't look round. "Yes," he said.

"Who is it?"

"Purser," said Marit gently, "you know we're not going to tell you that."

"Then how am I supposed to seal them?"

"When the time comes," said Tobiah, "you'll know." He turned then, and looked at her across the hollow with his broken, crooked face. "I'm sorry. I know it's hard. I know we're asking you to do a thing in the dark. But you understand why. If you know the name and someone asks you, it's in you to tell. If you don't know it, you can't."

She understood. She did not say so.

* * *

Proctor-Sergeant Vashti Kolar stopped her in the spine two nights later, outside the strongroom.

Annick had come down from the ring the long way, by the aft spoke, because she had begun to vary her routes; and she turned the corner by the Hold hatch and found the sergeant waiting there in the dim corridor in her grey armour, with her helmet under her arm and one boot hooked casually through a handhold, as if she had been there for some time. She was a tall, rawboned woman of about forty, with pale cropped hair and a face like a fist. She had commanded the Factor's proctors for nine years. Annick had entered the fines she levied. They were large.

"Purser," said Kolar.

"Sergeant."

"Late to be up."

"I'm going to the strongroom."

"I can see that." Kolar looked at the strongroom door, at the red letters over it, and then at the great double hatch of the Hold beside it. "You've been in the Hold a great deal."

"I'm reconciling the cradle manifests."

"At night."

"The cradle techs are on day shift. I can't hear myself think."

"No," said Kolar. "I expect not." She unhooked her boot and drifted a little closer, not quite threatening, the way a dog drifts closer. "You're ballast-born, aren't you, Purser? Callis stacks. I looked it up. Mother died in the Company's mines at Gethen. Sister in the Wake." She smiled, without anything behind it. "Must be strange. Keeping the Book on your own people."

"They're not my people. I'm crew."

"Yes," said Kolar. "That's what I'd say." She put her helmet on, and her voice came out of the speaker grille, tinny and flat. "Goodnight, Purser. Mind how you go in the Hold. It's very cold in there. People get lost."

* * *

The next time she went down, Tobiah was alone.

She had not expected that. She came in through the great hatch and up through the struts, three tiers in, and found the hollow among the cradles empty except for him, floating with his back to her and his hand on a window, and for a moment she thought she would turn round and leave. Then he said, without looking round, "Dace is on the coil. Marit's sleeping. I told them I'd wait for you."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted to talk to you without anyone listening." He turned. "Is that all right?"

She did not know if it was. She came the rest of the way into the hollow and caught a strut and held it.

He did not talk about the rising. She had expected him to; she had been ready for it, with every Ordinance she could think of. Instead he asked her about the Book — not what it said, but what it was. How the lattice grew. How long it lasted. Whether she had ever seen one destroyed, and what happened to a Book at the end of a voyage. She told him about the dead shelf, the four old Books strapped side by side with their lead seals, and about the law that said a Book could never be destroyed.

"Never?"

"Never. It can be closed. It can't be burned."

"What if a ship's taken? Pirates?"

"Then it goes in the ballast." She surprised herself by smiling. "Article Nine. There's a chute in the strongroom floor, a lead sleeve, a lever. If a ship's taken by anyone not lawfully in command, the purser drops the Book into Tank Four, and it sinks, and whoever took the ship can't write themselves into it."

He was staring at her. Then, slowly, he began to laugh.

It was a deep, soft laugh, like his voice, and it went on for some time, and he put his hand over his face while he did it. "*Down the sea*," he said.

"What?"

"The song. The coil song. *Heave and heave and we'll be free, when the Book goes down the sea.* You've heard it."

"I've heard it."

"I always thought it was just a rhyme. Something to sing. My father sang it in the quarry, and his father, and nobody I ever met knew what it meant." He took his hand away. His eyes were wet, from laughing or from something else. "It's spacers' talk, isn't it? From the old ships. *The Book's gone down the sea.* The ship's taken. The ship's ours."

"Yes," said Annick. "It's from the Ordinances. Nobody's used a drop in a hundred years. But the old crews used to say it. When a ship was lost, or mutinied. *Her Book's gone down the sea.*" She hesitated. "Benn told me. He said he'd heard old spacers say it on Callis when he was a boy."

"So the ship's ours when the Book's in the ballast."

"That's what it means."

He looked at her for a long time. Then he sang it — properly, in a low true voice that filled the hollow and went out among the cradles and the frost, all of it, the whole verse, the way the coil gang sang it scraping salt:

*Heave the iron, heave it slow,
the Book is heavy, down we go.
What is written, that is owed,
and the dark's a long cold road.
Heave and heave and we'll be free
when the Book goes down the sea.*

When he finished, the Hold was very quiet. Somewhere far off in the racks a cradle pump cycled, a soft sigh.

"My father died under a slab," said Tobiah. "In the quarry above Gethen town. I was nine. A block of granite as big as this hollow came off the face in the rain and he was under it, him and four others, and they didn't get them out for a week because the Company wouldn't stop the cutting. When they did, the foreman came to our house with a man from the Company's office, a clerk, with a slate and a little stylus, and the clerk read out what my father owed, and said it was ours now." He looked at her. "He had a voice exactly like yours. Very level. I hated him for twenty years. Then I met him again, in a bar in Callis, when I was thirty, and he was old and drunk and he didn't remember me, and I couldn't hate him any more. He was only the man who said the number."

Annick said nothing. There was nothing she could say.

"I don't hate you, Purser," said Tobiah. "I want you to know that. Whatever happens. I know what you are. I know what it costs."

"You don't know what it costs."

"No," he agreed. "I suppose I don't." He put his hand back on the cradle window beside him. "This is Lise Saal. Emeric's wife. Did you know? I come and sit with her sometimes. And the boys are just there, the next two." He nodded at the cradles above. "I thought someone should. Since nobody's told her."

She looked at the frosted window under his hand. Behind it, through the rime, was a woman's face — thin, young, with a straight nose and a mouth that turned down a little at the corners, and frost on her lashes. Annick had entered her name. She had never looked at her.

"What happens at Absalom?" she said.

He didn't answer at once.

"Quarry's right," she said. "That's what I can't get past. He's a liar and a thief and I think he'd sell his own mother at a discount, but he's right. There's nothing at Absalom. There's no colony office. There's no relief ship. There's no market for anything. If you take this ship to Absalom, you'll land four thousand people on a moon made of ice with what's in the holds and nothing else, and the nearest help is eleven jumps back the way we came, at a port that's on fire." She heard her voice beginning to rise, and stopped it. "How will you feed them? Who'll decide who works and who eats? Who'll keep the air running? What will you do when the first people start to die?"

"We'll work it out."

"That's not an answer."

"No," said Tobiah Ruck. "It's the only one anyone's ever had." He looked at her across the hollow, steadily. "You want a Book for it, don't you. A plan. Figures. Who owes what to whom, and when it's paid. I understand. That's how you're made. But there isn't one, Annick. There's just people, and the ice, and whatever we do next. That's what free means. It means nobody's written it yet."

It was the first time he had used her name. She noticed. She thought he noticed that she noticed.

"I have to go," she said.

"I know."

She went down through the struts toward the hatch. At the bottom she looked back, once. He was still floating in the hollow with his hand on Lise Saal's window, a great dark shape among the cradles in the blue light, and he was singing again, very softly, under his breath, to the sleeper behind the frost.

* * *

Haddad found her in the strongroom the next morning.

He came in without knocking, which he never did, and shut the iron door behind him, and stood with his back against it for a moment as though he expected someone to try it. He had the concentrator with him, still wrapped in its towel.

"I looked at it," he said.

She set down her stylus.

"There's a valve on the output side," said Haddad. "Between the reservoir and the line. It's a safety valve; it closes if the line's pulled out, so you don't waste oxygen into the room. It has a manual override, a little lever under the casing. You'd need a tool to reach it." He unwrapped the towel. The concentrator lay in his hands, small and grey and ordinary, with its green light dark now. "The override's been moved. Recently. There are scratches on the casing where someone got a blade under it. If you closed that valve while a man was asleep, the concentrator would go on running — the light would stay green, it would go on hissing, it would sound exactly the same — but nothing would come down the line. He'd be breathing ship's air through a tube. With his lungs, in his sleep, he'd be dead in an hour. And the reservoir would stay full." He looked up at her. "And then if you came back later and opened the valve again, it would go on hissing pure oxygen down the line into the nose of a dead man until somebody found him."

The strongroom was very quiet.

"Is that proof?" said Annick.

"No." Haddad wrapped the concentrator again, carefully, as if it were something alive. "Valves stick. Casings get scratched. He might have knocked it himself, getting into his bunk. A tribunal would laugh at me." He held the bundle against his chest. "There's a log on these. A service log — every time the casing's opened, it records the time and the code of whoever opened it. It's for the maintenance crews. I haven't been able to read it. It needs an engineer's reader, and I can't ask an engineer for one without telling them why."

"Can I read it?"

"Can the Book?"

She thought about it. "Not directly. But if you bring it to me with the right cable, I can open the annex and pull its log as a maintenance record. All ship's equipment reports to the annex eventually."

"Then I'll find a cable." He hesitated at the door. "Annick. If it's what I think it is, it was someone who could get into his cabin at night. Someone who knew about the valve. Someone who knew he'd refused."

"I know."

"That's not a long list."

"No," she said. "It isn't."

* * *

Edwin Quarry invited her to dinner that evening.

He did it properly: a card under her door, in his own elegant hand, *The Factor requests the pleasure of the Purser's company at the second bell*, as though they were in a hill house on Callis and not on a transport at the end of the world. She considered not going. She considered it for perhaps four seconds, which was how long it took her to understand that not going would tell him more than anything she could say at his table.

The Factor's cabin was the largest aboard after the captain's. He had made it beautiful. There was a carpet on the deck, a real one, Gethen wool in deep reds, and pictures on the bulkheads — little old oil paintings of the Callis hills in gilt frames — and a table laid with white linen and silver, and on the table, under covers, food she had not seen since Callis: smoked fish, preserved figs, a small round cheese with a black rind, a bottle of wine. In the light gravity near the hub the wine moved slowly in the glass, like Szell's whisky.

"Please," said Quarry. "Eat. I know what they give you in the mess."

She ate. She was hungry; it would have been stupid not to. Quarry talked. He talked well, lightly, about the hills above Callis and the house where he had been a boy, about a painter he admired, about the theatre in the port. He asked her nothing at all. He refilled her glass. It was only when the figs were gone and the cheese was half gone that he set down his own glass and looked at her across the table with his pleasant pricing eyes and said, "We're the same, you know, Purser."

"I don't think so, Factor."

"No? I was born in the Lowfield debtors' quarter at Callis. My father was a cooper. He owed the Charter for his shop, and his father owed it before him, and when I was eleven he defaulted and they took the shop and the tools and sold the debt on to a collector, and I went to work on the docks to pay my share." He smiled at her surprise. "I don't tell people. It's not interesting. But I tell you, because I think you'll understand it. I climbed out on the ledger. I learned the law. I learned that debt is the only honest relationship there is — everyone owes, Purser, everyone, the only question is whether it's written down — and once you understand that, you understand everything." He turned his glass. "You climbed out the same way. You learned the Book. You're the only person aboard this ship who reads it the way I do."

"And how is that?"

"As a fact," said Quarry. "Not a judgement. A fact. Like the weather. Like the coil." He leaned back. "Benn read it as a judgement. That was his trouble. He thought that because a thing was written, it had to be *just*. It doesn't. It only has to be written."

She set down her fork.

"The courier carried a second packet," said Quarry, as if he were changing the subject. "You saw me take it. You're wondering what was in it. I'll tell you: my commission. Two per cent of the Concern's price for the debts of the hold, payable at Shale on the Purser's seal." He spread his soft hands. "I'm not ashamed of it. Everyone aboard will be paid at Shale. The crew their wages and their bonus. The captain his pension. I my commission. It's written down."

"And the bonded?"

"Will be employed." He reached for the bottle and refilled her glass. "There's another thing I wanted to tell you. The coil's cooling faster than projected — something to do with the radiator fins, the First Officer says. She's very clever about these things. We'll be able to make the jump to the Fork in seventeen days, not twenty-one." He watched her face. "I'd like to see the assignment entry beforehand. Drafted. So we can go over the wording together. I want there to be no difficulty on the day."

"Seventeen days," said Annick.

"Seventeen days." He smiled. "Plenty of time."

The alarm went as he said it.

Not the whoop of an accident; something harder and higher, a stuttering two-tone that Annick had heard only in drills. *Security. Security.* And then the ship's voice, flat and urgent in every compartment: *Proctor detail to Ballast Two access. Proctor detail to Ballast Two access. Armed response.*

Quarry did not move. He sat at his white table with his glass in his hand and looked at her, and she understood, with a cold drop in her stomach, that he was not surprised.

"Excuse me, Factor," she said, and stood.

"Of course," said Quarry. "I expect you'll want to see."

She went. She went down the spoke to the spine so fast she hit the hatch rim with her shoulder and did not feel it, and along the main corridor past the strongroom and the Hold, aft, toward the long dim stretch where the access hatches for the ballast tanks opened off the spine — Tank One, Tank Two — and she turned the last corner and the corridor was full of grey.

There were eight proctors. Their visors were down and their flechette carbines were up, and the access hatch to the crawlway behind Tank Two was standing open, its cover plate bent back, and they were pulling things out of it. Lengths of pipe. Bundles wrapped in sacking. Cutting tools from the coil gang's lockers, their safeties filed off. A crude gun, a thing made of tubing and a gas bottle and a firing pin, which one of the proctors held up for the others to see, and somebody laughed. And against the far bulkhead, with their hands on their heads and stingers held to their necks, four bonded in dun.

One of them was the young man with the knife scar on his chin. He was bleeding from the nose and smiling, very slightly, the way some people smile when they are terrified.

The one beside him was Dace.

Vashti Kolar turned from the hatch and saw Annick and lifted her visor. Her face like a fist did not change. "Purser," she said. "Good. You'll want to take their numbers."
