# Chapter 2: Liberty Owes Liberty

*The Account · CR 929*

I struck Ewan Haskell to the Deep Ice twelve days before the Burning, for two hundred and twelve marks.

I'd like to tell you I remember it because of everything that came after. I don't. I remember it because I remember all of them. I've been an Assessor of the Office of the Account for twenty years, and in that time I've struck three hundred and eight people to the ice, and I can give you every name and every figure and most of the faces. It's the one gift the Office ever gave me that I didn't have to pay back. A good memory for numbers. They saw it in me when I was twelve and they lifted me up out of Gallows Reach by it, like a fish on a line, and I've been hanging there ever since, looking down.

Gallows Reach is the oldest settlement on Tamar. You won't find that in the Houses' histories, which begin at the Hulk and work outward, but it's true. The first people the Founders sent down to the ice were sent here — the first Struck, the first defaulters, whoever they were — and they dug the first galleries with hand tools into the face of a glacier eight hundred metres thick, and their children dug the next ones, and so on for three hundred years. The town goes down into the ice like a set of stairs. At the top, near the ferry port, the galleries are wide and lit and there's a Clear quarter with real windows looking out onto the black sky and the great rust-coloured face of Absalom hanging over the horizon. At the bottom, near the mine heads, the galleries are just big enough for a man to stand in, and there's no sky at all, and the walls sweat.

The Haskells lived near the bottom. Eleven hundred metres down, gallery forty, third door on the left past the tea shop that wasn't a tea shop. I'd been there twice before that year. I knew the way.

You learn to count as an Assessor, if you didn't already know how. Not the portions — anyone can count portions, the tally does it for you — but the other things. How many steps from the lift to the door. How many people in the room when you arrive and how many when you leave. How long a man takes to answer when you tell him the figure. How many times his wife looks at the door to the back room, which is where the son is. I counted all of those at the Haskells'. It was something to do with the part of me that might otherwise have done something else.

Piet Haskell was forty-six and looked sixty. He'd worked the refinery belts for thirty years, and the belts had taken his hearing and most of his left hand, and he paid his household's Liberty portion every quarter in labour marks from the belt and his wife's piecework, and for the last three quarters he'd been short. Two hundred and twelve marks short this time. Not much. About a month's wages for a Clear clerk in Saltmarch. Not much, except that it was the third time, and the Account doesn't care how much.

"I'll have it by the Burning," he said. "There's overtime at the Burning. Everyone knows there's overtime."

"There's overtime," I agreed.

"So I'll have it."

"You'll have some of it. You'll have about ninety marks of it, if the belt holds and you don't miss a shift. I've seen the overtime figures." I had them on the tally. I didn't show him. "And it doesn't matter, Mr Haskell. You know it doesn't. The quarter closed yesterday. Three short quarters and the portion's in default, and when a portion's in default the surety is struck for the shortfall. That's the law."

"He's seventeen."

"I know how old he is."

The schoolroom was next door. I could hear it through the ice — a thin, high chorus of children, perhaps twenty of them, reciting the Catechism of Mercy the way I'd recited it at their age in a schoolroom two galleries up, in the same singsong, with the same little hitch before the hard answers.

*Who carried us?*
*The Increase carried us.*
*Who would have sold us?*
*The Purser would have sold us.*
*What was her name?*
*Her name was Annick Vell, and it is written in ash.*
*Who saved us?*
*The Founders saved us.*
*What did they burn?*
*They burned the Book.*
*What do we owe?*
*We owe our Liberty.*
*To whom?*
*To those who paid for it.*

We learned it before we learned to count, which was the point. Every child on Tamar can tell you the name of the Purser who sold her own people. No child on Tamar can tell you the name of a single Founder, because the Founders are not named. That's piety, the Houses say. A Founder's name belongs to God and the Book, and the Book is ash. Ottilie used to say it was also very convenient, but Ottilie said a lot of things.

"There's a review," said Haskell's wife. She was a small woman with white burn marks on her forearms from the piecework, the kind you get from salt-etch. She hadn't sat down since I came in. "Before a strike. There's a review. Someone told me."

"There's an appeal to the Chief Assessor," I said. "You can lodge it. It takes six months, and the surety serves while it's heard, and it's never once been granted in my time." I made myself look at her. "I'm sorry, Mrs Haskell."

That was a lie, or not quite a lie. I'm always sorry. It doesn't cost anything and it doesn't change anything, and I said it the way I said the figure, in the same tone, because the Office trains you to keep your voice level at the moment of the bill. People can stand almost anything if you say it to them in a level voice. It's when your voice goes that they start to think there might be some other way.

The son came out of the back room on his own. I've always been grateful to the ones who do that. He was big for seventeen, a refinery boy, with his father's shoulders and his mother's pointed chin and a faint grey smudge on the inside of his left wrist where last year's Burning ash hadn't quite washed out. They mark you there on Burning Day, over the place where the old numbers used to go. Liberty's mark, the Houses call it. In Gallows Reach they call it something else.

"How long?" he said.

"Two hundred and twelve marks at the Deep Ice rate. Fourteen months."

He nodded. He'd known. They always know; there's nothing in Gallows Reach anybody doesn't know about the Deep Ice. Fourteen months in the penal mines at the bottom of the world, cutting volatiles out of the ice with a thermal lance in a cold so deep it gets into the bone. People come back from fourteen months. Most of them.

I took out my tally and held it to his wrist, and the tally read the chip under the skin and gave me his name and his portion and the figure, and I spoke the strike. *Ewan Haskell, surety of the Haskell portion, Gallows Reach forty, struck to the Deep Ice for the term of fourteen months in satisfaction of two hundred and twelve marks.* I said it level. The tally took it and sent it up to the Office on the Hulk, where it would be written into the Account in the presence of a Keeper and become a fact about the world, as permanent as the ice.

There was a long chain round my neck, under my shirt. I could feel it as I bent over the boy's wrist. On the end of it hung an iron key that had been my mother's, and her mother's, and so on back into the ice for longer than anyone remembered: a long, old-fashioned key the length of my palm, with a notched bit and a dull bead of something set into the bow. It didn't open anything. Every ice family in Gallows Reach has some piece of junk like it, handed down — a spoon, a ring, a button off a coat nobody living has seen. Ours was the key. My mother put it round my neck the day the Office lifted me, at the ferry port, with the Assessor who'd come for me standing a polite distance off, and she said, *Don't sell it.* That was all. Not *remember us*. Not *come back*. *Don't sell it.* I've never known whether she meant it as a joke.

I always feel it when I strike someone. I don't know why. It swings forward on its chain and knocks against my breastbone, about where you'd put your hand if you were swearing an oath.

* * *

The call came as I was leaving the gallery. It came on my Office handset with a priority tone I'd heard perhaps three times in my career, and it was Lucan Marsh himself, the Chief Assessor, which I'd never heard at all.

"Hollis." His voice was strange. "Where are you?"

"Gallows Reach. I've just closed the Haskell default."

"Leave it. Take the next Office ferry. Come straight to the Index." A pause, and then, in a voice I'd known for twenty-nine years and had never heard do what it did then: "It's Ottilie."

I took the next Office ferry.

From Tamar you go up to the Hulk by ferry from the Saltmarch ice port, a forty-minute burn in a squat armoured hopper with forty seats and no windows, and if you're an Assessor you go up in the forward cabin with the House stewards and the Clear clerks and you don't look at the ballast in the back. I looked at nothing. I sat with my hands on my knees and counted the burn — forty-one minutes, a minute long — and thought about nothing at all, very carefully, until we docked.

Increase Station rides at the inner Lagrange point between Tamar and Absalom, where the pull of the moon and the pull of the giant balance, held there by its station-keeping engines like a stone balanced on a blade. At the centre of it is the Hulk. Everything else — the docks, the radiator vanes, the spoke lifts, the great slow-turning ring of the Crown where the Houses live — was built onto her over three centuries, but she's still there at the heart of it, if you know how to look: a long black seed-shaped spine eleven hundred metres from bow to stern, scarred and patched and studded with later work, but with her old Charter lines still showing under it. At her bow is the dome they used to call the Lantern, where her captains stood. The Houses use it as a chapel now. At her stern, where the fold coil once was, there's a cluster of foundries. And down the length of her spine, in the zero gravity where the Founders lived when they first came, is the Office of the Account.

You move through the Office hand over hand. The handholds are three hundred years old, brass, worn into shallow saddles by the palms of generations of Keepers and Assessors and clerks, and the walls are lined with the old Charter fittings — conduits, hatch frames, the faded red and yellow hazard stripes of a ship that once carried thousands of people asleep across the dark. The Founders kept as much of her as they could. Out of reverence, the Houses say. The Office of the Account occupies her old purser's deck and the stores around it, and the Index occupies the long gallery where her crew once kept spares, and the Account itself — the master lattice, the great grown block of crystal in which every portion on Tamar is written — sits in her old strongroom behind a door of black iron, under four words of red paint that have been touched up so many times the letters have gone soft and swollen, like the knuckles of an old man.

WHAT IS WRITTEN IS OWED.

I touched the doorframe as I went past, as everyone does. Then I went down the gallery to the Index, and there were Office proctors at the hatch in their black coats, and Bastian Oduya was waiting for me with a face the colour of cheese.

"Assessor Fen. Sir. The Chief's with her. He said to bring you straight—"

"I know what he said."

The Index is the Office's memory. The Account is the law — every portion, every payment, every strike and Clearance, grown into the master lattice under seal — but nobody can read lattice fast, so for three hundred years the Keepers have maintained a parallel record on film: index cards, millions of them, in steel cabinets that line the gallery floor to ceiling on both sides and in rows down the middle, with narrow aisles between, so that walking the Index in zero gravity is like swimming down a canyon of drawers. Each card gives a portion's number, its family, its holder, its standing, and the coordinates in the lattice where the full entry lives. The living accounts are near the hatch. The Clear accounts are further in. Right at the end, in the old cabinets nobody opens, are the dead accounts — portions of families that died out or were extinguished, closed and cross-referenced and left.

Ottilie Brask was in aisle nine, among the Clearances. Someone had tied her to a cabinet handle by the belt of her coat, so that she wouldn't drift, and she hung there with her arms floating up a little in front of her and her grey hair spread out around her face like weed in water, and her head was at an angle no living head can make.

I'd known her for twenty-nine years. She'd been Keeper of the Index for twenty-two of them. When I was twelve, a ballast boy from the bottom of Gallows Reach with an Office scholarship and a number for a name, she'd been the one who taught me to read lattice — not the Account, which no junior was allowed to touch, but the training blocks, old Charter crystals with nothing on them but practice entries. *You read it like the rings of a tree*, she'd told me. *Every entry grows a layer. Look at the layer and you can see when it was written. Look at how thick it is, how even. You can tell if the purser's hand was steady. You can tell if they were tired. You can tell, sometimes, if they were lying.* She'd been fifty-four then, with ink on her fingers; she still wrote her notes on paper, which nobody did, and her fingers were always stained a little blue at the tips. I'd thought she was the cleverest person in the world. I'd thought that until I was about twenty, and then I'd stopped thinking it, and then at about thirty-five I'd started thinking it again.

On the cabinet face beside her, someone had sprayed three words in red paint.

WE WERE CARGO.

"The Hold," said Bastian behind me. He said it the way people said it in the Crown, as if it were a disease. "They've been painting it all over the Saltmarch docks. Sir, the proctors think—"

"I know what the proctors think."

Lucan Marsh was on the far side of her, holding a cabinet with one hand. He was sixty-eight, tall and stooping, with a long clever face and soft white hair, and he wore the Chief Assessor's chain over his grey coat — a heavy thing of brass links with the Office's scales on a medallion — as if it were a weight he'd been asked to carry for someone else. He'd lifted me out of Gallows Reach. He'd found me at twelve doing sums in the dirt outside the schoolroom, sums the teacher had set for the seniors, and he'd crouched down beside me in his good coat and checked them, and then he'd asked me my name. I'd told him my number. *No*, he'd said, *your name*, and I'd told him, and he'd said, *Well, Hollis Fen, how would you like to come up and see the stars?* For twenty-nine years he'd been the nearest thing to a father I'd had. I'd hated him for some of them and loved him for the rest, and I'd never once in all that time seen him look the way he looked over Ottilie's body.

"Hollis," he said. "Thank God."

"When?"

"Last night. The cleaners found her an hour into the morning watch. Doctor Vasquez thinks between twenty-one and midnight." He looked at the paint. "There's an Office ferry log entry. Two unregistered passengers from Saltmarch, yesterday evening, on the twenty o'clock. Ballast tickets. They didn't take the return."

"That's quick work."

"The proctors are very quick when an officer of the Office is murdered." His voice cracked. He steadied it. "I want you on it. Not the proctors. You. I want whoever did this found and struck and I want it done before the Burning. The Concordance verifier arrives in two days, Hollis, and the Speaker is—" He stopped, and something passed over his face, a weariness or a distaste. "The Speaker would prefer that the Concordance not arrive to find the Office of the Account in mourning for a Keeper murdered by the Hold. You understand."

"I understand."

"You're the best I have. You knew her." He put his free hand on my shoulder, briefly, the way he had at the ferry port when I was twelve. "Find them."

He went. Bastian hovered.

I looked at Ottilie for a long time. Then I started counting.

The paint was Charter script. That was the first thing. The Hold has been painting *WE WERE CARGO* on walls across Tamar for six years, ever since the first Concordance survey ship came out of the Long Lane and the Houses began talking about integration, and they paint it in ice-script — the blocky, run-together letters miners use, that you can cut into a gallery wall with a lance tip in four strokes. Every proctor in Saltmarch knows that. This was neat, upright Charter capitals, the kind they teach you in the Office's schoolroom. The kind someone would write if they'd seen the slogan on a newsfeed and never on a wall.

The paint had been sprayed over her outflung left hand. There was red on her knuckles. But there were no drips under them on the cabinet face, and in zero gravity paint doesn't drip, it beads and drifts — and there were no beads in the air of aisle nine, not one, which meant the paint had dried long enough before anyone found her that the air scrubbers had cleaned out the overspray. Fine. That fitted. What didn't fit was that there were tiny beads of red, dried hard, caught in the weave of her coat on the *other* side of her body — the side against the cabinet. She'd been turned round after she was painted. Or painted first and tied up after.

Her fingers were clean.

I looked at them twice to be sure. Ottilie Brask wrote on paper. She'd written on paper every working day of her life, with a steel-nibbed pen she refilled from a bottle of blue-black ink she'd had sent up from a craftsman in Saltmarch, and in twenty-nine years I had never seen the tips of her right fingers without that faint blue stain on them. They were pink and clean as a child's. She'd scrubbed them. You scrub your fingers before you put on gloves, if you're careful about the gloves, if you're going to handle something you don't want to mark.

And her spectacles were folded in the breast pocket of her coat. Not on her face. Not on their cord. Folded, the way you fold them when you're not going to read anything for a while. When you're going to talk to someone instead.

"Sir?" said Bastian.

"Get me the ferry manifest," I said. "The real one, not the proctors' summary. And the Index access log for the last three days. And find out who cleans aisle nine."

"Yes, sir. Sir — do you think it's the Hold?"

He was twenty-four. He was House-client stock, the son of a steward in House Temperance, and he'd been my junior for a year, and he was eager in the way the Crown makes its children eager — to be useful, to be seen being useful, to rise. I liked him well enough. He made good tea.

"I think," I said, "someone wants me to think so."

* * *

They'd put her in the Office's little medical bay, in a cold drawer, and after the proctors and the doctor and the photographers had all done what they did, I asked to be left alone with her for ten minutes, and because I was an Assessor of the Second Rank and Lucan Marsh's man, they let me.

I don't know what I meant to do. Say something, perhaps. People do. I'd watched them do it, in the galleries of Gallows Reach, over bodies that had come back from the Deep Ice wrapped in sacking: they'd lean over and say things in low voices, things they'd been meaning to say, and I'd stand in the doorway with my tally and wait to record the transfer. I'd always wondered what they said. I'd never thought to ask.

I pulled the drawer out and looked at her and couldn't think of anything.

She'd been dressed for the drawer in a plain grey shift, and her own clothes were in a bag clipped to the end of it. Her shoes were in the bag too. They were good Saltmarch shoes, soft leather, with a little heel she'd always said was her one vanity, and the left one was slightly heavier than the right.

I wouldn't have noticed if I hadn't been holding them. I'm not sure I'd have noticed then if I hadn't spent twenty years lifting things in other people's houses and wondering what was under them. I turned the shoe over. The heel had been hollowed out, very neatly, and plugged with a disc of leather that had been glued and then trimmed flush, and when I worked the disc out with the tip of my pocket knife a card fell into my palm.

An Index card. Standard film, standard size, folded twice. Not a printed card — a blank, of the kind Keepers keep for cross-references. On it, in Ottilie's handwriting, in blue-black ink, was written:

*D-0447*

Nothing else.

D for the dead accounts. Four-four-seven. A portion number. Somewhere at the end of the Index, in the old cabinets nobody opens, there was a closed account of a family long extinct, and that was its number, and Ottilie Brask had scrubbed her fingers and folded her spectacles and gone to meet someone in the middle of the night with that number hidden in the heel of her shoe.

I turned the card over. Nothing on the back.

I sat there for a while holding it. The number tickled at something. I couldn't think what. I've a memory for numbers, as I said — I remember three hundred and eight strikes, and every portion I've ever closed, and the overtime rates at the refinery belts — and this one sat at the edge of my mind like a word you can't quite reach, familiar the way a face on a ferry is familiar, someone you might have stood next to once. I turned it over and over. *Four-four-seven*. It wouldn't come.

My handset chimed. Not the Office tone; the personal one.

I nearly didn't answer. Then I saw the name and answered it at once, the way I always did, in case it was the one time it was bad.

"Da?"

"Hello, love."

"Are you on the ice? Mam says you were on the ice today. She says there was a default."

"I'm on the Hulk." I closed the drawer, quietly, so she wouldn't hear it. "I had to come up. Work."

"Oh." Wren was fifteen. She lived with her mother in Saltmarch, in a Clear-quarter flat I paid half the rent on, and she had a voice that still went up at the ends of things like a much younger child's when she was worried, and down like a much older woman's when she was angry, and right now it was doing the first. "I just wanted to ask. About the — you know."

"I know."

"Because it's eleven days to my birthday. And it's twelve days to the Burning, and Mam says if it's not done by the Burning it has to wait till after the new quarter, because of the holiday, and that's four months."

"I know, love."

"How much is it now?"

"Four hundred," I said. "Four hundred and six, if you want to be exact."

"Four hundred and six," Wren said. She said it the way you'd say the height of a mountain. Her mother's family had Cleared two generations back; Imke was born Clear and had never thought about it in her life. I wasn't. I'd been born with a portion, like everyone in Gallows Reach, and I'd paid it off myself by twenty-eight on an Assessor's wages, and I'd paid half Wren's since she was born, because a child of one Clear parent and one Cleared parent still inherits the Cleared parent's share of the founding Liberty. That's the law. It's very fair. Everyone owes for their own freedom; it's only right. And for fifteen years, every quarter, I'd paid double into Wren's portion, so that she would be Clear on her sixteenth birthday and never have to stand in a gallery with her son in the back room and listen to someone like me say a figure in a level voice. Four hundred and six marks. Two months' wages, if I didn't eat.

"Will it be done?" she said. "By my birthday? Da?"

I looked at the closed drawer, and at the card in my hand, and at the little steel bag with Ottilie's good shoes in it, one heavier than the other.

"It'll be done," I said. "I promise."

I didn't know yet that it was the first promise I'd make that month that I wouldn't be able to keep. I'd find out about the others in their turn. That's the thing about a ledger. You can't read the bottom line until you've written every entry above it, and by then, of course, it's far too late to change any of them.
