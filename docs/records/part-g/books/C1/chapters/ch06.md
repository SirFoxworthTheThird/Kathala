# Chapter 6: The Ash Case

*The Salvage · CR 1317*

The Ash Church is cut from the ice itself.

It hangs from the underside of the shelf at the top of Saltmarch, in the oldest part of the city, where the galleries are narrow and the lamps are oil and the walls sweat. You go in through a low door under a carved lintel and down nine steps, and then the ceiling lifts away from you all at once into a great blue vault, thirty metres high, and you are standing inside a bubble of ice so old and so pure that the lamplight goes into the walls and doesn't come out. Everything glows. The pillars glow, and the pews, and the floor under your boots, all the same deep cold impossible blue, like being inside an eye.

Yara hasn't been here since her mother's ash. She remembers the blue. She'd forgotten the cold.

There are perhaps forty people in the pews this morning, more than she expects. Old women, mostly, in diver's wool, and a few men, and a scatter of children kneeling on the ice with their parents' hands on their shoulders. They aren't praying, exactly. They're waiting. At the front of the church, on a low altar of ice, under a column of clear glass, is the Ash Case, and one by one the people in the pews get up and walk to it and stand in front of it for a moment and then dip a finger in a bowl of grey ash at its foot and touch the ash to the inside of their left wrist.

Yara watches them do it. Then, because she's here, she goes up too.

It's smaller than she remembered. A box the size of a big book, iron, black and blistered, sitting on a cushion of grey felt. Whatever was once on its cover — there's the ghost of a pattern, a circle with lines coming out of it — has melted and run and set again in drips, like candle wax. Its hasp is fused into a lump. There's a crack along one side where the iron split in the heat, and through the crack, if you lean close to the glass, you can see darkness and a glint of something underneath, like burnt sugar. That's all. A burnt box. People have walked across the ice for three hundred years to look at it.

*They burned the Book*, says the brass plate on the column. *And the ash was our freedom.*

"You're Hana Tull's girl."

Yara turns. There's an old woman at her elbow, in the grey robe of the Ash with a diver's harness belt cinched over it, of all things — a short woman, broad and bent, with white hair cropped close, one eye milky with cataract and the other sharp and pale as a gull's. Her hands are huge, knotted, scarred across the knuckles in the way only shell gauntlets scar you. Yara knows her by sight. Everyone in the Gutter knows her by sight.

"Mother Quell," she says.

"I gave your mother her ash." Quell looks at her with the good eye. "You were sixteen. You didn't cry. Your brother cried enough for both of you." She nods at the bowl. "Are you going to take it?"

"I don't—"

"Nobody does, these days, until they need to." The old woman dips her own thumb in the ash and, before Yara can move, takes Yara's left wrist in one enormous hand and turns it over and presses the thumb to the inside of it, where the skin is thin and the blue veins show. It's cold. It leaves a grey smudge. "There," says Quell. "Liberty's mark. Now you've paid. What do you want?"

* * *

Quell's room is behind the altar, down a short passage cut through the ice — a cell three metres square with a cot and a stove and a table covered in papers, and on the wall, pinned up with ice-nails, a drawing of the Hulk.

It's an old drawing. Hand-done in pencil on brown paper, gone soft at the folds, with notes in a neat cramped hand all over it — depths, temperatures, *ICE HERE*, *CLEAR TO HERE 1281*, *DON'T*. It shows the spine in section, deck by deck, from the Lantern to the stern, the way Sabeth Orrin's film plan showed it, but in a great deal more detail. Yara stands looking at it while Quell makes tea on the stove.

"You did that," she says.

"Forty years ago. When I was a diver and the Hulk was a block of ice and we went in with lances and came out with chilblains." Quell pours. "Sit down. You're making the room nervous."

Yara sits. The tea is bitter and salt, the old Gutter way. She holds the cup and tries to think how to begin.

"You want Fen," says Quell.

Yara nearly spills it.

"Oh, don't look at me like that. Everyone in the Gutter knows a Vey woman with good boots came to your can three nights ago, and everyone in the Gutter knows what Vey women with good boots want with divers this month. And you come in here on the first morning after, smelling of the Hulk, and look at the Ash Case like you've never seen it, and then look at my wall." The old woman sits down across from her. "So. Fen."

"I want the stories," says Yara. "That's all. What people say."

"What people say." Quell sips her tea. "All right. I'll tell you what the Church says, and what the songs say, and what the divers say, and you can work out for yourself which of them is lying. Most likely all three."

She tells it the way Yara has heard it a hundred times, at the noodle cart and the bars and her mother's knee, but she tells it better — slowly, in a low, level, practised voice, the voice of someone who has told it from an altar for thirty years.

"In the Crossing," says Quell, "our people were carried in the dark. They lay in the cold like the dead, and they woke to work, and they owed for every breath they took. The Purser kept the Book of what they owed, and she would have sold them. That's the first thing. The Purser who would have sold us." She sets down her cup. "But at Landfall, before all the people, the Book was burned. Thrown in the fire, and the debts went up in the smoke, and what was left was ash. And the ash was our freedom. That's what the Church says. That's what the Case is."

"And the Houses?"

"The Houses said the fire was theirs." Quell's mouth thins. "They said they'd lit it, and we owed them for it. Liberty, they called it. The Liberty Debt. Every child born owing for the freedom of the ones before. They wrote it in a new book of their own, the Account, and kept it in the Hulk, and lived up there in their ring with their gardens while we cut the ice to pay them." She shrugs. "For three hundred years. Then came the Night of the Hulk, and then the Dimming, and then they were gone, and nobody cared about the Account any more, because there was nobody left to collect it. Until this week."

"And Fen."

"And Fen." Quell looks at her for a moment with the good eye. Then she sings — not well; her voice is old and cracked, but she carries the tune — the verse every child in the Gutter knows, the one they sing skipping rope on the concourse:

*Fen was an Assessor, grey was his coat,
he counted the Hold and he cut every throat;
he went out on the tide when the Concordance came
with the Hold's blood on his hands and the Houses' gold to his name.*

"That's the song," says Quell. "Fen the Turncoat. Assessor of the Office of the Account, on the Night of the Hulk. Sold the Hold to the Houses, or the Houses to the Concordance, or both to each other, depending which verse you sing. Ran off with the Concordance ships and lived like a lord on some world with three suns." She picks up her cup again. "Songs lie in rhyme, girl. It's easier to remember that way."

"What do the divers say?"

"The divers say," says Quell, "that Fen's still in there. Counting." She smiles slightly. "You've heard it. Every time the Hulk groans. I said it myself, forty years ago, every dive. And there's another one, older. The Purser's ghost. A woman in a grey coat, with a lamp, going through the spine. Looking for her Book. I never saw her. I knew a man who said he did. He was drunk at the time and he drowned the next spring." She drinks. "And there's Fen's cache, which you know about, or you wouldn't be here."

"Did anyone ever look for it?"

"Everyone looked for it. For two hundred years every diver who went into the Hulk was looking for Fen's cache, or the Houses' gold, or the Speaker's jewels, or whatever they'd heard about in a bar that week." Quell gets up, stiffly, and goes to the drawing on the wall and puts one huge knotted finger on it, near the middle of the spine. "When I dived, the Office was all ice. Solid. We lanced our way into the upper decks — here, and here — and found nothing but film and paper and the Assessors' lockers. And this." Her finger moves down to a square shape drawn in heavy pencil, deep in the spine, right beside a long chamber labelled *OLD HOLD*. "The Office vault. The strongroom, the old Charter people called it. Iron door, a metre thick. We cut at it for two windows, five of us, with lances, and got about a thumb's depth in. Then the ice shifted and took one of us, and we gave up." She looks at Yara over her shoulder. "Nobody's been into the vault since the Night of the Hulk. Not in the ice, and not since the thaw. If there's a cache, that's where I'd look."

Yara stands up and goes and looks at the drawing close. Under the square, in Quell's cramped hand, someone forty years ago has written *VAULT — DOOR STILL SOUND — DON'T BOTHER*. And beside it, between the vault and the long shape of the Old Hold, there's another, smaller shape, round, with a line through it. *BALLAST 4*, it says. And under that, in a different pencil, darker, as if it were added later: *WIDOW*.

"The Widow was sealed when you dived," she says.

"The Widow's always been sealed. Office sealed her, long before the Night. Some sickness in the water, they said, back in the Houses' time." Quell goes back to her cot and sits. "Leave the Widow alone. Gorse was right about that, for once in his greasy life. She killed two good divers last spring and she'll kill more."

On the way out, Yara stops in the passage behind the altar, where a narrow stair cut into the ice goes up toward the shelf. There's a hum coming down it, faint and steady, and a smell of hot metal.

"The bell tower," says Quell behind her. "Do you want to see?"

The tower is a shaft bored up through the ice shelf to the surface, sixty metres, with a spiral stair around its wall and, at the top, under a dome of thick glass, a machine. It's old, and patched, and bigger than Yara expected — a squat steel cabinet the size of a ferry seat, studded with dials and switches, cabled up through the dome to an array of slender rods and dishes standing on the ice outside, black against the stars and the great rust face of Absalom. A clockwork thing on top of the cabinet ticks toward the hour.

"Charter-band array," says Quell. "Came out of the Hulk two hundred years ago, in the first years of the Dimming. It was the old Office's — they used it to talk to their seal-readers, so the story goes, and to the Concordance when there was a Concordance. Some priest with more sense than faith dragged it out and brought it down here and hooked a chime to it." She pats the cabinet like a horse. "Every radio in the system hears it. Seven notes, every hour, for two hundred years. It's the only thing on Tamar still talking on the old band."

"Does it only send?"

Quell looks at her oddly. "It sends what you give it. It hears what's sent to it, if anything ever were." She shrugs. "Nothing ever is."

The clockwork clicks. Below them, through the cabinet, something heavy engages; and from the array on the ice the chime goes out — seven falling notes, on the old band, to every receiver in the system. Yara feels it in her teeth. And at her hip, on her harness, where she's brought it without quite deciding to, the brass seal-reader from the drowned annex gives a single soft click.

Quell's good eye goes to it at once.

"Where did you get that?" she says.

* * *

Mitya is waiting for her on the ferry dock when she comes back up to Ringfall.

He's leaning against a bollard with his hands in the pockets of his diver's jacket, looking at nothing, and he doesn't straighten up when he sees her, just watches her come along the dock with the expression he always has when he's about to ask for something: half rueful, half amused, as though the two of them were sharing a joke about him.

"You went to Quell," he says.

"Who told you?"

"The whole Gutter told me. You were seen. Hana Tull's girl, taking ash." He takes her left wrist and turns it over and looks at the grey smudge on it, and something in his face goes still for a moment. Then he lets go. "You're doing Fen's cache."

"I'm doing copper."

"You're doing Fen's cache for the Vey woman in the good boots. Fifty thousand marks, Gorse's boys are saying. Gorse thinks it's copper. Gorse is an idiot." He looks at her. "You can't do the spine alone, Yara. Nobody can. You need someone on your line."

"I'll find someone."

"You've found someone." He spreads his hands. "Me. I'm someone. I'm the best line you've ever had and you know it."

She does know it. That's the trouble. Dmitri Saar has dived with her for six years, and for three of those years they slept in the same bunk, and she's never in all that time seen him lose his head in the water. Out of it, he loses it every week. He's Saltmarch Clear-quarter stock, fallen; his grandmother had a flat with a window and his mother had a flat without one and he has a can in the Gutter and a book with Gorse that's longer than his arm. He gambles. He doesn't drink, which is somehow worse, because it means he's sober when he does it. He's lost their dive money twice, once on a fight and once on a ferry race, and the second time it was money she'd put aside for Pim's clinic, and she'd moved her things out of his can that night and hasn't been back.

"A third," she says.

"A third's fine."

"I mean it. A third, and you don't gamble any of it until Pim's on the ferry to Vey."

"A third, and I don't gamble any of it until Pim's on the ferry to Vey." He holds up one hand, palm out, the way children swear on the concourse. "On your mother's ash."

"Don't."

"On mine, then." He grins. "When do we go?"

* * *

They go in at the front of the next window, when the Hulk is still glowing.

Six passes now. Yara can see it from the sled. The last perigee has taken more of the Crown — a whole section of the old ring hangs loose by a few twisted spars, swinging slowly as the Hulk tumbles, and the leading face is darker and glassier than before, and the plumes of vapour coming off her trailing side are thicker and whiter and don't stop. The vibration coming up through the sled clamps is a constant shudder now, not a breathing. It's like holding on to a dying animal.

"She's loud," says Mitya.

"Shut up."

They go in through the Mouth and down the ring to the nearest spoke and through it, hand over hand, into the spine. Sabeth's film plan is clipped to Yara's forearm; Quell's drawing is in her head. The spine route is longer than the ring, but it's open, mostly — the slosh hasn't reached here — and the water in the great square corridors is clear and still and so cold she can feel it through the shell like a hand on her back. Their lamps go a long way. The hazard stripes on the walls flicker past, red and yellow, red and yellow, faded almost to grey. HOLD ACCESS. PURSER'S DECK. The stencils are three hundred years older than the House names in the ring, and they look newer: plain Charter letters that nobody bothered to carve.

The Old Hold is a chamber so big their lamps can't find its far end.

Yara has heard about it all her life, and she still isn't ready. It runs down the middle of the spine for two hundred metres, sixty across, and it is full of water and full of cradles. They rise from every surface on a lattice of black struts — tiers and tiers of them, above, below, on every side, receding into a darkness the lamps can't reach — and every one is a white shell the shape of a coffin, with an empty window where a face once was, and its lid standing open. Three thousand of them. Four. The water moves among them very slowly, and the open lids move with it, all together, a few degrees one way and a few degrees back, like the gills of something breathing. In the Houses' time the Old Hold was a memorial, Pim told her once — a place you came on Burning Day to stand among the empty cradles and remember that your ancestors had been carried here in them like freight. There are names scratched on some of the shells. Hundreds of names, in every kind of hand, some of them so old they've worn nearly smooth. People came here for three hundred years and wrote their names on the cradles of the dead.

"Don't," says Mitya softly, over the band, when she slows.

"I'm not."

"You are. I can see you. Come on."

She comes on. They go through the Old Hold on a line strung between the struts, hand over hand, in the slow moving dark, with the open lids nodding all round them. At the far end there's a hatch, and beyond the hatch the passage turns, and beyond the turning there's the Office.

The Index is what makes her stop again.

It's a long gallery lined on both sides and down the middle with steel cabinets, floor to ceiling, rows and rows of them with narrow aisles between, and every cabinet has burst. Every drawer is open or gone. And the water in the gallery is full of cards. Millions of them. Little rectangles of film, each the size of a palm, drifting in the cold clear water in a slow endless snowfall that the lamps light up white, that turns and glitters and parts around her helmet as she swims into it and closes again behind. It's like swimming into a cloud. It's the most beautiful thing she's ever seen, and she's seen the Hulk at perigee from the sled, burning.

She catches one. She can't help it. It's soft with three hundred years of wet and the print is smeared, but she can read some of it in her lamp: a name, HASKELL, and a place, *Gallows Reach 40*, and further down a line that says *struck* and a figure and a date that might be 929 before the film tears in her gauntlet and comes apart into grey threads.

"Yara."

"I know."

"What's it say?"

"Nothing." She lets the pieces go. They drift away into the snow. "Someone owed something."

The vault is at the end of the Office, past the Index, past a warren of smaller rooms with their doors standing open on drowned desks; and they don't reach it, because between the Office and the vault the passage is full of ice.

Not all of it. The perigees have melted the ice from the outside in, so that the passage is a tunnel of water ending, twenty metres along, in a wall — a great pale grey face of old ice, cloudy and fissured, filling the passage from wall to wall. There are things frozen in it. Papers. A chair. A grey shape that might be a coat or might be something wearing a coat. Yara puts her gauntlet flat against it and feels, through the shell, the cold of it, and under the cold a faint, deep, continuous creaking, like a ship at anchor. The ice is moving. It's melting, slowly, from the face back, and as it melts it's shifting, settling, finding new ways to lie.

"Two passes," says Mitya, behind her. "Three, maybe. Then it's clear."

"We haven't got three."

"We've got six."

"We've got six before she comes apart. That's not the same thing." She takes her hand off the ice. "We can cut it."

"Through that?" He laughs, high. "There's a hundred cubic metres of ice there, Yara. We'll be cutting till she burns."

They're arguing about it — quietly, the way they argue in the water, in short flat sentences with long gaps — when the Hulk moves.

It's not a slosh. It's worse. It comes through the walls, not the water: a great iron groan that goes on and on and rises in pitch until it's almost a shriek, and the whole passage shudders, and a row of cabinets in the Index gallery behind them tears loose from the floor with a sound like a gunshot and comes tumbling down the gallery toward them through the snow of cards in a slow, enormous, unstoppable fall.

Yara sees it coming and can't move. That's what she'll remember afterward. Not the fear — she's had the fear before, plenty of times, the Hulk is full of it — but the strange slow stillness of watching a wall of steel come at her through the water, end over end, scattering cards, and knowing exactly how long she has and being unable to use any of it.

Then a hand closes on her harness from behind and yanks, hard, and she's pulled sideways and down into the doorway of one of the little drowned offices, and the cabinets go past a metre from her faceplate and hit the ice wall at the end of the passage with a sound she feels in her spine.

Silence. The snow of cards swirls and settles. Somewhere, far off, the Hulk groans once more and is quiet.

Mitya is wedged in the doorway beside her with one arm still locked through her harness. Through his faceplate, in the lamp light, his face is grey.

"You froze," he says.

"I know."

"You never freeze."

"I know." She can hear her own breath, loud in the helmet. "Thank you."

"A third," says Mitya, and lets go of her harness, and laughs — high and shaky, the way he laughed after the slosh — and then stops laughing, all at once, and looks past her, up the passage toward the Old Hold.

There's a light.

Not theirs. A long way back, at the far end of the Index gallery, where the passage turns toward the Hold: a glow in the water, yellowish, moving. Then a second. Lamps. Two divers, or three, coming slowly along the gallery through the snow of cards, the way Yara and Mitya came.

"Who's on the Hulk?" says Mitya very quietly. "Nobody dives the front of the window. Nobody but us."

Yara kills her lamp. After a moment, he kills his. They hang in the dark of the drowned office, side by side, and watch the yellow lights come on down the gallery toward the ice wall, swinging, searching, until they're close enough that she can see the shells behind them, and the colour of the shells.

Yellow. Gorse's yellow. Every crew on Gorse's permit dives in yellow but theirs, because Yara's mother refused to repaint her shell and Yara has never repainted it either.

"He knows," says Mitya.

"Somebody told him."

"Not me."

She doesn't answer. In the dark beside her she can feel him looking at her, and she doesn't answer, and after a while the yellow lights reach the ice wall and stop there, and swing back and forth across its face, and across the tangle of steel cabinets now lying against it, and then turn, slowly, and go back the way they came.

* * *

When they come up into the Gutter, eleven hours later, with no copper and their air nearly gone, there are bailiffs on the concourse.

Yara hasn't seen one before. They're tall, all of them, or seem tall: men and women in long black coats of some material that shines faintly under the salvaged bulbs, with the key-and-sword crest of the *Lawful Remedy* on their sleeves and narrow black helmets with no visors, only a band of dark glass where the eyes should be. There are twelve of them. They're standing in a line in front of the concourse screen, very still, while two more go along the walls with sheets of film and a heat-gun and fix the sheets to the plating over a hundred years of divers' marks.

Yara stops in front of one. It's printed in the old Charter letters, very clear.

*WRIT OF EXECUTION. HIGH CHANCERY OF VEY. In the matter of the Account of Absalom, the Paschal Recovery Company, assignee. ALL PERSONS of the lines of portion-holding families recorded in the said Account are LIABLE for the obligations of those lines as recorded, with interest accrued. Registration is MANDATORY. Persons failing to register will be held in DEFAULT and may be taken in satisfaction.*

Under it, in smaller print, there's a rate of interest, and a date of last entry, and a formula. Yara reads the formula twice. She's not good with figures, not the way Pim is. But she understands that it's a figure that has been doubling for three hundred and eighty-eight years.

There's a line along the concourse wall, two hundred people long. Divers, divers' families, welders, the noodle-cart man. At the head of it, under a canopy of black film, three bailiffs sit at a table with a machine on it, and one at a time the people in the line hold out their wrists, and the machine pricks them, and a bailiff reads something off a screen and says something, and the person moves away. Mostly they move away. Yara watches a man she knows — Tobin Rusk, an old welder from the can three down from hers — hold out his wrist and wait and listen and then stand there, not moving away, until two of the bailiffs from the line come and take him by the elbows, quite gently, and walk him off down the concourse toward the dock where the *Lawful Remedy*'s shuttle is berthed.

Tobin's wife is standing by the wall. She doesn't scream. Yara has heard people scream in the Gutter, when the Hulk takes someone. This is worse. Tobin's wife stands by the wall with her hands pressed flat against the plating, against the divers' marks and the new white sheet of the writ, and makes a sound like the Hulk groaning, low and long and steady, and doesn't stop.

"Default of registration," says someone in the crowd. "He didn't register yesterday. He was on a shift."

"He's registering now."

"Too late. Read the writ."

Mitya's hand is on her arm. "Come on," he says quietly. "Home. Pim."

She goes. She goes home along the concourse past the line and the bailiffs and the writs, and she doesn't look at the shuttle dock, and she doesn't look back at Tobin's wife; and all the way home she can feel, under her skin, on the inside of her left wrist where the old woman pressed her thumb, the grey cold smudge of the ash.

Six passes.
