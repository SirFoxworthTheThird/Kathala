# Chapter 19: Down the Sea

*The Crossing · CR 611*

Absalom came up out of the dark on the twenty-second day after the Fork, and everyone who could stand at a viewport stood at one and watched it.

Annick watched from the Lantern, because the Master had asked her to. She stood at the rail of the command ring beside Nadia Szell, under the great armoured dome, with the Book's key at her throat and her hands at her sides, and looked up.

It filled a quarter of the sky. Then a third. A gas giant, the survey records had said, a little larger than the Charter's standard and a little cooler, with a system of moons; she had read the entry in the Book a dozen times without ever seeing it. Nothing in the entry was like this. It was banded — rust and ochre and cream and a deep bruised brown — in stripes that ran round it like the grain in a cut stone, and every band was moving, very slowly, against the ones beside it; and where they met, along every edge, there were storms. Curls and eddies and spirals of cloud, white and gold and the colour of old blood, each one as big as a world, turning, turning. A long way down one flank there was a single great eye, a dark oval storm with a pale rim, that seemed to look at nothing at all. The light of the sun lay across it at a low angle, so that half the giant was in shadow, and on the shadowed half, very faintly, there were flashes. Lightning. Each flash, Szell said, would light a continent.

And against the face of it, small and white and cracked like an old plate, a moon.

"Tamar," said Szell beside her. "The survey called it Absalom IV. Ice, all the way down. Eight hundred metres of it at the thinnest, then a sea." She was quiet for a moment. "It's where we'll live."

Behind them, on the Lantern, the bonded woman from the coil gang who now sat on the Council was crying, silently, with her hand over her mouth. Lieutenant Orme at the helm was not. Tobiah was not there; he was in the Hold, where he had been most of the twenty-two days, among the cradles, with the people who had been thawed at the Fork and the people who had not.

"Tomorrow," said Szell, "we'll go into orbit. And at noon, in the cargo bay, we'll burn the Book."

"Yes, Master."

"And after, you'll seal two entries." The Master's voice was pleasant, unhurried. "The Council as trustee of the patent, for the people of the hold. And the opening of the Account. Edwin's drafted them both; they're very short. I'd like it done the same afternoon, before anyone's had time to think too much about it." She turned her head and looked at Annick with her dark considering eyes. "You don't object."

"After the Burning," said Annick. "Not before. So that it's clean."

Szell considered that. Then she nodded, slowly, as if a reading had come back as she expected.

"After," she agreed. "So that it's clean."

* * *

Dace was in the infirmary.

She had been there for twenty-two days. The needle in her shoulder had gone deep, and the one along her ribs had opened her side like a seam, and Haddad had packed the wounds and closed them and then opened them again when they went bad in the second week, and for four days at the end of the first jump Annick had sat beside her through the ship's night and listened to her breathe — too fast, too shallow, in the bonded way, saving air that nobody was billing any more. Dace had not woken for most of those four nights. When she did, she had looked at Annick sitting there and turned her face to the wall.

She was sitting up now, propped on pillows in the ring's light gravity, with her shoulder strapped and a great white dressing on her side under the gown, and she watched Annick come in across the long room and sit down in the chair by her bunk, and did not turn away.

"Tobiah came," said Dace.

Annick waited.

"Yesterday. He sat where you're sitting. He asked how I was, and I said I'd live, and he said good." Her sister's voice was flat and steady. "Then he told me about Marit. That the Factor asked you for a name, for me, and you gave him hers. He said you did it for me. He said he wanted me to know that, because I'd find out anyway, and he wanted me to hear it from someone who didn't hate you for it."

"Does he not?"

"He says not." Dace looked at her. "I do."

Annick said nothing.

"She talked to me in the Hold," said Dace. "Marit. Every night for three months. She taught me law. Did you know that? The guild settlement, and the bonded codes, and the Ordinances, all of them — she said I had a head for it. She said when we got to Absalom she'd make me her clerk. She was going to write the colony's charter. She'd started it. On paper, in the Hold, in pencil, under her pillow." Dace's voice did not shake. "They found it when they took her. The proctors. They burned it in the galley furnace. Kofi saw."

"I'm sorry."

"I know you are. You always are." Her sister leaned forward a little, wincing at the shoulder. "You think I don't know what you did. You think I don't know you lay in that strongroom all night with your hand on the Book and did your sums and got me on one side and her on the other and found me heavier. I know. I'd have done the same, maybe. I don't know. I hate you for it anyway."

"Yes," said Annick.

"Is that all? *Yes?*"

"What else would you like me to say?"

Dace stared at her. And then, unexpectedly, terribly, her face broke. Not much. Her mouth went, and her eyes, and she put her good hand over them and sat there in the bunk with her shoulders shaking and made no sound at all; and Annick sat in the chair and did not touch her, because she did not know if she was allowed, and the infirmary hummed round them, and somewhere at the far end of it one of the thawed was moaning in her sleep.

When Dace took her hand away, her eyes were red and quite dry.

"Why are you here?" she said.

So Annick told her.

She told it level, in the clerk's voice, very low, leaning close to the bunk, the way you'd read out a bill. She told her about the day room door at the Fork and what she had heard through it. Quarry's voice: *burn the Book; they'll weep; then raise an account; call it Liberty.* Tobiah's: *somebody's got to pay for the air.* Szell's: *I made sure he'd never be asked.* Orme on the bridge deck. *Nobody will ever think anything else.* She told her about the two entries the Master wanted sealed tomorrow afternoon, after the fire, before anyone had time to think too much about it.

Dace listened without moving. When it was done, she sat for a long time looking at the dressing on her side.

"Then tell them," she said. "Tomorrow. In the cargo bay. Stand up in front of everyone and tell them."

"Tell them what? That the woman who gave Marit Osei to the Factor overheard something through a door?" Annick shook her head. "Szell holds the ship. Every lock on her answers to Szell; I made sure of it. Tobiah holds the people. They love him. They'd follow him into the coil. They've been in the dark for fourteen months, and tomorrow he's going to give them a fire, and they'll weep, and anything I say after that will be the Ink trying to keep her Book." She looked at her sister. "And then they'll take the Book from me and burn it anyway, and the key with it, and seal whatever they like."

"So you'll let them."

"No," said Annick.

Dace looked at her. Something in her face changed.

"I need you to keep something," said Annick. "For a long time. Maybe for the rest of your life. Maybe longer — maybe you'll have to give it to someone, and they'll have to give it to someone. And never use it. Never sell it. Never tell anyone what it is, unless the day comes when—" She stopped. She did not know how to say it. "Unless the day comes when someone can stand up and say it to all of them, and be heard."

"Why me?" said Dace. "You don't trust me. I don't trust you."

"That's why," said Annick. "You're the only person on this ship who doesn't believe a word I say. So you'll check."

Her sister looked at her for a long time. Then, slowly, she held out her good hand across the blanket, palm up, and Annick took it, and held it. It was cold. It was always cold. Annick held it in both of hers, the way she used to on the stack roofs of Callis when Dace was five, and they sat like that without speaking while the infirmary hummed round them and the ship came down toward the moon where they would live.

* * *

She went to the strongroom at the change of the night watch and did not turn on the lamp at first.

She floated in the dark beside the cradle with her hand on the Book's iron lid, the way she had on the night before Marit died, and listened to the ship. The murmur had changed again since the Fork. It was louder, more broken: there were nearly two thousand people awake on the *Merciful Increase* now, the Wake and the thawed and the crew, crowded into a ring built for a few hundred, and the ship did not sleep any more; it muttered all night, like a city. Under the murmur, on the other side of the left-hand bulkhead, the water of Tank Four shifted in its long slow sleep. On the other side of the right-hand bulkhead, in the Hold, the cradles that were still full — one thousand nine hundred and four of them; she had counted — hummed in the blue cold.

She turned on the lamp. She opened the Book. She put the key in the hasp and turned it, and the lid unlocked with its deep, soft sound like a door closing far away, and the page-glass woke and filled with the voyage, blue-white, trembling.

She did not scroll back. She did not need to. She knew what was there.

She put her thumb to the bead.

"Entry," she said. "Annick Vell. Purser of the *Merciful Increase*, Voyage the Fifth, holding the Key and the Book together. In orbit of the world Absalom. Charter Year six hundred and eleven, the night before Landfall."

The page-glass took it. She watched the words grow in their neat column.

"I record that at Pell the Receivers of Vey ordered this ship and the debts of her hold sold to the Shale Concern, where the hold would have been worked until it died; and that Factor Edwin Quarry sought my seal to it, for a commission of two in the hundred.

"I record that Purser Aurelio Benn refused to seal it. And that First Officer Nadia Szell closed the valve of his air while he slept, and opened it again when he was dead. The concentrator's log records it, with her code erased. She has said it in my hearing.

"I record that at the Fork the bonded of the hold rose against the Factor; and that I sealed Nadia Szell as Master of this ship, because there was no one else who could fly her; and that Captain Harrow Lindqvist was shot on the bridge deck by Lieutenant Caspar Orme, on her order, and not by any bonded hand.

"I record that after the rising, Nadia Szell and Tobiah Ruck and Edwin Quarry agreed among themselves to burn this Book before the people at Landfall, and to raise in its place an account of what every person freed owes for that freedom, and to call it Liberty, and to keep it.

"I record that I gave the name of Marit Osei to the Factor, to save my sister's life; and that Marit Osei was put out of the forward lock for it, and her debt passed to her granddaughter, and I sealed it. Let it be written against me. It is owed."

She stopped. She breathed. Four in. Hold. Four out. It did not help. It never had.

"Now therefore," she said.

"Under Article Twenty of the Ordinances, I discharge every debt written in this Book. Of every person of the hold, the living and the dead, and of their heirs and assigns, forever. Emeric Saal's, and his children's. Marit Osei's, and her granddaughter's. Every mark of passage, and every meal, and every litre of air. Nothing written in this Book is owed by anyone.

"And whereas the Charter's patent of the world Absalom and all the worlds of its system stands recorded in this Book, to vest in the colony at Landfall: I vest it in the people of the hold and their heirs, in common. To be held by no one person, and no council, and no house. And no account of debt shall be raised against them under colour of this Book, or of any copy of it, or of any entry claiming to be made from it.

"And under Article Nine, this ship being taken by parties not lawfully in command, I commit this Book to the ballast, that it be not suborned. They will burn the dead book of the third voyage in its place tomorrow, and believe they have burned this. Let them."

She looked at the page-glass. At the words in their column, blue-white, trembling a little, as if the light were not quite sure of itself.

"What is written is owed," she said. "I have written what we owe each other."

She did not say *sealed*. Not yet.

* * *

She carried the Book into the Hold with the key still in its hasp and the lid open, so that the page-glass lit her way.

There was nobody there. At this hour, since the Fork, there was never anybody there; the cradle techs worked the day watch, and the thawed would not come in. The great hatch stood open. She went through it into the blue cold, and up into the racks, hand over hand on the struts with the Book held against her chest in one arm, three tiers in and halfway down, to the hollow.

Most of the cradles here were still full. Lise Saal's was. Annick floated in front of it and looked through the frost on the window at the thin young face, the straight nose, the mouth that turned down at the corners, the rime on the lashes. Above it, the two smaller cradles. Tam. Orrin. Further along the row, a cradle she had never looked at before, that she had had to find in the manifest this afternoon: a small white shell, child-sized, with a girl's face behind the frost. Nine years old. Dark, with her grandmother's narrow chin. *Osei, Abena.*

Around her, above, below, receding into the blue dimness on every side, one thousand nine hundred and four of them. Sleeping. Owing nothing.

*Article Eleven. An entry read aloud under seal before witnesses stands as the Book itself.*

*It was meant for wrecks*, Benn had said. *It never worked very well. Witnesses forget.*

She put her thumb back on the bead, and read it to them.

All of it. From *Entry* to *each other*. She did not raise her voice. In the Hold there was no need to; the cold carried every word out among the racks and the struts and the frosted windows, and gave it back to her, very faintly, from a long way off. She read it the way she had read Emeric Saal's transfer in the coil gallery, the way she had read every bill and every death and every name for eleven years: level, clear, without hurry. She read the part about Marit. She read the part about the patent. She read *nothing written in this Book is owed by anyone* to one thousand nine hundred and four sleeping faces behind the frost; and not one of them moved, and not one of them heard, and the cradles hummed, and the pumps sighed in the racks.

When she came to the end she stayed for a moment with her thumb on the bead.

Then she said the last line. It was not in the Ordinances. She had not planned it. It came out of her in the clerk's level voice, as if she had been writing it all her life.

"Read aloud under seal, in the Hold, before witnesses. Who were asleep."

Her voice shook. On *asleep*. She heard it, and the Book heard it, and took it, and grew it into the lattice where it would stay.

"Sealed," she said.

* * *

Back in the strongroom she worked quickly, because there was no longer any reason not to.

She took the key from the hasp. The page-glass went dark and the lid locked, and the Book was only a block of iron again, heavy in her arms. She set it on the floor beside the pedestal, against the drop hatch, where the red lever stood with its seal-wire looped through it.

She went to the dead shelf and unstrapped VOYAGE III.

It was identical to the Book in every way she had ever been able to find, except for the number stamped on the cover plate and the seal across its hasp: a bar of soft grey lead, crimped over the steel, with the Charter's mark pressed into it. She worked the lead off with the blade of the strongroom's pen-knife, slowly, careful not to scar the steel. It came away in one piece, a little curled strip the length of her finger. Underneath, the hasp was clean and bright. The number plate on the cover she could do nothing about; but it was small and low on the iron, below the sunburst, and nobody aboard but she had ever looked at a Book closely enough to read it, and tomorrow nobody would. She set VOYAGE III in the cradle, where the Book had been. In the dim lamplight it looked exactly as it always had.

She took the curl of lead to the bench under the plaque and flattened it with the base of the bead press until it was a rough grey plate the size of her thumb. With the point of the pen-knife she scratched into it, small, in plain clerk's capitals, the only thing she could think of that would mean something to someone who held the key and nothing to anyone who didn't.

ART. 9 — A.V.

Then she put the key into the privy box.

The little steel door opened to it the way it always had. Inside, on the shelf where generations of pursers had kept their wills and their letters, there was a single thing: a squat brown bottle with a wax seal, Callisian brandy, that Aurelio Benn had been too frightened to drink for twenty-eight years and had left, under the Ordinances, to a sister in the Callis hill district who would never receive it.

Annick took it out. She broke the wax with her thumbnail. She drank.

It was terrible. It burned all the way down and lay in her stomach like a coal, and she coughed, and her eyes ran, and she laughed — once, aloud, in the dark strongroom, a sound she did not recognise — and drank again. Then she put the bottle back in the privy box, beside the little grey plate of lead, and shut the door, and turned the key, and it locked.

The Book was where she had left it, against the drop hatch.

She knelt beside it. She broke the seal-wire on the red lever and drew it out of its loop and put it in her pocket. She pulled the lever. Under the floor something heavy moved — a bolt, a counterweight — and the drop hatch swung down and open, and beneath it there was a short dark throat of lead-lined iron, slanting away into the deck toward the left-hand bulkhead, just wide enough for one thing. From somewhere at the bottom of it, faint and cold, came the smell of water.

Beside the drop hatch, set flush in the floor, there was a round iron plate a metre across with eight bolts round its rim, that she had walked over for eleven years without thinking about. *Recovery port*, said a small stencil on its edge. *Ballast 4 well. For use at port by lawful owner only.* So that whoever came after, with the right to, could go down and fetch back what had been committed. She looked at it for a moment. Then she looked away.

She lifted the Book in both arms. It was heavy. It had never been so heavy.

"Down the sea," she said, very softly, aloud. She did not know who to.

She slid it into the throat of the drop.

It went with a long low iron sound, sliding, gathering speed, the lead sleeve hissing against the chute, down and away into the dark under the deck; and then, after what seemed a long time, from very far off on the other side of the bulkhead, so faint she was not sure she heard it at all — a sound like something heavy entering water. And then nothing.

She closed the hatch. She pushed the lever back up. She took the seal-wire out of her pocket and looked at it, and it was snapped, and would not loop; so she reached up to the back of her own head, where for twenty-two days, since the Fork, she had been tying her hair the way her sister tied hers, and pulled out the twist of fine steel wire she had begged from the coil gang's stores, and looped it through the lever and twisted it closed. It was not Charter wire. It would do. Nobody aboard had ever looked at it closely enough to know.

Then she sat on the floor of the strongroom with her back against the cradle, where a dead book from a voyage twenty-three years gone now lay in the Book's place, and let her hair fall round her face, and waited for the shaking to stop.

* * *

She went to the infirmary in the last hour before dawn watch. The long room was dim and full of the breathing of the thawed. Haddad was asleep at his desk with his head on his arms.

Dace was awake. She watched Annick come down the row between the bunks and sit in the chair, and she did not say anything, and she held out her good hand, palm up, on the blanket.

Annick put the key in it.

Her sister's fingers closed round it. She looked at it — the long iron key, the notched bit, the dull bead in the bow, the small stamped number on the shank — for a long time. Then, without a word, she lifted the edge of the great white dressing on her side, wincing, and slid the key in under the packing against the wound, flat, along her ribs, and smoothed the dressing back down over it with the flat of her hand.

"Haddad changes it every morning," said Annick.

"Not this one he won't," said Dace. "I'll do it myself. I've watched him for three weeks." She lay back against the pillows, pale, with her hand over the dressing. "What does it open?"

"A box."

"What's in the box?"

"Something that will tell whoever opens it where to look."

"And where's that?"

Annick looked at her sister. Then she leaned close and told her. Two words, very low, so that even Haddad asleep at the desk ten metres away could not have heard.

Dace closed her eyes. When she opened them again they were wet, and she was almost smiling.

"The song," she said.

"The song."

"*When the Book goes down the sea.*" Her sister's hand tightened on the dressing. "I always thought that was just a rhyme."

At the end of the long room, at his desk, with his head on his arms, Yusuf Haddad had opened his eyes. He did not lift his head. He looked down the row of bunks at the two of them for a long moment, at Dace's hand flat on the dressing; and then, very deliberately, he closed his eyes again, and turned his face the other way.

* * *

They burned it at noon, in the cargo bay, before all the people.

The *Merciful Increase* had come into orbit of Tamar in the morning watch, and through the bay's long ports the moon hung white and cracked and very close, with the vast banded face of Absalom behind it filling half the sky. The bay had been emptied of stores. Nearly two thousand people filled it: the Wake in their dun, and the thawed in whatever they had been given — crew coveralls, blankets, the sleeping greys of the dead — and the crew in their uniforms, all floating together in the zero gravity in a great close silent mass, holding the stanchions and the cargo rails and each other. Children on shoulders. Old men held up between their sons. Ama Brook from the galley, at the front, with her grey braids and her face wet.

At the centre of the bay, on a disc of black iron welded to the deck for the purpose, stood a crucible: a squat steel bowl on three legs, packed with thermite.

The Council of the Founders floated beside it. Seven of them. Szell in a clean Master's coat. Quarry in his good one. Orme, the bonded woman from the coil, two others. And Tobiah Ruck, enormous, in plain dun, the same as the Wake, with his scarred head bowed.

Annick floated a little apart, at the edge, where the Purser stood at any entry. She held nothing. Her hands were empty.

Szell brought the Book from the strongroom herself. She carried it through the crowd in both arms, slowly, so that everyone could see it — the iron case, the worn sunburst on its cover, the bright steel hasp — and the crowd parted for her and closed behind, and a sound went through it, low, like wind. She set it in the crucible. Nobody looked at the little number plate below the sunburst. Nobody looked at anything but the Book.

Tobiah spoke.

Annick would never be able to remember afterward exactly what he said. She would remember that it was short, and that his voice was low and soft and carried to every corner of the bay without his raising it, and that he did not say anything about Liberty. He said that they had been carried in the dark. He said that they had been counted, and billed, and sold, and that some of them had died of it, and he said their names — Emeric Saal, Marit Osei, Joss Imre, and others, a great many others, more than Annick had known he knew. He said that this was the Book in which every one of them had been written down. He said that from today, on this world, nobody's name would be written in it again.

Then he took the striker from Szell's hand and lit the crucible.

The thermite caught with a sound like tearing cloth. White light — light so fierce that the whole bay went white with it, every face, every hand — and a roar, and a pillar of white smoke boiling up toward the ribs of the ceiling and spreading out under them in a grey cloud. In the heart of the light, the iron case of the dead book of the third voyage blistered, and blackened, and ran. The sunburst on its cover went soft and slid. The hasp fused. Something inside it cracked, very loud, like ice breaking.

The crowd made a sound Annick had never heard a crowd make. Not a cheer. Something lower and older — a great broken cry that went up from two thousand throats at once and went on and on, and turned, as it went on, into weeping. People held each other. People hid their faces. An old man near the front was on his knees in the air, if you can kneel with nothing under you, with his hands over his eyes. Ama Brook had her arms round a girl Annick did not know.

And then, from somewhere at the back, near the hatch, a man's voice began to sing.

*Heave the iron, heave it slow.*

Another took it up. And another. Not quietly, this time; not under their breath, the way the Wake had sung it when Marit went out of the lock. Loud. The whole bay. Two thousand voices in the white light and the grey smoke, the coil gang's song, the quarrymen's song, the song nobody aboard had known the meaning of —

*Heave and heave and we'll be free,
when the Book goes down the sea —*

Annick floated at the edge of the bay with her empty hands and her face lit white by the burning of a book whose debts had been paid twenty years before, and listened to them sing it. She did not sing. She looked across the crucible and found that Tobiah Ruck was looking at her, through the smoke and the light; and his broken face was wet, and he was singing with the rest; and she could not tell, she would never be able to tell, what he was thinking.

* * *

"There's no key," she said.

They were in the strongroom: Szell, Quarry, Orme, Tobiah, and Annick. Two hours after the fire. The bay was still full of smoke; you could smell it even here, in the spine, through the iron. On the strongroom bench, beside the cradle where VOYAGE III had lain until noon, there were two sheets of film in Quarry's elegant hand and a new lattice block, grown that morning in the ship's laboratory, small and clean and the colour of weak tea, waiting for its first entry.

"I'm sorry?" said Szell.

"There's no key. I haven't got it." Annick stood by the empty cradle with her hands at her sides. "I can't seal anything. Not the trusteeship. Not the Account. Nothing. There is no purser's key on this ship any more."

There was a silence.

"Where is it?" said Szell. Quite gently.

"Gone."

"Gone where?"

Annick said nothing.

Orme searched her. He did it thoroughly and without any expression, as he did everything; he searched her coat and her clothes and her boots and her hair, which she had let fall, and then he made her take off her clothes, and searched her, and then he searched the strongroom — the bench, the shelves, the cradle, the dead books on their straps, the gap where the third had been. He tried the privy box, which would not open. He looked at the drop hatch and the red lever with its loop of wire, and Annick stood naked by the cradle and watched him look at it, and did not breathe; and he looked away. He went to her cabin and searched that. He came back and shook his head.

"The lock," said Quarry softly. "She went to the forward lock last night, I expect. Or the galley furnace." He looked at Annick with his pricing eyes, and there was something in them she had not seen before — not anger; something closer to interest. "It doesn't matter, Master. The Book's ash. You don't need a purser's seal for a council's account. A council has its own seal. I'll have one made."

"It won't be the same."

"No," said Quarry. "But nobody will know the difference. Not for years. Not ever, if you're careful." He did not take his eyes off Annick. "I've always wondered what she'd cost. I see I was wrong about the price."

Szell looked at Annick for a long moment. Then she said, in the same pleasant unhurried voice she used for everything, "Then she's a traitor. She hid the key so that the Charter's creditors could claim us back. So that one day, when the Receivers came up the Lane, there'd be someone with a key to the Book's own records, ready to sell us to them." She nodded slowly, as if testing the shape of it. "*The Purser would have sold us.* That's what we'll say. They'll believe it. They watched her enter Marit's death."

"They'll want her put out of the lock," said Orme.

"Yes."

"No," said Tobiah Ruck.

He had not spoken. He had stood by the door the whole time, through the search, with his great arms folded and his eyes on the floor. He lifted his head now.

"No more lock," he said. "Not today. Not on the day we burned it. I won't have it." He looked at Szell. "There's a survey party going down to the ice tomorrow. To find a site. Cut the first galleries. Send her with them. Let her dig."

"She'll die on the ice."

"Maybe. Maybe not. That's for the ice." His soft voice did not rise. "Send her down."

Szell considered him. Then she shrugged, very slightly. "As you like," she said. "The ice, then." She turned to the door. "Edwin, the seal."

They went. Orme went. Quarry went last, and at the door he stopped and looked back at Annick standing naked by the cradle, and then, very briefly, at the drop hatch in the floor with its loop of fine steel wire; and then at her again. He smiled very slightly.

"A Factor learns the Ordinances too, Purser," he said. "All twenty-two." And went.

Tobiah stayed.

He picked up her clothes from the floor, where Orme had thrown them, and held them out to her without looking at her, and she took them and put them on. He waited until she had. Then he looked at her — for a long moment, steadily, the way he had looked at her across the mess the night Marit died. And then his eyes went, as Quarry's had, to the floor beside the pedestal. To the drop hatch. The red lever. The twist of wire through its loop that was not Charter wire.

"Down the sea," said Tobiah Ruck, very quietly.

It might have been a question. She did not answer it.

He looked at the lever for a long time. Then he looked at her again, and something moved in his broken face that she would think about on the ice for the rest of her life, and never be sure of — and he turned without a word, and ducked his great head under the red letters over the door, and went out, and left the strongroom to her.

* * *

The survey shuttle went down at dawn watch the next day with nine people in it: six crew volunteers with sledges and lances and two months' stores, and Annick Vell under guard, and her sister.

Dace had walked out of the infirmary at four in the morning on her own feet, against Haddad's orders, with her shoulder strapped and her side bound tight under a clean dressing she had changed herself, and gone to the shuttle bay and sat down on the bench beside her sister and said to the guard, "I'm going too," and refused to be moved. Nobody had argued long. One more mouth on the ice was one less in the ring.

Haddad had come to the bay to see them off. He had brought a bag of dressings and a bottle of something and a book. He gave Dace the dressings and Annick the book, and did not say anything at all, and Annick did not look at the title until much later.

The shuttle fell away from the *Merciful Increase* and turned, and for a moment, through the small port beside her seat, Annick saw the ship. The whole of her, at once, for the first time in eleven years: a long dark seed-shaped spine eleven hundred metres from bow to stern, with the great ring turning slowly about her middle and the dome of the Lantern catching the light of the sun at her bow, hanging against the banded face of Absalom. She looked small. She looked very old.

Somewhere in the middle of her, in the dark beside the Hold, there was a tank of black water twelve metres deep. And at the bottom of it, in the silt, in its lead sleeve, the Book lay with every debt in it discharged and every name in it free and the world it had been given held by no one; in the ballast, where the Ordinances said it should be, waiting — as long as it had to — for someone lawfully in command to come for it.

Beside her, on the bench, Dace's good hand found hers. Under the clean dressing on her sister's side, against the wound, Annick could feel through her own palm the faint hard shape of the key.

Below them, rising, white and cracked like an old plate, the ice.
