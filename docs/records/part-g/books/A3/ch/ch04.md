## The Director's Office
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Ruth Achterberg @@Hale Varga @@Teodor Basu
Items: ^The Director's Seal ^The Tally
When: +5 days
]

There were three of them in the end, because three was what the Director's office would admit. Dagny for the Wick crews. Mariam Oyelowo for the strip-farms, a broad, slow-spoken woman with soil in the creases of her knuckles that no scrubbing would shift. And Ezra Hesketh for the pumps and stills, who was seventy and had been a Speaker longer than Dagny had been alive, and who walked with a stick and had told her on the way up, in the lift, that he expected nothing and would be disappointed anyway.

The Counting House sat at the fore end of Lantern, at the root of the Wick, where the spin was least and the light was best. It had been built first, before the Galleries, before the farms; there was a story that the Company's surveyors had lived in it for two years while the rest of the rock was hollowed out round them. Its lower floors were the vault, where the Tally ran, and nobody Dagny knew had ever seen them. Its upper floors were offices. The top one was the Director's, and it had a window.

Dagny had seen the window from below all her life. It was a single long pane, curving, set into the face of the Counting House so that it looked straight down the length of Lantern, along the Wick, to the dark aft end of the rock eleven kilometres away. From the Galleries it looked like a strip of mirror. From inside it was the whole world, spread out and lit and upside down above you, every terrace and roof and tank and farm, and the Wick blazing down the middle of it like a sword.

She stood at it for a moment while they waited, because she couldn't help it. Gallery Six was a speck. She found it by the line of the water mains. Somewhere in that speck Pim was at school.

"It's a good view," said a voice behind her. "I've never got used to it."

It was the Comptroller. Basu. A small, neat, brown man of fifty or so, in a Company suit that had been well cut once and was now a little big for him, as though he had lost weight in a hurry. He stood a careful distance from the glass.

"You don't like heights," Dagny said.

"No." He smiled, briefly. "An unfortunate thing on Lantern. Everything is a height." He looked at her, and then away, and she had the odd impression that he wanted to say something else and had decided against it.

Then the inner door opened and the Director came in, with Hale Varga behind her, and they all sat down.

## The Proposal
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Ruth Achterberg @@Hale Varga @@Teodor Basu
When: same day
]

Dagny had thought about how to begin. In the end she didn't begin; Hesketh did, which he had told her he would, because he was the eldest.

"Director," he said. "There are nine thousand one hundred and forty indentured people on this rock. You've told us you're leaving and they're staying. We've come to ask what happens to them."

"I told you that, too," said Ruth Achterberg. She sat very straight at the bare desk. The brass disk of the seal lay against her collarbone, on its chain. "Their contracts are held in trust. Their systems are run by the Tally. Nothing changes."

"Everything changes," said Mariam Oyelowo.

"Nothing that matters to the air."

"It matters to the air that there'll be nobody on this rock who can hear a complaint," said Dagny. "Five days ago a man died in a lock on Gallery Nine because his balance was four over. There was no counter on Nine. The override was here." She put her hand flat on the desk. "In this building. With you. In fifty days there won't be anybody in this building at all. What happens then, Director? Who does the next one ask?"

Achterberg looked at her for a moment. "I'm sorry about Mr. Adeyemi," she said, and it was hard to tell whether she meant it; her voice was the same voice she used for everything. "Mr. Basu has prepared a proposal which I think addresses that. Teodor?"

The Comptroller cleared his throat. His hand went to the folder in front of him and then away again.

"The— the Tally's dimming line," he said, "is currently set at minus nine hundred credits. For the duration of the closure the Company proposes to reset it to minus fifteen hundred. It also proposes to suspend interest on all accounts. So that no balance grows except by— by actual consumption. Nobody's debt will grow merely by— by being there."

"It's a substantial concession," said the Director. "It costs the Company a great deal."

"It costs the Company nothing," said Dagny. "The Company won't be here."

There was a small silence.

"And what happens at minus fifteen hundred?" said Hesketh, mildly. "Same as at nine. Door shuts. Ration stops. Lock goes to recovery. You've given us six hundred credits of rope, Director. That's a year for a child. Then what?"

"Then they'll have to work," said Achterberg. "As people do everywhere."

"With what? You're taking the refinery staff. You're taking the ice contracts. You're taking the—"

"We are taking," said the Director, "what is ours, Mr. Hesketh. As is our right." She did not raise her voice. "I want to be absolutely clear with all three of you, because I think you have been told things by people who wanted you to believe them. The _Covenant_ can carry fourteen hundred people. She will carry the Company's essential staff, who number four hundred and six with their families, and she will carry their effects and such Company property as can be moved. She cannot carry nine thousand. Nothing in this system can carry nine thousand. If I could take you all, I would not have to, because you would be a cost and not an asset; but I can't, so the question does not arise."

"Then leave us the Tally," said Dagny. "Unlocked. Take the books with you if you want them, take every number in it, but take the locks off the air before you go. Let the scrubbers run for everybody, debt or no debt. That's all we're asking. Not passage. Not money. Air."

Achterberg turned and looked at Teodor Basu. Basu looked at the desk.

"Tell them," said the Director.

"It— the Tally isn't a lock on the air plant, Ms. Ostrova," Basu said. "It is the air plant. That's— that's how it was designed. There is no separate control system. Every valve on Lantern asks the Tally whether to open, and the Tally answers by— by checking whose air it is. If you took the Tally out, the valves would have nobody to ask. They'd fail closed. Every one." He spread his hands, helplessly. "There's no way to unlock it without— without stopping it. And if you stop it—"

"Everyone dies," said Achterberg. "Yes. I'm sorry. It was not built to be convenient for this moment. It was built, ninety years ago, to make sure no one on this rock ever took air that was not paid for. It has done that very well. It will go on doing it." She folded her hands. "I would ask you to look at it a different way. The Tally is the only reason this colony can survive our withdrawal at all. It runs itself. It needs no engineers, no clerks, no Director. As long as you work, it will keep you alive. That is not nothing. In most closing systems in the Concord's history the colonists starved within a decade. You will not."

"We'll just owe," said Mariam.

"You'll owe what you have always owed. To the people who built this place and paid for your passage to it." Achterberg stood. "And one day the Throat will open again, and the Company — or its successors — will come back, and the debt will be honoured. That is what _in trust_ means. It means we haven't forgotten you."

Behind her, Hale Varga, who had not said a word, shifted his weight very slightly, the way a big man does when he wants a room to remember he's in it.

Hesketh got up on his stick. "Six hundred years," he said. "That's a long time to be remembered."

## The Crawl
[
Place: #The Wick
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Teodor Basu
Items: ^The Director's Seal ^The Comptroller's Key ^The Tally
When: same day
]

He came to find her that night on the Wick.

Dagny was on the third crawl, two hundred metres up the spine, half inside a coolant junction with her lamp in her teeth, and she heard him long before she saw him: the clank of somebody climbing who did not climb, the scuff of shoes that were not boots, the hard short breathing of a man who has looked down. When she backed out of the junction he was clinging to the crawl rail ten metres below her, white as the lamp, with his eyes shut.

"You'll want to open them," she said. "It's worse with them shut. You can't see the rungs."

"I— yes. Thank you." He opened them. Below him the whole of Lantern hung, lit, at every angle, the far terraces small as stitches. He shut them again. "No. I— I don't think I can."

She climbed down to him, because the alternative was to leave a Company comptroller to fall off the Wick in the middle of a shift and spend the next week explaining it to Varga. She hooked a spare tether from her belt to his and clipped it to the rail. "There. You can let go now, you won't go anywhere. Sit."

He sat on the crawl, very slowly, with his knees drawn up and his back to the rail, like a child on a high wall. The light of the lamp lay on him from above, harsh and white, and showed every line on his face. It was the face of somebody who had not slept in a long time.

"What do you want, Mr. Basu?"

"I wanted— I wanted to speak to you where there aren't cameras." He gave a small breathless laugh. "There's only one place on Lantern the Tally doesn't watch. The Wick's too bright for the lenses. Did you know that? I designed the camera net and I couldn't make it see up here. It was the first thing I ever failed at."

"And the second?"

He didn't answer that. He looked at his hands.

"What the Director told you today was true," he said. "The Tally can't be unlocked without stopping it. I built it so. I was twenty-six and very good at my work and very proud, and the Company wanted a system that could not be argued with, and I gave it one. No back door. No override that a frightened counter could be bribed to use. Every valve asks it. It answers. That's all." He breathed. "But it's not quite all."

Dagny waited.

"Every system has a root," said Teodor Basu. "The place where the rules are kept, as opposed to the place where they're applied. The Tally's root is in the vault. A console, about so big, behind three doors. From there you can change anything. The dimming line. The rates. Who owes what to whom. Whether there's a line at all." He was speaking faster now, the stammer gone, the way some people's went when they were on ground they knew. "To open the root you need two keys, together, at the same moment. One is the Director's. You've seen it. She wears it round her neck. The other is the Comptroller's."

"Yours."

"Mine." He reached down — she watched him, puzzled — and unlaced his left shoe, and took it off, and put two fingers inside it and drew out a thin slip of something pale, like a long fingernail, which he held up to the light. It was ceramic. There were marks burned into it, too fine to read. "It never leaves me. Not in the bath. Not in bed. It was a condition of my contract."

"And with both of them," said Dagny slowly, "you could—"

"With both of them, at the root, a person could do anything with the Tally that the Tally can do. They couldn't switch it off. It isn't built to be switched off. But they could rewrite its book." He put the key back in his shoe and laced it again, with great care, his fingers not quite steady. "I'm not telling you that you can do this. You can't. The Director will be wearing her seal when she walks up the ramp onto the _Covenant_ and the seal will go through the Throat with her. And I will be on the _Covenant_ too. I'm essential staff. I'm on the list."

"Then why are you telling me?"

He looked up at her at last. She thought for a moment that he was going to weep; then she saw that he was past that, a long way past it, in some dry country on the other side.

"Because you stood on a table," he said, "and said that nobody on this rock was going to owe air to an empty office for six hundred years. And I built the office. And I have done other things, Ms. Ostrova, that I can't— that I won't— " He stopped. He shook his head. "I wanted one person to know there was a door. Even if it's locked. Even if there's no getting to it. I wanted someone to know it was there."

Below them, very far down, the bell rang once in the Dock Ring. They both stopped, and listened to it come up the spine, faint and sour, and fade.

"Who was that?" said Basu.

"I don't know. I'll know in an hour."

"Yes," he said. "Everybody always knows." He unclipped the tether from his belt and held it out to her, and then, when she didn't take it, clipped it back on. "Would you— could you help me down? I don't think I can do it on my own."

She helped him down. It took forty minutes. At the bottom he thanked her, very formally, and walked away along the spine-road toward the Counting House without looking back; and Dagny stood at the foot of the Wick in the hard white light and thought about a key in a shoe, and a seal on a chain, and what it meant that a man would climb two hundred metres up a lamp he was afraid of to tell her about a door she could not open.

## The Slake
[
Place: #The Dock Ring
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Kas Ostrova
Items: ^Fuel Cassettes
When: same day
]

The Slake was the worst bar on the Dock Ring and therefore the one the hauler crews used, a low cave cut into the ring wall with a counter made of a hauler's old heat-shield and a smell of spilt still-spirit that had soaked into the rock itself. Kas was at the back table playing banker for a game of shove with four crewmen off the _Agnes_ and the _Dorrit_, and losing. He lost cheerfully. Kas always lost cheerfully; it was why people kept letting him play.

He saw her face in the doorway and folded his hand.

"Gentlemen," he said, "my sister has come to save me from myself. Hold my seat. Don't look at my cards. I'll know." And he came over and steered her to the end of the counter, where the noise was worst and nobody could hear.

"Well?" he said.

"Nothing. She gave us six hundred credits of rope."

Kas whistled softly. "Generous."

"Basu says the Tally can't be turned off without killing everyone."

"Basu would know." He signalled for two cups of the thin brown beer the Slake brewed in a tank out the back, and when they came he pushed one to her and drank half of his own in one go. "So what now? Strike?"

"Maybe. Hesketh thinks it's too early. Mariam thinks it's too late." She turned the cup in her hands. "Kas. When's your next run?"

"Two days. Why?"

"Do any of the haulers go through the Throat? Even now? I was thinking — if somebody could get a message through. To the Concord. The papers, the courts, the Labour Office, I don't know. Somebody who'd care that nine thousand people were being left behind in a box with a meter on the air."

He laughed, not unkindly. "Dag. Nothing goes through the Throat that the Company doesn't send. Haulers haven't got the drive for it, and the throat-watch would see you coming a week out, and Varga's men would have you before you got halfway. The only things going through now are the _Covenant_ and the last two data-runs. And the data-runs are the Company's." He drank again. "Besides, who in the Concord would care? You think we're the first? There's a hundred company towns past a hundred throats. Nobody cares about any of them."

She didn't answer. After a while he put his hand on her arm.

"Listen," he said, in a different voice. "I've had an offer."

"What kind of offer?"

"Company work. Hauling." He glanced round; nobody was near. "They want every tug that can push a load moving cargo from the aft stores up to the fore dock. Fuel cassettes. Out of the stores and into the _Covenant_'s hold. Hundreds of them. They're paying in hard credit, Dag. Concord credit. Not chits. Real money, that spends on the other side."

She stared at him. "They're taking the fuel."

"Some of it. They say they're leaving enough for the Wick. Three hundred years, at the length she burns now." He shrugged. "They're taking the rest. It's theirs. They bought it."

"And you're going to haul it for them."

"I'm going to get paid for hauling it for them." His hand tightened on her arm. "Think, Dag. Think about what hard credit's good for. On this side of the Throat it's worth nothing. On the other side it's worth everything. If a man had enough of it—"

"There's no other side, Kas. Not for us. You just said so."

"Not for us," he agreed, and let go of her arm, and smiled, and the smile was the one he had used on her since he was five years old and had broken something. "Course not. Drink your beer."

## Under the Bell
[
Place: #The Dock Ring
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Odile Marchbank @@Pim Ostrova
Items: ^The Mercy Bell
When: same day
]

She found Pim on the way home, asleep on the bench by the Bell Hook with his school bag for a pillow and his collection of bolts spilling out of his pocket. Odile was sitting beside him with her engraver in her lap, not working, just sitting, the way old people did.

"I let him stay," Odile said. "He wanted to see the new name. It's a woman from the stills. Bronwen Price. Her heart."

"Thank you."

"He asked me why we cut the names."

"What did you tell him?"

Odile looked up at the bell, brown and furred and silent on its iron hook above them. "I told him what my master told me. A ship's bell remembers its crew. Everybody that sailed on her. Everybody that died aboard. When the ship's gone, the bell's what's left, and the names are what it remembers." She sniffed. "He said Lantern isn't a ship. I said it was the only ship any of us has got."

Dagny sat down on the other side of her sleeping son, and for a while the three of them stayed there, under the bell, in the dim light of the ring at night.

"They're leaving the Tally to count what we owe them," Dagny said at last. "Every hour of air, for six hundred years."

"So I hear."

"Somebody ought to count what we're owed."

Odile turned her head and looked at her, with her old faded eyes, for a long moment. Then she looked up at the bell.

"I'm running out of bell," she said.
