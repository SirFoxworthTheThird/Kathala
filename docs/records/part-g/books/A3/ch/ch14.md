## Ninian
[
Place: #The Wick
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Orrin Sallow @@Ninian
Items: ^Fuel Cassettes
When: +3 days
]

The last master Wickwright on Lantern lived in a tin hut on the master ring at the root of the Wick, and he had not come down in eleven years.

Tamsin had never been up the spine. Nobody from below Fifteen had. The spoke-shafts were Count property, and the spine-road at the top of them ran past the Counting House, and the bailiffs on the spine-road knew a cutter's rig when they saw one. So they went up the way Orrin knew, which was the way counters went: through the Counting House by the service stair, at the change of third shift, with Orrin in his grey coat walking a little ahead and Tamsin a pace behind in a borrowed grey coat of his that was too long in the sleeves, carrying a slate she could not read, with her eyes down. Nobody stopped them. Nobody looked twice at two counters. That was the thing about the Count, Orrin had said on the stair, not quite joking: it had never once imagined that anybody would want to be in it who was not.

At the top of the service stair there was a door, and beyond the door there was the light.

She had lived her whole life under the Wick. She had never been close to it. It was the difference between a fire seen across a valley and a fire you could put your hand in. The lamp went up from the master ring in front of her, a column of white so bright it had no edges, straight up, into a sky that was not a sky but the far side of the world — terraces, farms, roofs, tanks, all hanging overhead, all upside down, all lit — and dwindling away along the axis, kilometre after kilometre, smaller and smaller, until two-thirds of the way down it simply stopped. Beyond the stop was the dark. From here, at the root, she could see the whole length of what was left: the lamp, the light, the Galleries under it; and beyond it, at the aft end of the world, the Cold, a great grey bowl of shadow, like the inside of a cup.

There was no weight. She had not been ready for that either. Her stomach turned over, and her feet lifted a little off the grating, and Orrin caught her sleeve and clipped her to the rail with a tether from his belt, the way someone must have taught him to.

"Don't look down," he said. "There isn't a down. That's what makes it worse."

The hut was on the far side of the master ring, a box of corrugated tin welded to the catwalk, with a stovepipe and a door made of an old collimator cover. The man who opened it was the oldest person Tamsin had ever seen. He was tiny, bent, bald, with skin like folded paper, and his eyes were milky white from edge to edge, both of them, with no colour left in them at all.

"Counter," said Ninian. He had not looked at them; he was looking at a point between them, a little to the left. "You're the one who's been reading my light ledger."

Orrin went still. "How do you—"

"Counters' boots. Soft soles. And you breathe like a man who's been sitting down all his life. And you've got somebody with you who hasn't." He turned his blind eyes toward Tamsin, not quite on her. "Cutter. You smell of the Cold. Copper and frost." He stood back from the door. "Come in, then. I don't get visitors. I've got tea."

## Ninian's Tea

Items: ^Fuel Cassettes
When: same day
]

The tea was terrible. The hut was warm, and smelled of hot metal and old man, and every surface in it was covered with tools Tamsin did not know the names of, laid out in rows so precise that she understood at once that he found every one of them by touch. Ninian sat on his cot with his cup in both hands and listened while Orrin told him. He did not interrupt. When Orrin got to the stencil on the floor of the fifth hall, and the fourteen units, he set his cup down very carefully on the floor between his feet.

"Fourteen," he said.

"In the Hulk's reactor. Held. Since the Founding."

The old man was silent for a long time. Then he laughed — a thin, dry, terrible sound, with no breath in it.

"I asked," he said. "Did you know that? Forty years ago. When I was made master. I went to the Comptroller-General of the day — not Vosk, the one before the one before him — and I said, _There's a line in the light ledger I don't understand. Reserve. Held. What's held?_ And he told me it was a story. A Founders' story. A line they'd carried forward for luck. He said, _You're a Wickwright, Ninian, you keep the lamp. Leave the ledgers to us._ And I did." He turned his white eyes toward the doorway, toward the blaze of the lamp outside. "I've shortened her six times since then. Six times I've gone down the spine with my crew and closed the collimators on the aft end and watched another two hundred metres of her go dark, and another gallery go cold. Every time, the Count told me there was nothing else. Every time I believed it." He picked up the cup again. His hands were quite steady. "Fourteen."

"Can the Wick take them?" said Tamsin. "If we got them up here. Could you load them?"

"Could I load them." He said it flatly, as if she had asked whether he could breathe. "Girl, I've loaded every cassette that's gone into her for fifty years. There's a port at the master ring. The Founders' port. Right there." He pointed, without looking, at the wall of the hut, beyond which, she supposed, it was. "She takes them one at a time, through the port, into the bottle. Each one burns about a third of her length for three years, or the whole of her for one. Fourteen—" He stopped. "Fourteen would light her end to end. All the way down. For fourteen years. The Cold Wards and all."

Nobody said anything. Outside, the lamp hummed.

"But you'd have to get them here," said Ninian. "And they're not light. Each one's the weight of four men, and the size of a coffin, and hot, a little, always, even asleep. You don't carry a cassette. You move it on a sled, on rails, with a winch. And the Hulk's under the Counting House, and the Counting House is the Count."

"There's a lift," said Orrin. "On the old plans. In the archive. A freight lift from the Hulk's reactor deck straight up to the spine-road, beside the Counting House. It's marked _sealed_."

"The Founders' lift." Ninian nodded slowly. "I know it. There's a door on the spine-road. Big. Welded. I've walked past it every day of my life." He was quiet again. "You'd have to come up from inside the Hulk. From frame forty. Through the Sanctum."

"Yes."

"Nobody goes through the Sanctum but the Comptroller-General."

"Yes," said Orrin.

The old Wickwright sat with his cup in his hands and his blind eyes on the doorway for a long time. Then he said, so quietly that Tamsin almost did not hear it over the hum of the lamp:

"Do you know the Twilight Song?"

Tamsin did. Everybody below Fifteen did. It was one of the old ones, from before the Count, that they sang at the Hook sometimes: a slow, low tune, about a time when the Wick crews had turned the lamp down to a dusk and the whole world had stood on its terraces and looked up at the light and seen it, for once, as a thing someone had made. Nobody knew when it was from. Nobody knew if it had happened.

"My master taught me that the Wick crews made it," said Ninian. "In the Closing year. Before the Count. The crews took the lamp down, to make the Company listen. And the Company didn't, and men died on the spine, and the crews brought her back up." He turned his head toward Tamsin, and for a moment his white eyes seemed almost to find her. "But they took her down. For three days, they took her down. Because she was theirs." He set the cup on the floor again. "I'll load your fourteen, cutter. If you bring them. I'll load every one of them if it's the last thing I do. It will be, probably. I'm ninety."

## Payment
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Orrin Sallow
Items: ^Tally Chit
When: +1 day
]

The second thousand went into the Cold at second shift the next day. Agathe Hask was in the third, in three days.

Tamsin tried to pay.

She had known it would not work. She did it anyway, because her mother had been a churchgoer for thirty years and had believed in the Count's arithmetic as she believed in the air, and Tamsin wanted, once, to have done the thing the Count said you could do, so that afterwards she would know. She sold the copper. All of it: the stash under the floor of the Hask room that she had been keeping for the winter, eighteen metres of it, to Brannock at the Salt Docks for washers, and the washers at the chit-shop on Nineteen at a quarter off. She sold her spare rig. She sold the good saw, and kept the old one. She sold her mother's winter coat, which her mother would not need where she was going — the thought came to her like that, plain and cold, and she sold the coat. When she was done she had three hundred and eleven credits on her own chit. It was more money than she had ever held at once in her life.

The Count's public window was on the ground floor of the Counting House, at the end of the third hall, behind a brass grille. There was a queue. There had been a queue every day since the lists went up — people from the third list and the fourth and the fifth, with whatever they had been able to sell, trying to buy their way back over the line. Tamsin queued for three hours. When she got to the grille the counter behind it was a heavy grey-haired man with ink on his cuffs, and she knew him at once, from the table at the Lip.

"Payment," she said. "Against the account of Hask, Agathe. From Hask, Tamsin."

Senior Counter Abney took her chit and touched it to his slate, and the slate chimed. He looked at it.

"Three hundred and eleven," he said. "Posted. The account of Hask, Agathe, now stands at minus ten thousand eight hundred and ninety-five."

"So she's off the list."

"No."

Tamsin stood very still.

"Payments during a Calling are accepted and posted," said Abney, in the voice of a man who has said the same sentence two thousand times in three days and will say it two thousand more. "They reduce the balance. They do not alter the order of the lists, which was fixed at the time of posting. Hask, Agathe, is on the third list. The third list goes on the twenty-fourth."

"Then what's the payment for?"

"It reduces the balance," said Abney, "which will be released from the Tally's charge on the twenty-fourth, and held quit by the Account." He handed her back her chit. Her own balance on it read zero. "Next."

She did not move. Behind her the queue shifted and muttered.

"You taught him long division," she said.

Abney looked at her properly for the first time. His eyes were small and grey and tired.

"Who?"

"Orrin Sallow. Your counter. Third hall. He says you're the most careful man he knows." She leaned close to the grille. "He found an error in her account. Two thousand credits that aren't hers. You refused it. And now she goes into the dark on the twenty-fourth with two thousand on her that she doesn't owe, and three hundred of mine that she does, and there's not a thing in your whole careful Count that will stop it. Is that right? Have I done the sum right?"

Senior Counter Abney was silent for a moment. Then he said, quite quietly, without any change of expression: "Yes. You've done it right."

"Next," he said, past her.

She walked out of the Counting House into the white light of the Upper Wards with her empty chit in her fist. Orrin was waiting on the steps. He had watched it all through the window of the third hall; she could see it on his face. He did not say anything. He fell into step beside her and they walked down the terraces together, out of the light, toward the galleries, in silence, all the way to Fifteen.

## The Salt's Price
[
Place: #The Salt Docks
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Orrin Sallow @@Wenna Blackwood @@Jory Hask
Items: ^Fuel Cassettes
When: same day
]

"Ten," said Wenna Blackwood.

They were behind the cargo-net curtain at the back of Brannock's cabin, the four of them — Tamsin and Orrin on one side of the table, Wenna on the other, Jory on an upturned crate by the stove eating the goat stew nobody else had touched — and Wenna had just been told about the fourteen.

She had listened to all of it without a word. When Orrin got to the stencil she had shut her one eye for a moment, as if something hurt. When he got to the auxiliary lamp in the Upper Wards she had laughed, once, softly. And when he had finished, and Tamsin had told her what they meant to do, she had sat back in her chair and folded her great scarred arms and said: "Ten."

"Ten what?" said Orrin.

"Ten of the fourteen. To the Salt. You want my people to go into the Hulk with you, under the Count, past Lusk and every bailiff he's got, and bring out fourteen cassettes the size of coffins and get them up the spine on sleds. That's a war, counter. That's my people's lives. Ten." She shrugged. "You can have four for your blind old man and his lamp."

"Four's a year at full length," said Tamsin. "Barely three at a third."

"Then he'd better make them last."

"And what do you do with ten?" said Orrin. "There's nobody to sell them to. There's no market for fuel on Lantern except the Count."

"Then I sell them to the Count." Wenna smiled, without any warmth. "At a price. Or I build a lamp of my own on the Salt Docks, and light it, and see how many people come aft along the ring road to live under it, and what they'll pay." She leaned forward. "You think I'm a bad woman, counter. I can see it on your soft face. I'm not. I'm a practical one. The Count's going to put four thousand people in the light and leave the rest to the dark. I'm offering to put a few thousand more in a different light. Mine. That's not a lifeboat with a man at the door. That's two lifeboats."

"With a man at each door," said Tamsin.

Wenna looked at her with her one eye. "There's always a man at the door, girl. Always. The only question's whether you're on the inside of it when it shuts."

Nobody said anything. By the stove Jory had stopped eating.

"Seven," said Tamsin at last. "Seven to the Wick. Seven to you. And you light yours on the Salt Docks, and you open it to everyone, Owing or Called or whoever comes aft, and you don't charge for the light."

"I don't charge for the light," Wenna repeated, as if testing the taste of it.

"You've never charged for it. You've never had any. Start as you mean to go on."

The silence went on for a long time. Then Wenna Blackwood laughed — the big gravel laugh — and slapped the table so hard the stew jumped in the pot.

"Agathe Hask's girl," she said. "God help the Count." She put out her hand. "Seven. And I'll see you on the spine."

Tamsin took it.

## The Steps
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Orrin Sallow @@Brother Lusk
When: same day
]

Orrin went back up to the Counting House alone, late, through the back of the Upper Wards, with the old freight-lift plans folded flat inside his shirt where he had carried them all day, and his head full of frame numbers and sled weights and the timing of the bailiffs' rounds in the fifth hall. He was thinking so hard about all of it that he did not see the man on the steps until he was almost on top of him.

Brother Lusk was sitting on the top step of the Counting House, in the white light, with his docket-baton across his knees. He did not get up.

"Counter Sallow," he said. He had a slow, mild, unremarkable voice. Orrin had never heard it before; Lusk did not, as a rule, talk to counters. "You've been going down a great deal."

Orrin stopped. "I've had sightings."

"Have you." Lusk looked at him, pleasantly, from under his pale brows. "Twenty-Two, mostly. And the Salt Docks. I didn't know the Count had accounts on the Salt Docks."

"It— there are some. Yes."

"I'm sure there are." Lusk turned the baton over in his big pale hands, once, and laid it back across his knees. "The galleries are no place for a counter, Counter Sallow. Not these days. People are upset. Things happen to men in good coats, down there. It would grieve the Comptroller-General very much if anything happened to you. He thinks highly of you. He's told me so."

"Thank you, Brother."

"Owing and owed," said Brother Lusk.

"Owing and owed."

He went up the steps past him, and in at the door, and along the third hall, and sat down at his desk in the dark with his hands flat on the slate and the plans pressing into his chest under his shirt, and did not move for a long time, because he could not make his hands stop shaking.
