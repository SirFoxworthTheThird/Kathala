## The Marine
[
Place: #The Wick
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Hale Varga
When: +6 days
]

The Wick crews kept a locker room at the foot of the spine, a long low cave of steel cabinets and benches where they changed in and out of their rigs, and since her door on Gallery Six had dimmed Dagny had slept there, on a bench, with Pim on the next bench and her coat for a blanket. It was warm. The lamp's waste heat came down through the rock. That was something.

Varga came at the start of third shift, when the room was empty. She woke because the door opened and a big shape filled it against the white light of the spine-road, and she was on her feet with a wrench in her hand before she knew she was awake.

"Put it down," said Hale Varga. "If I'd come to take you, I wouldn't have come alone."

He was not in armour. He was in Company grey fatigues with the sleeves rolled, and he was alone, and he stood in the doorway with his hands open at his sides where she could see them. Behind her, on the other bench, Pim had sat up.

"Outside," said Dagny.

They stood on the spine-road with the Wick going up over them into the white, and the long curve of the world falling away on every side. Varga looked up at it for a while before he spoke. He had the face of a man who had not slept either.

"I know you went to Caul," he said.

She said nothing.

"I took it off the log. The flight. And the camera on the comptroller's floor. Nobody else knows." He did not look at her. "I wanted you to know that I know. And that I took it off."

"Why?"

"Because I was there." He said it flatly. "At the three-hundred-metre station. I took the squad down. We got there twenty hours after the blowout. There were eleven of them at the lift station, on our side of the ice. Alive. Hurt, but alive. Behind them the shaft was solid. You've seen it."

"I've seen it."

"I called up to the Director. I told her I had eleven alive and could bring them up. She asked me if they'd seen the shaft. I said yes. She asked me if they'd talk." He was quiet for a moment. "She said the seal was to be poured at the station. She said it had to be poured at the station, because the shaft below was unstable, and a seal any higher wouldn't hold. She said the eleven were to be— she said they'd been exposed to the pressure ice and would not survive the climb anyway, and it would be kinder."

"And you poured it."

"I poured it." He turned his head and looked at her for the first time. His eyes were very pale in the white light. "My squad poured it. I gave the order. We put the hatch in and welded it, and they were on the other side of it, and we could hear them. Then we went up the ladder. It's three hundred metres. You can hear a long way, in a shaft."

Dagny did not say anything. She thought of the marks on the inside of the hatch. Five, then five more.

"I'm not telling you so you'll forgive me," said Varga. "I don't want that. I'm telling you because you're going to try something, and Basu's going to help you, and I want you to try it knowing the truth. Not the truth you'd like."

"Which is?"

"The inspectors won't save you."

She looked at him.

"I was a Concord marine for twelve years before the Company bought my contract," he said. "I did two tours on throat garrisons. I've seen what happens when the Labour Inspectorate comes through a throat. They'll come. They'll find Caul — you'll show them, and they'll find it. They'll fine the Company. They may put the Director in front of a court; they may not. And then they'll look at the Sabra Concern and see nine thousand indentures held in trust by a company that's about to go bankrupt, and they'll do what the Concord Court of Instruments told them to do in the Tannet Yards ruling seven years ago. They'll appoint a receiver. And the receiver will sell the instrument. Your contracts. Your debt. Every chit on every wrist on this rock, sold at auction in a lien-house on Kessane to whoever pays most for it." He shrugged his heavy shoulders. "And the new owners will send their own men. Better men than the Company's, probably. More efficient. Less tired. They'll have a better Tally. They'll take the line down from fifteen hundred back to nine. And they'll run Lantern for a hundred years, or two hundred, or six, until there's nothing left in it worth having."

The Wick hummed over their heads. Somewhere down the spine-road a crew was coming off shift, laughing at something.

"There is no rescue through that door, Ostrova," said Hale Varga. "There's only a different landlord."

She found that her hands had closed into fists, and made them open.

"Then why does it matter?" she said. "Why tell me? If it's the Company or it's the Concord, if it's the same chain either way—"

"Because it isn't the same," said Varga. "Not for them." He turned and looked aft along the spine, past the curve of the rock, as if he could see through it to the white ball of Caul. "If the Throat closes, Caul's under the ice for ever. Nobody ever hears the names. Nobody ever has to answer for it. Not her. Not Basu. Not me." He was quiet for a while. "I've been a soldier all my life. I've done things. I've never had to carry one where nobody would ever even know it had been done."

"So you want the Throat open."

"I don't know what I want." He said it simply, the way a man says he does not know the time. "I know what I did. That's all I know. I'll be on the _Covenant_ when she goes. I'm on the list. I don't know what I'll do when I'm on her." He looked back at her. "I just wanted you to know where the floor was. Before you jumped."

He went away up the spine-road without saying goodbye. She stood and watched him go, a big grey shape in the white light, getting smaller, until he turned in at the Counting House door and was gone.

## Dimmed
[
Place: #The Galleries
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Pim Ostrova
Items: ^Tally Chit
When: same day
]

The Tally dimmed Pim on the thirty-fourth day.

She found out at noon, when he came back to the locker room from school three hours early with his face very white and his chit held out in front of him like something he had found dead. The little screen on it was grey. When she touched it to the reader on the wall, the reader told her, in its polite blue letters, that account Ostrova, Pim, dependant of Ostrova, Dagny, had passed the dimming line at the eleven o'clock posting, following apportionment of strike-related losses to dependant accounts under section forty of the Concern's charter.

"They wouldn't let me in," he said. "The school door. It said I'd have to see a counter. Mister Okonkwo came out and said he was sorry and it wasn't up to him." He swallowed. "Everybody was looking."

"I know."

"I didn't strike. I wasn't even on the Wick. I was just—" He stopped.

"You were on the Wick," said Dagny. "At the end."

"Because you were there!" It came out of him in a shout, the first time he had raised his voice since the strike, and he looked shocked at it himself. "Because you were there and they— and Joss—" And then he was crying, at last, the way he had not cried on the Wick or at the bell or since, great ugly gulping sobs that bent him over, and she held him on the bench in the empty locker room with the waste heat of the lamp coming down through the rock round them, and let him.

When it had worn itself out she took him to a counter.

There was one at the chit-shop on Gallery Five, a tired man with a tired slate behind a grille, and Dagny put her own chit and Pim's on the counter in front of him and said she wanted to transfer six hundred from her account to her son's. The counter looked at the chits. He touched them to his slate. He looked at the slate for a long time.

"I can't," he said.

"I'm in credit. Just. I've got six hundred and eleven."

"You're flagged." He turned the slate a little so she could see it, and kept his voice low. "Every Speaker who voted for the strike. Transfers out are frozen for the rest of the closure. Director's order. I'm sorry."

"He's twelve."

"I know how old he is." The counter looked at Pim, and away. "I've got three of my own. I'm sorry."

So she took him back to the locker room and gave him her ration — the cold bean paste and the hard bread that the Tally still issued to her because she was still, by eleven credits, on the right side of the line — and watched him eat it, and did not eat anything herself. She was not hungry. She was something else, something hard and clear and cold, like the ice in the shaft at Caul.

"Mum," said Pim, when he had finished.

"What?"

"Is Uncle Kas going on the _Covenant_?"

She looked at him.

"People are saying." He was picking at the bread crumbs on the bench, not looking at her. "At school. Abi Adeyemi's aunt does nights loading the harvest up at the fore dock. She says Uncle Kas has been hauling for the Company, every day, fuel and stuff, up to the _Covenant_. She says he drinks with the Company men after. In their bar. In the Upper Wards." He looked up. "She says he's been asking about seats."

"People say a lot of things."

"I know." He looked down again. "I just wanted to know if it's true."

"I'll ask him," said Dagny.

## The Founders' Band
[
Place: #The Dock Ring
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Odile Marchbank @@Pim Ostrova
Items: ^The Mercy Bell ^The Caul List
When: same day
]

She brought Odile the folder that night.

The old woman took it on the bench under the bell and opened it on her knees and turned the pages under the dim night-lamps of the ring, one after another, slowly, with her lips moving. Teodor had typed them in Company format, surname first, in alphabetical order, with a payroll number beside each and, for some, a Gallery address on Lantern where somebody had once been sent a letter. Odile did not say anything for a long time. She turned pages.

"Nine hundred and four," she said at last.

"Yes."

"I've cut a hundred names in a year. In a bad year. When the stills fever came, in seventy-one, I cut a hundred and forty, and my hands were no good for a month after." She closed the folder. "You've got eighteen days."

"I know."

"I'm seventy-four, Dagny."

"I know."

Odile looked up at the bell. Brown and furred and silent on its iron hook, with Joss's name bright on its shoulder, and Lemuel's, and Bronwen Price's, and above them, round the crown, the last of the room.

"There's no bell left," she said.

"There's the band."

"The founders' band." Odile's voice went sharp. "Ninety-one years, nobody's touched the founders' band. My master told me. His master told him. The _Mercy_'s people left it plain when they cut the crossing round the lip, so there'd be one clean thing on her, for the founding. You don't cut it."

"The founders are dead," said Dagny. "These are dead. Every one of them. Under the ice, standing up, with their eyes open. The Company wrote them out. The Throat's going to bury them. Nobody's ever going to know their names except you and me and a man who forged their letters home." She put her hand on the folder in Odile's lap. "Cut the band, Odile. Please."

The old woman looked at her for a long, long time.

Then she looked past her, at Pim, who was sitting on the end of the bench where he always sat, with his school bag at his feet and his dimmed chit on its cord round his neck.

"How steady are your hands, boy?" said Odile Marchbank.

## Achebe, Ruth
[
Place: #The Dock Ring
Characters: @@Odile Marchbank @@Pim Ostrova @@Dagny Ostrova
Items: ^The Mercy Bell ^The Caul List
When: same day
]

They began at midnight.

Odile cut the first name herself. _Abara, Joseph._ She stood on her box under the bell with the engraver in her right hand and her left flat on the bronze, on the plain cast belt round the bell's waist that nobody had touched in ninety-one years, and set the burr to it, and the whine went up into the night ring. The letters came up bright. When she had finished she stood back and looked at them, and her face did not change at all, and Dagny could not tell whether she was grieving or furious or both.

"Next," Odile said, and held out the engraver to Pim.

He cut _Achebe, Ruth_. It took him forty minutes. The letters were uneven, too deep at the start and too shallow at the end, and the second _e_ went wandering off the line. Odile stood at his shoulder the whole time with her hand over his, not guiding, only resting there, and when he had finished she looked at it and said, "It's a name. That's all it has to be," and Pim, who had not smiled since the Wick, looked as if he might.

By the end of the night there were four of them. Mariam Oyelowo's daughter Efe came down from the farms, because Mariam had heard; and a widow from Gallery Ten called Grace Mbeki, whose husband had been on Lemuel's pump crew, who had worked fine metal in a jeweller's in the Concord before she came, and whose hands were better than Odile's. They took turns on the box. Two hours on, two off. One read the next name aloud from Teodor's folder; one cut; one rested; one held the lamp. By morning they had cut thirty-one names in a straight cramped line round the founders' band, and Odile was asleep on the bench with her mouth open and her shaking hands folded in her lap.

People came to watch, the next day. By the day after there was a crowd. Nobody on the Dock Ring had ever seen the bell being cut by anyone but the dockmaster, and certainly never round the clock, never like this, never so many names. They did not know whose names they were. Odile would not tell them. _Dead_, she said, if anyone asked. _Ours._ And people stood and watched the engravers take turns on the box, and listened to the names being read out one by one in the night ring, Achebe and Adair and Agbaje and on, and after a while some of them began to stay, and to sit on the ring road with their backs to the rock, and to say each name after the reader, under their breath, the way they said _Owing and owed_ at the bell.

## Two Seats
[
Place: #The Dock Ring
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Kas Ostrova @@Pim Ostrova
Items: ^The Mercy Bell
When: +1 day
]

She found Kas in the Slake two nights later, and he was not playing shove, and he was not losing. He was sitting at the end of the counter with a cup of the good Concord spirit in front of him — the real stuff, amber, that cost a week's wage a glass and was only ever served in the Upper Wards — and he was alone.

He saw her in the mirror behind the bar and did not turn round.

"Pim says you've been asking about seats," she said, and sat down beside him.

"Pim says a lot." He drank. "Clever boy. Gets it from you."

"Is it true?"

Kas turned the glass in his fingers for a while. Then he said, in a light easy voice that she had known since he was five, "Course I've asked. Everyone who's hauled for them's asked. They tell you there's no seats, there's never going to be seats, and then they pour you another one of these." He lifted the glass. "Hard credit, Dag. Spends on the other side. Not on this. I'm trying to drink it all before the door shuts."

She looked at him. He looked back at her in the mirror, with his handsome tired face and his easy smile, and she could not read him at all, for the first time in her life.

"I need you," she said.

"Course you do."

"Not for hauling. For a run." She leaned close, and kept her voice under the noise of the bar. "The Throat isn't closing, Kas. The Company's closing it. There's a shear on the _Covenant_ — a military thing, from the wars, Basu found it — and she's going to fire it behind her when she goes through, to bury Caul." She watched his face in the mirror. It did not change. "And Varga says the Concord won't save us. If the Throat stays open, they'll just sell us. Some lien-house on Kessane. Same chain, new name."

"Varga says."

"I believe him."

"So what's left?"

"Two things." She held up a finger. "One. On the morning the _Covenant_ leaves, the Director and Basu go down to the root together to close the Company's books. Both keys, one room, door open. We go in after them. Basu gives us his. We take hers. And we take the line out of the Tally — for good, for everybody, before they ever get on that ship." Another finger. "Two. The bell."

"The bell?"

"Odile's cutting them, Kas. The Caul dead. Every one of them, round the founders' band. Nine hundred and four. They'll be done by the last morning, if she lives." She held his eyes in the mirror. "And then somebody's going to take that bell through the Throat. In the _Covenant_'s wake, before the shear goes. A hauler can't make a throat on her own. But in a big ship's wake, with the lips held open for her—"

"The _Magpie_," said Kas.

"The _Magpie_. Out through the Throat, behind them, with the bell in her tank-frame. So the names get out. So there's one thing on the other side, in the Concord, that says what the Company did. Let them close the door on us. Fine. Let them bury us. But the names get out, and somebody has to answer for them." She drew breath. "Even if it's never us. Even if we never know."

Kas didn't say anything for a long time. He finished the amber spirit in his glass, slowly, and set it down on the heat-shield counter with a small click.

"Out through the Throat," he said. "In her wake. With the shear about to go off behind me."

"Yes."

"And then?"

"And then you're in the Concord. With the bell. And you find somebody who'll listen."

"And I never come back."

"No," said Dagny. "Nobody does."

He turned, then, and looked at her — not in the mirror, straight on — and for a moment his face was not easy at all; it was younger, and frightened, and very like their mother's.

"All right," he said. "All right, Dag. I'll fly it."

She put her hand over his on the counter. He let it stay there for a moment. Then he slid his own out from under it, and smiled — the smile from when he was five and had broken something — and signalled the barman for another glass of the amber.

In the doorway of the Slake, where neither of them saw him, Pim stood with his dimmed chit in his fist and watched his uncle's face in the mirror behind the bar, and did not come in.
