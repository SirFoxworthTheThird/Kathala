## The Vote
[
Place: #The Galleries
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Joss Teague
When: +8 days
]

They met in the old chapel on Gallery Four, because it was the only room on Lantern with no Company camera in it. The Company had never thought a chapel worth watching.

It was a long room cut into the rock with a low ceiling and benches made of hauler crate-wood, and at the far end, where in a chapel on a planet there might have been an altar, there was a window of coloured resin somebody had poured in the colony's first decade: a crude picture of a ship crossing a dark sea toward a lamp. The _Ardent Mercy_, coming to Lantern. The resin had yellowed. The lamp in the picture had gone the colour of tea. Under it, on the night of the fourteenth day of the Closing, sixty-one Speakers from every crew on the rock sat shoulder to shoulder on the crate-wood benches and argued for four hours.

Dagny let them. She had learned that from Hesketh, years ago, when she was a new Speaker and wanted every meeting to end in the first ten minutes. _People need to say it_, the old man had told her. _Even if it's been said. Especially if it's been said. You can't ask a man to risk his door for a thing he hasn't heard himself say out loud._ So she let them say it. That the Company was leaving. That the debt was staying. That the dimming line was a rope with a noose on the end, and that six hundred extra credits only made the rope longer. That there were children on Gallery Nine already sleeping in the corridors because their doors had dimmed. That the farms couldn't feed the rock without the Company's refinery-tech. That the stills couldn't run without the Company's spare parts. That the Company would take the parts. That the Company would take the fuel. That the Company would take everything except the debt and the dead.

At the end of the fourth hour, when people had stopped saying new things and begun saying old ones again more loudly, Mariam Oyelowo stood up in the front row, slow and broad, and said:

"The _Covenant_ eats."

The room quietened. Mariam did not raise her voice; she never did.

"Fourteen hundred people, eight months to the nearest Concord port, that's what my sister in the Company kitchens says. Fourteen hundred mouths for eight months. Where's that coming from? Not the Concord. There's no ship from the Concord. It's coming off the strip-farms. Off my farms. The Company's been taking our harvest into the _Covenant_'s cold holds for a week. They need two more harvests to provision her. Maybe three." She looked round at them, her face heavy and calm. "Farms don't grow in the dark."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then everybody turned and looked at Dagny.

"The Wick," said somebody at the back.

"The Wick," said Dagny.

She stood up. Her knees hurt; they always did, now, after a long sitting. She went to the front, under the yellowed window, and turned and faced them.

"I can bring the lamp down," she said. "Not out. Never out — if it goes out, it may not light again, and then we're all dead in a week and the Company's won. But down. To a twilight. Enough to keep the scrubbers' bacteria alive and the water from freezing in the mains. Not enough to grow a crop." She looked at Mariam. "How long before the farms suffer?"

"Three days, the first beds start to turn. Ten and the harvest's gone."

"Then we tell them we'll bring it back up," Dagny said, "the day the Director goes into the root of the Tally with her seal and takes the dimming line out of it. Not lower it. Take it out. Nobody on this rock dimmed again, for anything, ever. Let them have their trust. Let them have their debt on paper and their books full of numbers. But nobody goes into a lock again and doesn't come out because the number's wrong."

"She'll say she can't," said Hesketh, from his bench, leaning on his stick.

"She can. Basu told me so." It was the first time she had said it aloud, and she felt the room take it in. "With the seal, at the root, she can do anything the Tally can do. She can take the line out. She won't, because a debt nobody's made to pay is worth nothing to anybody who might buy it. That's the only reason. Not the air. Not the safety. The price." She held up her wrist, the chit on it. "So we make the price higher."

There was a long silence. Then Joss Teague, sitting on the end of the second bench, with his bad knees stuck out into the aisle, said in his big cracked voice: "Wick crews are in."

"Pumps are in," said Hesketh.

"Farms," said Mariam.

"Stills."

"Docks."

It went round the room. Not everybody. The hauler crews held back, and the noodle-sellers' association, and a few of the old Gallery Two families who had lived nearest the Upper Wards for three generations and were not going to start being brave now. But enough. Dagny counted the hands, and counted them again, and then looked up at the tea-coloured lamp in the window over her head and thought, absurdly, that she ought to say a prayer, and did not know one.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Second shift. We bring her down."

## Twilight
[
Place: #The Wick
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Joss Teague @@Pim Ostrova
When: +1 day
]

There were forty collimators on the Wick, ring after ring of them down the three-kilometre length of the lamp, each a squat grey housing the size of a cupboard that shaped the magnetic bottle at that point and so decided how much light the lamp gave out there. Every Wick crew on Lantern knew how to trim one. You did it every shift, a little this way, a little that, to keep the light even as the fire inside the bottle wandered. Nobody had ever trimmed all forty at once, together, all the way down.

They did it at the start of second shift. Dagny took the master ring at the root of the lamp, by the Counting House, where she could see the whole length of the spine. Joss had the middle. Every collimator had a crew on it, two or three to a housing, clipped to the rails in the hard white light, waiting with their hands on the trim wheels and their eyes on her.

She raised her arm. She lowered it.

The light went down.

Not fast. The fire inside the bottle did not like to be squeezed, and fought it, and they had to coax it, a quarter-turn at a time, watching the gauges, easing it down by degrees. But it went. Dagny felt it before she saw it — a coolness on the backs of her hands, on her face, as if a cloud had passed over a sun she had never seen. Then the shadows began to lengthen. Then, all along the curving walls of the world, the terraces went from white to gold, and from gold to amber, and from amber to a deep slow blue, like the dusk in the picture-books Pim had read when he was small, from planets, where the sun went down.

It took twenty minutes. When it was done Lantern lay in twilight, and the Wick hung down the middle of it not as a sword any longer but as a dim, smouldering bar, like a coal blown on and left.

Dagny clipped off at the master ring and looked down.

She had expected noise. Shouting, sirens, the Company screens all lighting up at once. There was nothing. All over the rock, on every terrace, people had come out of their houses and were standing looking up. She could see them. Thousands of them, small and still, faces turned toward the lamp. Nobody spoke. Nobody moved. On Lantern you were taught at five not to look straight at the Wick, because it would burn your eyes; and here were nine thousand people looking straight at it, and it was not burning, and they could see it, for the first time in their lives, as a thing that someone had made.

On the Gallery Six terrace somebody began to sing. Dagny could not hear the words from so high. It was one of the old crossing songs, she thought, from the tune — one of the ones the _Mercy_'s people had brought, about a ship and a dark sea and a light on the far shore. Another voice took it up. Then a gallery's worth. It went round the curve of the world, terrace to terrace, under the dim red lamp, the way the bell's voice went round the Dock Ring, until it seemed to Dagny that the whole rock was singing, very quietly, to itself.

"Mum."

Pim was beside her on the master ring. He was not supposed to be on the Wick at all, let alone at the root of it, but he had come up the spoke-shaft behind the crews and nobody had had the heart to send him down. He was looking at the twilight with his mouth open.

"It's beautiful," he said. "Why's it beautiful?"

"Because it's ours," said Dagny, and was surprised to find her throat had closed on it.

Below them, across the gulf of the dim air, in the Counting House, the long window of the Director's office was a strip of hard white light: the only full-lit thing left on Lantern. While she watched, a figure came and stood in it, small and dark against the light, and stood there, looking up the length of the dimmed Wick toward her, for a long time.

## The Spine
[
Place: #The Wick
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Joss Teague:dead @@Hale Varga @@Pim Ostrova
When: +2 days
]

The Company let it go for two days. On the third, Varga came up the Wick.

He came at shift-change, which was clever: the moment when there were most crews on the spine and least of them clipped on. He came up all four spoke-shafts at once with forty men in grey, in pressure armour, with stunners and with slug-guns slung across their backs, and he came fast. Dagny was at the master ring with the crews when the alarms on the spoke-hatches went, one after another, like bells, and she had time to shout once — "_Clip on! Everybody clip on!_" — before the first of them was out of the shaft and onto the crawl and coming hand over hand toward the collimators.

There was no fighting near the axis. Not real fighting. Nobody had weight there; you couldn't swing a punch, you couldn't plant your feet. What there was, was a long slow struggle of bodies on the rails, crews clinging to their trim wheels and Varga's men prising them off, one finger at a time, and the stunners going with their flat ugly crack, and men and women going limp on their tethers and floating out from the crawl like weed in water. It was almost quiet. That was what she remembered afterwards. How quiet it was.

She saw it happen to Joss because she was looking at him. He was on the middle ring, a hundred metres along, with two of his crew; he had his big arms wrapped round the housing of the twentieth collimator and his face turned toward her, and he was grinning, the mad grin he had when a job went bad and there was nothing to do but enjoy it. A man in grey was coming along the crawl toward him. Joss let go of the housing with one arm and swung at him — a stupid, slow, weightless swing that would never have connected — and the man in grey, startled, did what he had been trained to do with a target that moved. He fired.

Not the stunner. The slug-gun.

The shot made almost no sound in the thin air at the axis. Joss jerked once. Then he let go of the housing. His tether had not been clipped — he had unclipped it to swing — and he simply drifted out, away from the crawl, away from the lamp, turning very slowly, with his arms spread and his big body loose, into the open air at the centre of the world.

Dagny screamed his name. She did not remember doing it. She remembered the sound coming back to her off the curve of the rock.

At the axis, a body with no weight can drift for a long time. Then the spin finds it. Joss drifted out from the Wick for nearly a minute, so slowly that she thought — she let herself think — that somebody would reach him, that a crew would get a line to him, that he would come back. Then the slow invisible hand of the rock's turning took hold of him, and he began, very gently at first, to fall. Not down. Out. Toward the curving wall of the world, toward the terraces of the Galleries, which were spinning round underneath him at four hundred kilometres an hour and which he would meet, when he met them, at that speed.

She made herself watch. Someone had to. He fell for a long time, getting smaller, turning, through the twilight. She lost him against the terraces of Gallery Nineteen. She did not see him land. She saw, a long way down, a sudden small stir among the people on the terrace there, like a stone thrown into a pond.

When she turned round, Hale Varga was on the master ring beside her, clipped on, in his pressure armour with the faceplate up. His face was grey. He was looking where she had looked.

"I didn't order that," he said.

"You brought guns onto the Wick."

"I didn't order it." He turned his head and looked at her, and there was something in his face she had not expected and did not want: a sort of sickness. "Bring the lamp up, Ostrova. Please. Before anybody else goes off it."

She looked past him. All along the spine his men held the collimators now, their crews stunned on their tethers or pinned against the rails. Down at the far end of the master ring a man in grey had his arm across the throat of a boy, holding him clipped to the rail, and the boy was Pim.

"Let him go," she said.

"Bring the lamp up."

She brought the lamp up.

## The Price of Light
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Ruth Achterberg @@Hale Varga
When: same day
]

They took her to the Counting House afterwards, to the long window, and the Director told her what it would cost.

Every Speaker who had voted for the strike: five hundred credits, posted to their accounts at the hour, for the loss of three days' light to the Company's farms. Every crewman who had held a collimator: two hundred. The farms' lost harvest, which was the Company's property, apportioned across every account on the rock according to its share of the light. When the Tally had finished posting, four hundred and eleven doors on Lantern had dimmed that had not been dimmed the day before. Dagny's was one of them.

"I'm sorry about Mr. Teague," said Ruth Achterberg. She stood at her window with her back to the room, looking up the length of the Wick, which blazed again white and hard from end to end as if nothing had happened. "I did not want anyone hurt. Mr. Varga has disciplined the man who fired. I want you to understand that I have nothing against you, Ms. Ostrova. You were doing what your people asked. I'm doing what mine ask. In thirty-five days we'll both be relieved of it."

"You could take the line out," said Dagny. Her voice came out cracked, as if it belonged to someone else. "Today. Go down to the root and take it out. Nobody dimmed again. What does it cost you? You'll be gone."

The Director turned from the window.

"It costs a debt that nobody has to pay," she said. "And a debt that nobody has to pay is not a debt. It's a story. The Company does not hold stories in trust, Ms. Ostrova. It holds assets. You have a son, I understand. When he is grown, and the Throat opens, and somebody comes to ask what this place is worth, I'd like there to be an answer. That's all the line is for." She sat down at her bare desk. "Mr. Varga will see you down."

Varga saw her down. In the lift he stood with his faceplate under his arm and said nothing at all until the doors opened on the spine-road at the foot of the Wick. Then, as she stepped out, he said, low, not looking at her:

"His name's Corrie. The man who shot your friend. He's twenty-two. He's from Gallery Eleven."

"Is that supposed to make it better?"

"No," said Varga. "It's supposed to make you know who did it. Somebody ought to." And the doors closed.

## The Shape of the Keys
[
Place: #The Dock Ring
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Teodor Basu @@Odile Marchbank @@Pim Ostrova
Items: ^The Mercy Bell ^The Director's Seal ^The Comptroller's Key
When: same day
]

Odile rang the bell for Joss Teague at nine that night, and the whole Dock Ring stopped for him, and then went on.

Dagny watched her cut the name. She had brought Pim, because there was nowhere to leave him — their door on Six was dimmed, and would stay dimmed until she found five hundred credits she did not have — and he sat on the bench under the Hook with his knees drawn up and his face closed, and did not look at the bell at all. He had not spoken since the Wick. When she had gone to him at the end of the master ring, after Varga's man let him go, he had let her hold him and said nothing, and he had said nothing since.

The engraver whined. _Joss Teague._ It came up bright on the bronze, high on the shoulder, just under Bronwen Price, just under Lemuel Adeyemi.

"I'm nearly out," said Odile, when she had finished. She said it differently this time. Not as a joke. "Look. There's the shoulder and the crown, and then there's nothing but the hook."

"There's the band on the waist," said Dagny. "The plain one."

"That's the founders' band. You don't cut the founders' band." Odile put the engraver down. Her hands, which had been still as stone on the bronze, had begun to shake again. "Thirty years I've kept her. Ninety-one years she's hung there. And now there's three hundred Company men going to walk past her up the ramp to their ship and not one of them's ever going to have his name on her, and she's full." She looked at the bell for a long moment. "Joss used to buy me a drink every Christmas. He said it was for keeping his mother. I cut his mother twelve years ago."

Dagny did not know what to say. So she said nothing, and sat down on the bench beside her son, and the three of them stayed there under the bell, as they had a week before, while the Dock Ring went about its night business round them.

That was where Teodor Basu found them.

He came along the ring road in a dark coat, without his Company suit, and without any of the two or three security men who usually walked a little behind Company officers on the lower rings. He looked as if he had not slept since she last saw him. He stopped in front of the bench and looked at Pim, and at Odile, and then at Dagny.

"I'm— I'm sorry," he said. "About Mr. Teague."

"Everyone's sorry."

"Yes." He sat down, uninvited, on the end of the bench, very upright, his hands on his knees. "I've come to tell you two things. I don't know if either of them is any use. I've decided that isn't for me to judge any longer."

Dagny waited.

"The first is about the keys." He spoke low and fast, without the stammer, the way he had on the Wick. "I told you the Director's seal never leaves her. That's true. And mine never leaves me. That's true too. But there's one more time they must be used together, before the _Covenant_ goes. On the morning of departure the Director and I go down into the root, together, and close the Company's own accounts on the Tally — the staff accounts, the Concern's operating accounts — and transfer the trust. It's in the charter. It has to be done by both keys, at the root, in person. It will take about twenty minutes." He looked at her. "That's the only time, between now and the Throat, that both keys will be in the same room as the root. With the door open."

Dagny was very still.

"And the second thing?" she said.

"The second thing is something I don't understand." Basu looked down at his hands. "I keep the _Covenant_'s manifest. Every crate. Every cassette. I've been through it a dozen times, because it's my job, and because— because I wanted to see what was being taken. There's one item on it I didn't put there. It came aboard under Mr. Varga's seal, from a Concord military shipment, three months ago, before the throat-watch said anything at all. It's listed as _device, Concord ordnance, class T_. It's in a shielded bay behind the drive. I asked the Director what it was. She told me it was a precaution." He raised his eyes. "I've worked for her for twenty years, Ms. Ostrova. I know when she's telling me the truth. I know when she isn't. She wasn't."

"What's class T?"

"I don't know." He stood up. "I'm going to find out. I think— I think I may not like it."

He went away along the ring road, very upright, into the night. Dagny sat and looked after him. Beside her, Odile had picked up the engraver again and was turning it in her shaking fingers.

"That man," Odile said, "has done something he can't carry."

"Yes."

"They always come to the bell in the end. The ones who can't carry it." She looked up at the bronze, at the bright new name on the shoulder. "Dagny."

"What?"

"If you're going to make them pay," said the old woman, "I'll need more bell."
