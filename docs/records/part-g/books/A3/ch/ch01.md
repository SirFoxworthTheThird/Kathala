## The Pressure Lock
[
Place: #The Galleries
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Joss Teague @@Lemuel Adeyemi:dead
]

The call came down the spine at the end of second shift, when Dagny Ostrova was forty metres up the Wick with her face in a collimator housing and her knees locked round a rung that had been warm for ninety years.

"Gallery Nine," said the speaker on her collar. It was Joss, below her on the crawl, and his voice had the flatness it got when something was already too late and he hadn't decided to know it yet. "Lock Nine-Forty. Man in it. It's gone to recovery."

She was moving before he finished. You did not think about a lock in recovery. You thought about it afterwards, at the bell, with a drink in you.

Going down the Wick was going down only in the sense that the rock's spin pulled you, gently at first and then less gently, toward the curved floor of the world. At the axis there was no weight at all, only the lamp: three kilometres of magnetic bottle and shielding and slow white fire, with the crawls and catwalks wrapped round it like ivy round a pole. Its light fell on everything. It fell on the terraced Galleries that climbed the inner wall in every direction, on the water tanks and the strip-farms and the tin roofs, on the long seam of the Upper Wards where the Company people lived with real glass in their windows. On Lantern you could look up from anywhere and see someone's house above you, upside down, lit from the side by the same lamp that lit yours. Children were taught early not to drop things from high terraces. It was the first rule. The second was to watch your balance.

Dagny dropped through the spoke-shaft hand over hand, letting her weight build, and hit the Gallery Nine landing at a run with Joss three seconds behind her, wheezing. A crowd had already made a half-ring round Lock Nine-Forty. Nobody was touching it. On Lantern, nobody touched a lock in recovery, because the Tally logged who touched what.

The lock was a steel bottle three metres long, set between the Gallery Nine pressure zone and the Gallery Ten one, so that if either breached the other would hold. You walked into it, the door behind you sealed, the Tally read the chit on your wrist, and if you were in credit the door in front of you opened. If you were not, it didn't. And because the Company had built Lantern on the principle that air was the dearest thing in the Sabra system and must be recovered wherever it could be, a lock with an unauthorised occupant began, after ninety seconds, to draw its air back into the main for reclamation.

Lemuel Adeyemi was in the lock. Dagny knew him a little. Pump-fitter, Gallery Ten. Fifties. A cough. A wife who had died two winters back of the same cough, and whose name was on the bell. He was standing with both palms flat on the inner door's window, and his mouth was open, and his breath was making less fog on the glass than it should have.

"How long?" Dagny said.

"Two minutes in recovery," somebody said. "His shift got docked. Pump fault on Ten, they docked the whole crew a half-day. It posted on the hour. He was already in the lock when it posted."

Dagny looked at the panel by the door. The little screen showed his number and his balance, in the Company's blue, and the balance was in brackets, which was how the Tally wrote a debt it had stopped being patient with. Minus nine hundred and four. The dimming line on Lantern was minus nine hundred.

Four. He was four over the line. Four was a meal. Four was an hour of water.

"Transfer," she said, and slapped her own chit onto the panel. "I'll stand it. Transfer from Ostrova, nine-oh-six-two-two, to—"

The panel chimed politely. TRANSFERS BETWEEN ACCOUNTS POST AT THE HOUR. PLEASE SEE A COUNTER.

"There's no counter on Nine at shift-end," Joss said behind her. "There's no counter on Nine ever."

"Then get me the override."

"Dag. The override's in the Counting House."

She knew that. Everyone knew that. She said it anyway because it was the next thing to say, and because the alternative was to stand and watch, and the crowd was already standing and watching. Inside the lock Lemuel had seen her. He took one hand off the glass and made a small shape with it, a sort of apology, as if he were sorry to be making a scene.

"Torch," said Dagny.

"It's a pressure door. It'll take—"

"Torch, Joss."

He had one on his belt, because Wick crews carried everything. She lit it and put the flame to the hinge side of the outer door and the steel went cherry-red and then white and began, very slowly, to run. The crowd made a noise behind her. The panel spoke again, in the same tone: TAMPERING WITH COMPANY PRESSURE EQUIPMENT IS CHARGED AT TWO HUNDRED CREDITS PER INCIDENT TO THE ACCOUNT OF THE TAMPERER. It read her chit through her glove. She watched her own balance drop by two hundred in the corner of the screen and kept cutting.

It took four minutes. A man can live for four minutes in a lock in recovery; Dagny had seen it. What a man cannot do is live for four minutes in a lock in recovery after he has already been in it for three. By the time the hinge gave and Joss got a pry-bar into the gap and the two of them hauled the door back on its seal, there was nothing in the lock but cold, thin air, rushing out past them into the gallery with a sound like a long breath let go, and Lemuel Adeyemi on his knees with his forehead against the inner door, as though he had decided at the end to pray to it.

Dagny went in and turned him over. His eyes were open. His lips were the colour of the shadow under a terrace. She put two fingers on his neck because she had been trained to and because people were watching, and then she took them away.

Behind her the panel on the wall chimed a third time. Lemuel's number went grey, and under it, small and blue and patient, the Tally posted his last entry: ACCOUNT CLOSED BY DEATH. BALANCE TRANSFERRED TO ESTATE. And then, because Lemuel had no estate but a cough and a bunk and a wife's name on a bell, it posted the next one, which Dagny had seen a hundred times and which never stopped being the worst thing she had ever read: ESTATE INSOLVENT. BALANCE TRANSFERRED TO NEXT OF KIN.

"Who's his kin?" she said.

Nobody answered. Then a girl at the front of the crowd, eight or nine, with a chit on a cord round her neck and her hair cut short against lice, said, "Me."

## Next of Kin
[

Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Joss Teague
When: same day
]

Joss sat on the landing with the torch in his lap and wept the way big men did on Lantern, quietly and with his face turned to the wall. Dagny did not weep. She went to the girl, whose name was Abi and who was Lemuel's sister's child, and she knelt so that their faces were level, and she looked at the chit.

Minus nine hundred and four, plus the girl's own, which was the ordinary minus three hundred that a Lantern child had by eight from being fed and housed and breathed. Minus twelve hundred and four. Three hundred over the line. The girl's door would be dimmed by morning. Her ration would stop at the next posting.

"You've got people on Ten?"

"My aunt's at the farms. She's on nights."

"Go to her. Tell her the Wick crews will stand it. Tell her Dagny Ostrova said. Tomorrow I'll put it through a counter."

The girl looked at her with the flat, measuring look of every Lantern child who had already learned that adults said things. Then she went.

"You haven't got twelve hundred," said Joss, when she'd gone.

"The crews have."

"The crews have got a hole in the bottom of the bucket same as you, Dag."

"Then we'll stand it with the hole." She stood. Her knees hurt. Her balance, in the corner of her own eye, was smaller by two hundred for a door that had not saved anyone. "Get him covered. I'll take his name to Odile."

## The Bell
[
Place: #The Dock Ring
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Odile Marchbank @@Pim Ostrova @@Kas Ostrova
Items: ^The Mercy Bell
When: same day
]

The bell of the _Ardent Mercy_ hung at the inner lock of the Dock Ring, on a hook of black iron that had been drilled into the rock the year Lantern was first pressurised. It was not large. It came up to Dagny's hip. It had been cast in the shipyards at Tannet Reach for a colony carrier that had crossed the Throat ninety-one years before, and the carrier had been cut up for her hull plate within a decade of arrival, as such ships always were; but the bell had been kept, because it was bad luck to melt a ship's bell, and because somebody on that first crossing had started the habit of ringing it for the dead.

There had been thirty-one dead on the crossing. Their names were cut round the lip in a neat, old-fashioned hand. After them came Lantern's dead, in a hand that changed every generation as the dockmasters did, round the lip and up the waist and onto the shoulder and in among the decoration on the crown, so that the bronze was now more letter than metal. From the length of the lock you could not see the names. You could only see that the bell was a strange colour, a dull brown furred all over with scratches, like an old animal.

Odile Marchbank was waiting for her. She had heard; everyone on Lantern heard everything inside an hour, it was the one thing the rock did faster than the Tally. She was seventy-four and three-quarters deaf and she had kept the bell for thirty years, and her hands, which shook when she held a cup, went still as stone when they held the engraver.

"Adeyemi," Dagny said. "Lemuel. Ten."

Odile nodded. She didn't need the spelling; she had cut his wife. She took the bell-rope, which was a length of braided hauler cable, and she rang the bell once.

It was not a loud sound. Lantern swallowed noise; the rock was too big and too full of air and machinery. But the bell had a voice that went a long way anyway, low and a little sour, and all along the Dock Ring people stopped what they were doing at the sound of it. Hauler crews in the gantries. Customs men at their desks. The women who ran the noodle-stalls along the ring road. They stopped and stood, a breath's worth, and then went on.

Dagny felt a small hand slide into hers. She didn't look down.

"You're supposed to be at school," she said.

"School finished," said Pim. "Everyone came out when they heard the lock. Mister Okonkwo said it was a teaching moment." He was twelve, and thin, and he collected bolts, and he had her husband's eyebrows, which she had never got used to. "Was it Mister Adeyemi from Ten?"

"Yes."

"Did you try?"

"Yes."

He thought about that. "How much was he over?"

"Four."

Pim did not say anything else. He held her hand while Odile fitted the engraver's burr and bent to the bronze, and the thin whine of it went up into the ring, and the first letters of Lemuel's name came up bright on the shoulder of the bell, among nine decades of brown.

"I'm running out of bell," Odile said, without looking up. She said it every few months. It was a sort of joke. "One day I'll have to start on the hook."

## The Magpie Comes In
[
Place: #The Dock Ring
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Pim Ostrova @@Kas Ostrova
When: same day
]

The _Magpie_ came in an hour later with eighty tonnes of Caul ice in her tank-frame and a crack in her forward window that had been there so long it had a name. Her pilot came through the inner lock with his helmet under his arm and the whole of the Dock Ring's gossip already on his face.

"Who?"

"Adeyemi. Gallery Ten."

"Lock?"

"Lock."

Kasimir Ostrova closed his eyes for a moment. He was nine years younger than his sister and twice as handsome and he owed money, at a conservative estimate, to a third of the Dock Ring, none of which ever seemed to cost him a night's sleep. But he had grown up in Gallery Six the same as her, and he had seen the same locks.

"Uncle Kas," said Pim, and was swept up and hung upside down by the ankles, which at twelve he was too old for and permitted anyway.

"How's the run?" Dagny said.

"Cold. Long. The usual." Kas set Pim the right way up. "Quiet out there, though. Quieter than it was."

"Quiet how?"

"Caul Deep's still shut. You know they closed the Deep in the spring — the big shaft, the one that took nine hundred on a shift. Nobody's working it. Beacon's dark, gantries are up. Funny to fly past. Used to be you'd see the lights of it from a thousand klicks out like a city." He shrugged. "They said the vein ran out. Sent all the Deep crews home through the Throat. Contracts bought out, every one. Lucky bastards."

"I never heard of the Company buying out a contract in my life," said Dagny.

"Well. You never heard of a vein running out, neither. It's a season of firsts." Kas grinned at Pim. "Come on. I'll buy you a noodle. I've been paid."

"You've been paid and you owe Marguerite at the noodle stall forty."

"Then I'll buy him a noodle and her a smile, and we'll all be square." He put an arm round his sister's shoulders and squeezed, briefly, hard. "Sorry about Lemuel, Dag."

"Yes," she said. "Me too."

Behind them, Odile's engraver whined on in the bronze.

## Fifty-Two Days
[
Place: #The Galleries
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Pim Ostrova @@Joss Teague @@Ruth Achterberg:phone
Items: ^The Tally
When: +1 day
]

The announcement came at eleven the next morning, on every screen on Lantern at once.

Dagny was in the canteen on Gallery Six with Joss and two of the Wick crew, trying to work out how many hours the Speakers could find between them to stand the Adeyemi girl's debt, and getting a number that was not enough. The screen on the canteen wall was usually showing either the Company's safety notices or a football match from somewhere in the Concord that had been played eleven years before and brought through the Throat on a data-run. It went dark in the middle of a goal, and then it showed the Company's mark, the two letters H and A leaning on each other like tired men, and then it showed Ruth Achterberg.

The canteen went quiet in the way Lantern went quiet for the bell.

The Director was sitting at a desk with nothing on it. She was a lean, grey woman of sixty in a dark Company suit, and she wore at her throat, on a fine chain, the brass disk of the Director's seal, which every child on Lantern had been told was the key to everything. She did not look cruel. She looked like someone who had been awake all night doing arithmetic and had come out of it with an answer she disliked but would not apologise for.

"People of the Sabra Concern," she said. "I am speaking to you today with news that affects every one of us.

"As you know, the Throat which connects this system to the Concord is not a fixed thing. It breathes. Its throat-watch station has measured that breathing for the whole of this colony's history. Three weeks ago the station recorded the beginning of what our physicists call a closing phase. Throats which enter a closing phase do not reopen on any timescale that is useful to us. The best estimate, from the Concord's own literature, is somewhere between six and eight hundred years.

"The Throat will remain passable for fifty-two days. After that, this system will be cut off."

Somebody in the canteen laughed, the high bark of a person who has been hit. Nobody else made a sound.

"The Halden-Ash Company has a duty to its shareholders and to its staff," said the Director. "It cannot maintain a concern which it cannot reach. It will therefore withdraw from the Sabra system before the Throat closes. The carrier _Covenant_, now at our fore dock, will carry essential staff and their families and such Company assets as can be moved. I have asked the department heads to prepare those lists.

"I want to speak directly to the indentured population of Lantern." She looked into the lens. Dagny would remember that afterwards: that she did not look away. "Your contracts are held by the Company. The Company does not abandon what it holds. Your contracts will remain in force, in trust, for the full term of the closure, and will be honoured when the Throat reopens. Lantern's systems — its air, its water, its heat, its light — will continue to be administered by the Tally, which has kept you safe for ninety years and which will keep you safe after we are gone. The Tally does not need us. It needs only that you go on working, as you always have, and it will go on keeping you alive.

"I know that this is hard news. I wish that I had better. Thank you."

The screen went back to the Company's mark. Then, after a moment, to the football match, which had been paused at the instant before the goal and now went on as if nothing had happened, the ball crossing the line, the crowd eleven years ago and four hundred light-years away going wild.

## Standing on a Table
[
Place: #The Galleries
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Pim Ostrova @@Joss Teague
When: same day
]

What happened in the canteen in the next ten minutes Dagny remembered afterwards only in pieces. A man on Gallery Six put his chair through the screen and the screen went on showing football in a web of cracks. Two women began to fight over nothing, over a bench, and had to be pulled apart. Old Mister Okonkwo the schoolteacher sat with his hands flat on the table, saying, "Six hundred years, six hundred years," as if it were a sum that would come out differently if he said it often enough. Somebody shouted that the Company would take the fuel. Somebody shouted that the Company would take the water. Somebody shouted that they should go up to the Upper Wards now, all of them, and see how many lists the department heads had made when they had their own windows broken.

And Pim was beside her, she didn't know when he had come in, saying, "Mum. Mum. In trust. What's in trust mean?"

She understood it, she realised, before she understood anything else. It was Lantern; you learned the Company's language before you learned your prayers. _Held in trust_ meant that the Company was leaving, and the debt was staying. It meant that every chit on every wrist on Lantern would go on counting down, hour by hour, against the air, for six hundred years, and that if the Throat ever opened again somebody in a Company suit would come through it with the books in their hand to see how much was owed. It meant that the Adeyemi girl's twelve hundred would be eighteen hundred by the time she was grown and nine thousand by the time she died, and would go to whoever came after her.

It meant that the lock on Gallery Nine would go on doing what it had done to Lemuel, with nobody even in the Counting House to blame for it.

She found she was standing on a table. She did not remember getting up there. The canteen was looking at her, the way the Wick crews looked at her when the lamp went wrong and someone had to say a number out loud.

"All right," Dagny said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "All right. Shut up and listen. I'm a Speaker for the Wick crews, and from tonight the Wick crews are going to want to talk to the other crews. Pumps, farms, docks, stills. Everyone who wears a chit. Fifty-two days is a long time. It's long enough for the Company to pack. It's long enough for us to make it listen first."

"Listen to what?" somebody shouted.

"To the one thing it hasn't said." She held up her wrist, with the chit on it, so that the canteen could see the blue numbers. "She said the Tally keeps us alive. It doesn't. We keep it alive. We fed it ninety years. If the Company's going, the Company can take its ledger with it, or it can switch the bloody thing off before it goes. Either way, nobody on this rock is going to owe air to an empty office for six hundred years."

There was a silence. Then the man who had broken the screen began to clap, slowly, and others took it up; and Joss, below her, looked up with his wet red face and said, very quietly, so that only she could hear: "Dag. You know what you just did."

She did. She had just volunteered.

## The Clean Window
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Teodor Basu
When: same day
]

On the top floor of the Counting House, behind the only clean window on Lantern, Teodor Basu, Comptroller of the Sabra Concern, watched the feed from the Gallery Six canteen camera with the sound off. He watched the woman on the table. He watched her hold up her wrist.

He ran her number. Ostrova, Dagny. Wick crews. Indentured nineteen years. Balance minus six hundred and eighty-one, including a tampering fee of two hundred posted last night at Lock Nine-Forty.

His hand, on the desk, was shaking. He put the other one over it until it stopped.

Then he opened a file that was not on any list the Director had asked for, and looked for a long time at a number that was not a balance at all, and was nine hundred, and closed it again.
