## Frame Forty
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Orrin Sallow @@Brother Lusk
When: +2 days
]

They went in on the night before the third list, because there was no other night left.

Orrin had found the hatch on the second evening, on his knees again in the fifth hall with his stylus, scraping. It was where the stencil had said it would be: a square of plating a metre across, two paces from the wall, with a recessed ring-pull at one edge and a seam all the way round that three centuries of grey paint had filled until it looked like floor. REACTOR ACCESS — FRAME 40. Under the Count, under the ledger halls, under the plaque and the brass rail and the children's hands, a way down into the Hulk that nobody had opened since the Founding.

"It'll be welded," Tamsin had said, when he told her.

"It won't." He had been very sure. "It's a reactor hatch. You don't weld those. You'd need to get in, if anything went wrong. It'll be locked. With a Company lock. And I've read every Company lock manual in the archive in the last four days." He had smiled, a little wildly. "I've had trouble sleeping."

So at two in the morning on the twenty-third day, when the fifth hall was dark and the bailiffs' round had passed and would not come again for an hour, five people came up the counters' service stair in grey coats: Orrin in front, and Tamsin, and Dobbs, and two of Wenna's cutters with a sled and a winch folded flat between them, and a coil of tether, and a case of tools. The coats were Orrin's, and his mother's, and three he had taken from the counters' cloakroom on the second floor. Dobbs's did not close across his chest.

The hall was very quiet. The plaque inside the brass rail caught a little light from the night-lamps by the door. Tamsin walked past it without looking.

Orrin knelt by the seam with his case of tools open beside him and began on the lock. Tamsin knelt beside him and held the lamp. She could hear him breathing — fast, shallow, a counter's breathing — and see his hands, which were quite steady. She would remember that afterwards. That his hands were steady.

It took him eleven minutes. Then something under the plating went _clunk_, deep and heavy, and Orrin sat back on his heels and looked at her, and his face in the lamplight was white and astonished and very young.

"Dobbs," he said.

Dobbs put his fingers in the ring-pull and lifted, and the hatch came up out of the floor of the Count on hinges that screamed like an animal, and under it there was a shaft going down into the Hulk, and a ladder, and warm air coming up, smelling of oil and metal and something else, something faint and dry and hot, like a stove.

"That's it," said Tamsin. She did not know how she knew. "That's them. That's the fourteen."

"Counter Sallow," said Brother Lusk.

He was standing in the doorway of the fifth hall. He had a lamp in one hand and his docket-baton in the other, and behind him in the corridor there were bailiffs, a great many of them, more than Tamsin could count, in grey, with batons, and some of them with the short ugly slug-guns that the Count kept in a locker on the third floor and had not taken out of it, Orrin said later, in living memory.

"I did warn you," said Lusk, mildly. "About the galleries."

Afterwards Tamsin could never put it in order. Dobbs moved first. He came up off his knees between the hatch and the door like a wall going up, with the pry-bar in his hands, and roared — the only sound she ever heard him make — and went at the doorway; and the bailiffs came through it at him, and the hall was full of shouting and lamps swinging and the flat hard crack of a slug-gun, once, twice, very loud under the low roof. Somebody had her arm. It was Orrin. He was dragging her backward, away from the door, toward the far end of the hall where there was another door, the service door, the one they had come in by. She was fighting him. She wanted the hatch. It was _there_, open, a metre away, with the warm dry air coming up out of it—

"Go," Orrin said. "Go, go, there's too many, go—"

She saw Dobbs go down in the doorway with three bailiffs on him and his pry-bar spinning away across the floor. She saw one of Wenna's cutters run past her for the service door, and the other one not run. She saw Lusk walking toward them up the middle of the hall, not hurrying, with his baton held loosely at his side, the way a man walks toward a door he means to close.

Then she was through the service door and on the stair, and Orrin was not.

She turned. He was in the doorway behind her, with his back to her, facing into the hall, with his arms out on either side of him, holding the frame. He was not strong. He would not hold it for more than a moment. He was holding it anyway.

"Orrin—"

"Tell your mother," he said over his shoulder, quite clearly, "that I'm sorry about the arithmetic."

Then Lusk reached him.

## Struck
[

Items: ^The Count Key ^The Tally
When: same day
]

They did not hurt him much. He had expected them to. He had sat in the bailiffs' room on the third floor for four hours with his wrists tied behind the chair and blood drying in his eyebrow from the one blow Lusk had given him in the doorway, almost absently, the way you would brush away a fly; and he had waited for the rest, and it had not come. At six in the morning they untied him and took him up to the long window.

Imre Vosk was standing at the glass with his hands behind his back, looking down the length of the world, at the Wick and the Galleries and the Cold. He did not turn round.

"Sit down, Counter."

Orrin stood.

"As you like." Vosk was silent for a while. Outside, the Wick stammered — once, twice, a long flicker that sent a shadow rippling down the whole length of the Galleries — and steadied. "It's getting worse. Ninian says weeks now. Not months. You know that, I imagine. You've been reading his reports."

"I've been reading everything."

"Yes. So I gather." Vosk turned. In the white light from the window his face was tired, and calm, and not unkind. "You've seen the reserve. You've seen the auxiliary lamp. You've seen the hatch at frame forty. And you think I'm a monster."

Orrin said nothing.

"I'm not. I'm a man doing sums." Vosk came and sat at his desk, and folded his hands on it. "The Founders saw this day coming, Counter. Three hundred years ago. They knew the Wick's fuel would run out. They knew there would be more people on the rock by then than any reserve could ever light. So they set aside fourteen cassettes and wrote _held_ against them and told every Comptroller-General after them, in a letter that I have read and you have not, that when the day came the reserve was to light the Count, and the Count was to go on keeping the Account. Not for its own sake. For Caul's. So that whatever happened to the rest, there would always be somebody keeping the books." He spread his hands. "That's not cruelty. That's a plan. It's the only plan there's ever been."

"There are thirty thousand people on Lantern."

"There are thirty thousand people on Lantern and fuel for four. You can light the Wick end to end with your fourteen, Counter, and everyone lives for fourteen years, and then everyone dies. Or you can light a small lamp for a small number, and some of them live for a very long time. That's the arithmetic." He tilted his head. "You were the most careful counter in the third hall. Do it yourself. You'll get the same answer."

Orrin had done it himself. On the second night, on his knees by the hatch, he had done it. He had got the same answer.

"And the Account?" he said. "_Holder, none._ Who are you keeping it for, Comptroller-General? Who's Caul?"

Vosk looked at him for a long moment.

"Caul is the reason people work," he said gently. "That's all it has ever been. A debt that can't be paid, owed to someone who can't be found, kept by people who can't be argued with. It's the finest machine the Founders ever built, finer than the Wick. It's kept thirty thousand people breathing in a rock in the dark for three hundred years. I don't need to know who Caul is, Counter. I only need everybody else to believe it's owed."

He stood up, then, and went to a cabinet in the wall behind the desk — a small brass-fronted thing, old, Company-made — and opened it with a key from his waistcoat pocket. Orrin could not see what was inside. A glint. Something round. Vosk did something to it, briefly, with his back turned, and closed the cabinet again, and locked it.

On the desk, the slate in front of Vosk's chair chimed once.

Orrin felt it before he understood it. A small cold weight on his wrist, where his chit hung on its cord. He looked down. The little screen had gone grey.

"Hask, Agathe, goes into the Cold at second shift," said Vosk. "With the third list. So do you. Released from the Tally's charge, and held quit." He sat down again. "I'm sorry, Orrin. I am. I'd have made you senior counter by thirty. But I can't have a man in the Count who has seen the reserve and doesn't understand what it's for. Brother Lusk will see you down."

## The Third Thousand
[
Place: #The Cold Wards
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Agathe Hask @@Jory Hask @@Orrin Sallow @@Brother Lusk
Items: ^Tally Chit
When: same day
]

They went at second shift.

Agathe Hask walked. Tamsin had found a chair with wheels, from somewhere, from Wenna, and her mother had looked at it and said, "I'm not being pushed into the dark in a chair, Tam," and got up off her bunk, and put on Tamsin's own winter coat because her own had been sold, and taken the biscuit tin with the farmhouse on its lid down from its shelf and put it under her arm, and walked. Down Twenty-Two to the ring road. Along the ring road past the Salt Docks to the Lip. It was a kilometre and a half. She stopped eleven times to cough. She did not sit down once.

Jory walked on her other side. He was fifteen, and a dependant, and the law said a dependant under sixteen went with the Called parent; he had not argued with it. He carried the bedding and a pan and a sack of ration bricks and his face was set like the face of a much older man.

Tamsin carried everything else. She was twenty-four, and not on any list, and the law said nothing at all about her, and she had not argued with that either.

The ring road was lined both sides. Not with bailiffs. With the Owing. All of Twenty-Two had come out, and Twenty-One and Twenty-Three, and the Salt Docks, and people from galleries Tamsin had never been to, standing along the road in silence as the third thousand came past. Nobody rang anything. Nobody sang. Old women touched Agathe's sleeve as she passed. Somebody put a twist of paper into Jory's hand with sugar in it. A man Tamsin did not know fell into step beside her for twenty paces and said, very low, "Wenna says the Salt's not done," and was gone.

At the Lip there was the table with the lamp on it, and Senior Counter Abney behind it with his slate, and his ink-stained cuffs. He touched Agathe's chit to the slate, and it chimed, and went grey, and he looked up at her and for a moment seemed about to say something; and did not; and looked down.

"Next," he said.

And beyond him, at the iron post where the light ended, Brother Lusk stood with his hands folded, nodding to each as they passed, like a priest at a door.

Beside Lusk, in the dusk, there was a man in a grey coat with the Count's silver torn off the collar and a cut over his eye. He was standing very straight, the way he had stood in the Hask room on the first day with his slate held against his chest, as if it might be taken off him; except that now there was nothing in his hands at all.

Tamsin stopped.

"Counter Sallow is released from the Tally's charge," said Lusk pleasantly, to nobody in particular. "He'll be going with you. I'm told you know each other."

Orrin looked at her. He did not say anything. His face was quite calm. Then he looked at Agathe, and something in his face broke, just a little, at one corner.

"Mrs. Hask," he said. "I'm sorry. I tried."

"I know you did, Counter." Agathe coughed, and wiped her mouth, and looked at him with her pale eyes. "Come on, then. You can carry the tin."

She held it out to him. After a moment he took it.

They walked past Lusk into the Cold together, the four of them: Agathe in front, very slow, and Jory with the bedding, and Orrin with the biscuit tin, and Tamsin last. She did not look at Lusk as she passed him. She felt him look at her. She thought he was counting.

## Owing and Owed
[
Place: #The Cold Wards
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Agathe Hask:dead @@Jory Hask @@Orrin Sallow
When: +1 day
]

The Count's provision for the Called was a pallet of ration bricks at the Lip, restocked when the bailiffs remembered, and the Cold.

Three thousand people were in it by now. The first thousand had found the old Pump Galleries, where the air was least thin, and the second thousand had found them, and the third came after, and there was nowhere else to go. They made fires of what would burn: old insulation, the dead wood of the strip-farm frames, furniture from the empty houses, doors. The smoke hung under the low roofs and stung the eyes. People slept in heaps, families together, under every blanket they had brought. Nobody had enough. In the first night forty people died, the old and the sick mostly, and in the second night more, and by the third morning the Called had stopped counting and had started carrying them, quietly, to a dead gallery further aft, and laying them in rows, under the frost.

Agathe Hask died on the second night.

She died in the dark, in a corner of Pump Gallery Twelve, with her back against the cold rock and the biscuit tin in her lap. She had not let go of it since Orrin gave it back to her at the Lip. Tamsin was with her. Jory was asleep, at last, with his head on Tamsin's knee. Orrin was sitting a little apart, against the opposite wall, keeping the fire.

It was not dramatic. Tamsin had thought it would be. She had dreaded it for two years, the way the lung-rot took people at the end, the drowning. It was not like that. Agathe coughed for a while, in the early part of the night, and then she stopped coughing, and lay quiet with her eyes open looking at the fire, and her breath went slower and slower and quieter and quieter, whistling a little, in and out, like a tap that someone has nearly turned off. Once she said something. Tamsin bent close.

"Their names," Agathe said.

"What, Mam?"

"Say their names. When I'm in the tin. Somebody's got to." Her hand moved on the lid of the tin, very slightly. "Nobody's going to cut them on anything."

"I'll say them," said Tamsin.

Her mother's breath went in and out a few more times after that. Then it went in and did not come out. Tamsin sat with her for a long time, with her hand on her mother's hand on the lid of the tin, and the fire crackling, and the frost falling sideways through the firelight from the roof of the gallery, very slowly, the way things fell on Lantern.

Then she got up, carefully, so as not to wake Jory, and went to the gallery wall where an old water main ran along it at shoulder height — iron, frost-furred, three hundred years dead — and picked up a length of pipe from the floor, and struck the main.

Once.

The sound went out through the Pump Galleries, flat and hard, the sound of the Hook. And out in the dark, from the other fires, from all through the Cold where the Called were sleeping or not sleeping, other sounds answered it: someone striking a rail, someone a pipe, someone a pan. Not many. A dozen. They rang once each, for Agathe Hask, whom most of them had never met, and then went quiet.

"Owing and owed," said Orrin, very low, from the other side of the fire.

Tamsin turned round and looked at him.

"No," she said. "Not any more. She doesn't owe anything any more. She's been released." She laughed, once, and it hurt. "Held quit. That's what they call it."

She came back and sat down by the fire, beside her sleeping brother and her dead mother, across from the counter.

"Orrin," she said.

"Yes."

"The keel-ways."

He looked at her blankly.

"Cutters' roads. In the rock. Under the Galleries, in the outer skin." She was speaking low and fast. "The Company cut them for the engineers, for the pipes and cables. They run the whole length of the rock, fore to aft. Most of them are blocked, or iced, or fallen in. Cutters use the aft ends to get into the deep Cold. Nobody goes fore in them. There's no reason to. Nothing up there but the Count." She looked at him. "They come out under the dock levels. Under the Counting House. Under the Hulk."

Orrin did not move.

"The hatch at frame forty," she said. "You opened it. It's a reactor hatch. A reactor's not just got a door at the top, has it? Not on a ship. There'll be one underneath. For the engineers. Into the keel."

"Yes," said Orrin slowly. "There'll be one underneath."

"And there's three thousand people in the Cold," said Tamsin, "with nothing left to lose. And seven thousand more coming. And they all know how to walk into the dark."

Across the fire, Orrin Sallow looked at her for a long time. Then he looked down at the biscuit tin in the dead woman's lap, with the farmhouse on its lid, and the chits inside it.

"Owing and owed," he said again, but differently this time, as if it were a question, or the beginning of a sum.
