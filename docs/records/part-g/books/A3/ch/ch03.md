## The Mouth
[
Place: #Port Ambry
Characters: @@Corvin Saal
]

From the observation gallery of Port Ambry you could see the Throat begin to heal, if you knew where to look and were prepared to look for a long time.

Corvin Saal had been told this by four different people since he came off the packet that morning, and he had been told it the way people on frontier stations told you things, as if they had personally arranged it. Port Ambry was a station that had waited three hundred years for one event. It had been built, at enormous expense, by a consortium of lien-houses and throat-speculators who had calculated that the Sabra Throat must heal some time in the thirtieth or thirty-first century and wanted to be first through it when it did. The consortium had gone bankrupt twice. The station had been sold, sublet, mortgaged, half abandoned, reoccupied. Generations of people had been born on it, grown old on it, and died on it, staring at a patch of black sky where nothing happened. And now something was happening, and the whole of Port Ambry was drunk on it.

He found a space at the gallery rail between a woman selling hot noodles from a tray and a priest of some throat-cult in orange, and looked.

At first there was nothing. Stars. The faint brown smear of a dust lane. Then, as his eyes learned the sky, a place in the middle of it where the stars were wrong: a little smeared, a little doubled, as if seen through old glass. And in the centre of the wrongness, so faint it might have been his own pulse in his eyes, a colour. Not a light. A colour, a sort of bruised pink, coming and going.

"She's breathing," said the noodle woman, with satisfaction. "Four months ago you couldn't see a thing. Now look at her."

"When will it open?"

"Open? It's open. You could put a probe through it today and it'd come out the other side in a hundred bits." She handed a carton to someone over his shoulder and took a coin. "A ship? Three weeks. The throat-men say three weeks. That's when the lips settle. And then—" she spread her hands, taking in the gallery, the station, the long docks below them where Corvin had counted eleven ships under refit when he came in— "then it's the Rush."

"Don't call it the Rush," said the priest in orange, mildly. "It's the Return."

"It's the Rush, Father, and you've got a berth on the _Saint Ishmael_ same as everybody."

The priest smiled and looked at the bruised place in the sky and said nothing. Corvin looked at it too. He tried to feel something about it. Seven hundred and two years. On the far side of that patch of wrongness there was a star called Sabra, and a gas giant, and a rock, and once there had been nine thousand people on the rock, and after the Throat shut nobody in the Concord had heard a word from them since.

He felt, mostly, the weight of the case in his hand, and in it the instrument he had been sent here to enforce.

## The Instrument
[
Place: #Port Ambry
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Quennell Haste
When: same day
]

Brightwater Recovery had rented an office on the station's third ring, behind a door with the firm's name freshly lettered on frosted glass. Quennell Haste was waiting for him inside it.

Corvin had met Haste twice before, both times in the firm's tower on Kessane, both times across a table with a great deal of polished wood on it. Haste was in his forties, slim and pale and beautifully kept, with the kind of patience that cost money to acquire. He was a junior partner. At Brightwater that meant he owned a slice of the firm and the firm owned the rest of him; Corvin had never been sure which part was talking.

"Mr. Saal. Good of you to come so far."

"I wasn't given the choice, Mr. Haste."

"No," Haste agreed pleasantly. "But it was good of you all the same. Sit. You'll have read the file."

Corvin sat. He had read the file. He had read it on the packet from Kessane, six weeks of it, and then read it again, and the more he read it the less he liked it, which was usually a sign that it was a good one.

In 2296 the Halden-Ash Company had opened the Sabra Concern: an ice-mining and fuel-refining colony beyond a newly charted throat, worked by indentured labour. Ninety-one years later, in 2387, the throat had entered a closing phase and Halden-Ash had withdrawn its staff. Its indentured workers, nine thousand of them, had been left behind with their contracts held in trust. Two years after that, Halden-Ash had failed. Its assets had been broken up and sold. Most of them were worthless, because most of them were on the far side of a dead throat. One of the worthless assets — the Sabra Concern's receivables, which was to say the debt of nine thousand people who could not be reached and might not be alive — had passed through the hands of nineteen lien-houses in seven centuries, at prices that fell to almost nothing and then, as the throat-watchers began to publish their numbers, rose again. Brightwater had bought it eleven years ago.

"You've seen the figure," said Haste.

"I have."

"Say it, would you? I like to hear it said."

Corvin said it. It was a number with more digits than the population of the Concord. It was what nine thousand people's air and water and heat and housing came to, at the Company's 2387 rates, plus seven hundred and two years of compound interest at the rate written into the contracts.

"Of course nobody expects to collect it," said Haste. "That number is a legal fiction. What matters is the instrument. Under the Perpetual Instruments Act, a debt held in trust by a Concord company, over persons under that company's contract, attaches to those persons and their issue for the life of the instrument. The instrument has no term. Halden-Ash wrote it so. Which means, Mr. Saal, that if there is anyone alive on the far side of that throat — anyone at all who is descended from a Halden-Ash indenture — then the moment we come through, Brightwater holds their contracts."

"And if there's nobody alive?"

"Then we have a rock, a fusion lamp, a refinery, and first title to a system nobody else has a claim on." Haste smiled. "Brightwater wins either way. That's why we bought it."

"And what do you need an adjuster for?"

"Because if there are people," Haste said, "someone has to go and stand in front of them and explain." He leaned back. "You're very good at that. I've read your file too. Eleven years on the colony circuit. Four hundred and twelve settlements. A very low rate of incident."

Corvin said nothing. A low rate of incident meant that when he stood in front of people and explained, they mostly didn't kill him. It meant he was good at the voice. The calm, sorry, unmovable voice of the man who has come for what is owed and will not leave without it.

"There's one more thing," said Haste, "and then I'll let you go and find your berth. Your bond."

"I know what my bond is."

"I know you do. I'd like you to know what it could be." Haste took a slip from his pocket and laid it on the desk between them and turned it round with one finger so that Corvin could read it. It was a discharge. Fully drafted. It wanted only a partner's seal. "Your father's water debt was sold to Brightwater when you were nine. You have been working it off for thirty-five years. At your current rate you'll be clear of it at seventy-one. If you bring us the Sabra Concern — the instrument, enforced, on a living population, or clean title to the system if there isn't one — this is yours. That's not a promise. It's a term. It's in your assignment letter."

Corvin looked at the slip for a long moment.

He thought of his father, who had signed him over in a water-office on Kessane with his hand shaking, and had died four years later, and whose face Corvin could no longer quite remember. He thought of the colony circuit, the four hundred and twelve settlements, the faces he could remember.

"I'll need to see the Company's records," he said. "Everything that came through before the Closing. And someone who can read them."

"You'll have both," said Haste. "The records are aboard. And the someone—" he glanced at the frosted door, where a shape had just stopped, hesitating, with something large on a trolley— "is, I think, here."

## The Crate
[
Place: #Port Ambry
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Fen Adaire @@Quennell Haste
Items: ^The Mercy Bell
When: same day
]

Her name was Fen Adaire and she was twenty-nine and she had brought a bell.

It was in a crate on a station trolley, packed in grey foam, and she would not let it out of her sight. She had come straight from the packet dock with it, she said, because she had been told her berth was on the _Good Title_, and she wanted to see the hold before she agreed to put anything in it, and then the porter had brought her up to the third ring by mistake and here she was. She said all this rather fast, standing in the doorway with one hand on the crate, a slight woman in a travelling coat too warm for the station, dark hair cut short, a provenance archivist's loupe on a cord round her neck. Haste introduced her with the air of a man producing a clever purchase.

"Miss Adaire reads dead companies," he said. "Ledger formats, charter law, corporate scripts. She did the Tannet salvage for us four years ago. When we found out what else she had, we thought she ought to come."

"What else she had?" said Corvin.

Fen looked at Haste, and then at Corvin, and then she knelt and undid the clasps on the crate and folded back the foam.

The bell lay on its side in a nest of grey. It was not large — he could have got his arms round it — and it was a strange dull colour, a brown furred all over with fine pale scratches, so that at first he took it for something badly corroded. Then she turned it a little toward the light and he saw that the scratches were letters. Every surface of it was cut with names. Round the lip, up the waist, onto the shoulder and across the crown, in among the casting's raised decoration, in a dozen different hands: names, thousands of them, so close and so many that the bronze between them was only a sort of mortar.

Across the waist, in raised capitals that had been part of the original cast, larger than everything else and nearly worn away, were two words. ARDENT MERCY.

"She was a colony carrier," said Fen. "Built at Tannet Reach in 2290. She crossed into the Sabra system in 2296 with the first Halden-Ash settlers. She never came back out. Carriers didn't; they were broken up for plate on arrival. This was her bell."

"Then how is it here?"

"That," said Fen, "is the question." She sat back on her heels. "My family's had it six generations. My great-great-great-grandmother bought it at a wreck auction at Tannet. The lot was listed as _bell, bronze, inscribed, recovered from unidentified hulk, condition poor_. She paid eleven crowns for it. Nobody knew what it was. My grandmother was the one who read the waist and looked up the name. We've been trying to find out how it got out of Sabra ever since."

"And?"

"And nothing. There's no record of it on any manifest. No record of any Sabra ship being wrecked on the Concord side. The Throat closed in 2387 and the bell turned up at Tannet thirty years later on a hulk nobody could identify, and that's all anybody knows." She ran her thumb along the lip, very lightly. "Somebody brought it out. Somebody carried it through, before the end. I'd like to know who. And I'd like to take it home."

"Home," said Corvin.

"Back through. To wherever it came from." She looked up at him and, for the first time, seemed to see him properly: the case, the suit, the face. Her mouth tightened a little. "Mr. Haste tells me you're the man who'll be explaining to them what they owe."

"If there's anyone to explain it to."

"Yes," said Fen. "If."

He crouched beside the crate, because it seemed rude not to. Close to, the names became legible. They were tiny, most of them, cut with a fine burr, and the oldest were so worn that he could only make out a letter here and there. The newer ones were sharper. He tilted his head and read the nearest bright line, high on the shoulder, where the light caught it.

_Lemuel Adeyemi._

Just a name. One of thousands. He did not know why it stopped him. Perhaps only that it was so clear, so cleanly cut, as if someone had made it the day before.

"How many names?" he said.

"I've counted two thousand nine hundred and eighty, give or take the ones I can't read." Fen closed the foam over the bell, gently, as if putting a child to bed. "A carrier's crew was forty. Her colonists were six hundred. I don't know who the rest of them are, Mr. Saal. I'd very much like to."

## The Good Title
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Fen Adaire @@Mabry Ode @@Tol Brask @@Idris Kell
Items: ^The Mercy Bell
When: +2 days
]

The _Good Title_ was a Throat-runner, which was to say she was old, strong, ugly, and built around an engine much too big for her. She had been built to carry cargo through healed throats in the years when throats were still healing, in the last great age of opening, three centuries back. She had run the Ashcombe Throat when it was a raw wound full of debris and the Pellan Throat the week it opened, and she had the scars of both down her flanks, sealed over in four different colours of hull compound. Her master had been running her for thirty years.

Captain Mabry Ode was sixty-three, gaunt as a gantry, and she did not shake hands. She met them at the ship's lock in a coverall that had been washed so often it had no colour left, and looked at Corvin, and at his case, and at Haste behind him, and at the crate on its trolley with Fen beside it, and said, "What's that?"

"A bell," said Fen.

"I can see it's a bell. What's it for?"

"It's going home."

Ode looked at her for a moment longer, as if deciding whether this was a joke, and then turned and spat, accurately, into the gap between the ship's lip and the dock. "Tol," she said. "Find a place for the lady's bell. Not the hold. The hold's spoken for."

A thin man with grease to his elbows unfolded himself from a hatch and came and looked at the crate with more interest than he had shown in any of the people. "What's she weigh?"

"Three hundred and twelve kilos."

"Bronze?"

"Mostly tin bronze. Some lead."

"Huh," said Tol Brask, and took the trolley without another word, and went off down the corridor talking to it under his breath.

"The hold's spoken for?" said Corvin.

Ode jerked her head at Haste. "Ask him."

Haste only smiled. "Agricultural stock. For the colony, if there is one. Seed, embryos, a soil culture library. If there's no one there, we'll need to start something. It's all sealed and certified. The captain's been very patient about it."

"The captain's had her hold sealed by a lien-house for the first time in thirty years of running throats," said Ode, "and the captain's got a ship that's mortgaged to that lien-house to the last rivet. So the captain's been very patient about it. Yes."

Behind them, on the dock, there was the sound of boots. Corvin turned. Ten men and women were coming up the dock in a loose column, in the dark grey field uniform of Brightwater's bailiffs, with kit bags on their shoulders and long cases slung on their backs that were not tools. At their head was a heavy, unhurried man of fifty with a sergeant's flash on his sleeve and the bored eyes of someone who has seen every kind of trouble and found none of it interesting.

"Sergeant Kell," said Haste. "And his people. You'll find them very quiet."

"I wasn't told about bailiffs," said Ode.

"You were told about a recovery team, Captain."

"I was told about clerks."

"Sergeant Kell is a very good clerk," said Haste, and stepped aside to let the column file past him into the lock. Kell nodded to the captain as he passed, civilly enough. Ode watched him go with an expression Corvin had seen before, on four hundred and twelve settlements, on the faces of people watching the bailiffs come off the boat.

## The Last Filing
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal
When: same day
]

That night, in the cabin he had been given — a steel box two metres by three, with a fold-down desk and a bunk and a porthole looking out on the dock lights — Corvin opened the case and began, again, on the Company's records.

There were not many. When a throat closes, the last news through it is whatever happened to be on the last ship. Halden-Ash's final filings from the Sabra Concern had come out on a data-run forty days before the Closing: a few thousand pages of accounts, inventories, staff lists, a throat-watch summary, and a statement from the Director to the Company's board. After that there was nothing at all. Seven hundred years of nothing.

He read the Director's statement again. It was short and dry and he could find nothing wrong with it. _The throat-watch has confirmed the onset of a closing phase. The Concern will withdraw in good order. Essential staff and Company assets will be transported. The indentured population will remain, its contracts held in trust in accordance with the Company's charter, its life-support administered by the Tally, which is fully autonomous and requires no Company supervision. All Concern operations will be wound down prior to withdrawal._

And then a list of the operations, and how each had been wound down. The refinery. The greenhouses. The water contracts with the ice-haulers. And, near the end, one line he had noticed on the packet and noticed again now, because it was the only line in the whole document that was about people rather than things:

_Caul Deep: mine closed, spring 2387; workforce of 900 repatriated through the Throat per contract, completed prior to this report._

He read it twice. Nine hundred people, sent home. Their contracts bought out, presumably, or completed. A decent thing for a company to do, before it left the rest behind. It was the only decent thing in the file.

He made a note to ask the archivist where the nine hundred had been landed, and turned out the light.

Outside the porthole, very faint, beyond the dock lights and the station's sprawl, the patch of wrong sky was breathing pink.
