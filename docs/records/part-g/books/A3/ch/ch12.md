## Roach's Keys
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Fen Adaire
Items: ^The Mercy Bell
When: +3 days
]

Fen broke the throat-watch core on the sixth day out from the Throat, at four in the morning, and came and knocked on Corvin's cabin door with her hair standing up and her eyes red and a slate held against her chest, and said, "I need you to look at something so I know I'm not mad."

He followed her up to the observation blister. The bell was there, strapped upright to the couch legs, and round it on the deck she had spread every slate and printout she owned in a rough ring, like a ritual circle. The core from the throat-watch station sat in the middle of them, cabled to her own machine.

"It was Roach," she said. "He kept his own keys. In a text file. Unencrypted. Called _keys — do not lose_." She laughed, a little wildly. "Seven hundred years of Company cryptography and a lonely man in a can wrote his passwords down because he was afraid of forgetting them. I could kiss him."

"What did you find?"

She sat down cross-legged among the slates and pulled one into her lap.

"Three things," she said. "The first one's the throat. The raw readings. Every six hours, for eleven years, all the way to the end." She turned the slate toward him. On it was a long line, rising and falling in a slow, perfectly regular wave, like breathing. "That's the Throat. That's its mouth opening and closing, the way the lips did when we went through. Except it's steady. Look at it. There's no change. No closing phase. Not in the last month, not in the last year, not ever. It's breathing exactly the way it always breathed, right up to—" she ran her finger along the wave to its end, where it simply stopped, cut off in the middle of a rise— "there."

"Where the station died."

"No. The station didn't die there. It went on recording for weeks after. Roach went on writing in his book. That's where the Throat died." She looked up at him. "There's no closing phase, Corvin. The Director's statement — the thing that came through on the last data-run, that every lien-house in the Concord's been trading on for seven hundred years, _the throat-watch has confirmed the onset of a closing phase_ — it's not in the throat-watch. The Throat was fine. And then, on one day, in one reading, it wasn't there any more."

He looked at the line on the slate for a long time. The slow regular breathing. The cut.

"Something closed it," he said.

"Something closed it. Or somebody." She put the slate down. "And there's a thing in the Concord military register, from the Succession Wars—"

"I know what's in the register." He did. Every adjuster did; throat law was half of lien law. "That's the first thing. What's the second?"

She pulled another slate into her lap. This one was a list.

"The Director's statement said nine hundred people from the Caul Deep mine were repatriated through the Throat before the Closing. Contracts bought out. That line." He nodded; he knew the line. "The statement came with staff rolls. The whole Concern. Including Caul Deep — every miner, name and number, on the day the mine was shut. Nine hundred and four of them. I've got the Concord immigration registry for that decade on file. It's what I do. Provenance. Where people went." She held up the slate. "None of them went anywhere. Not one of the nine hundred and four ever came through a Concord port. Ever registered. Ever worked. Ever died, in the Concord's books. They were repatriated, and then they just— weren't."

"Lost in transit," said Corvin. He said it the way an adjuster says it. He heard himself.

"There's no record of a transport being lost. There's no record of a transport. There's a line in a report, and nine hundred and four people who stop." She looked at him steadily. "And then there's the third thing."

She got up on her knees and reached for the bell.

She had marked the grey band on the waist — the dense cramped band of names in the hurried hands, cut over the bell's plain belt — with small strips of white tape, every so often, like the ruled lines on a page. Now she put her lamp to it and her loupe to her eye, and began to read aloud, very slowly, one name and then the next, and as she read she ran her other hand down the slate in her lap.

"Abara, Joseph. Achebe, Ruth. Adair, Michael. Agbaje, Femi." Her finger moved on the slate. "Abara, Joseph. Achebe, Ruth. Adair, Michael. Agbaje—"

She stopped.

"They're alphabetical," she said. "On the bell. In the band. Whoever cut them was working down a list. And it's this list." She held up the slate. "The Caul Deep roster. I've checked four hundred. I'll check the rest. But I don't need to, do I." She lowered the loupe. "They're all here. All nine hundred and four. Cut on Lantern's bell, by hand, in a hurry, at the very end. With all the rest of Lantern's dead."

The blister was very quiet. Above them, through the scratched glass, the banded face of Nhai filled half the sky, ochre and bruise-purple, enormous now, with the hard bright line of its ring.

"They didn't go home," said Corvin.

"No."

"They died. On Caul. And the Company wrote them out of the books and put a line in a report and closed the Throat behind it—"

"And somebody on Lantern knew," said Fen. "Knew enough to get every name. And cut every one of them into the bell, so they'd be remembered with everybody else. And then—" she put her hand flat on the bronze— "somebody took the bell away. Out through the Throat. Before the end." She shook her head. "I don't understand it, Corvin. I've had this thing in my house my whole life. I don't understand it at all."

He sat down on the couch, heavily, with his elbows on his knees.

"There's one more thing," Fen said, after a while. "Small. I nearly didn't notice. The throat-watch recorded the Tally's hourly postings, the same ones we've been hearing. Every hour, for eleven years, Roach's station logged the packet and filed it. Roach's keys open them. Most of them." She turned a slate toward him, and on it was a long column of dates, each with a little green mark, and then, near the bottom, the marks went red. "Until the forty-ninth day after the Director's statement. Then the Tally changed its keys. All of them. In one hour. And nothing after that opens."

"Changed them," said Corvin. "Who changed them?"

"I don't know." Fen looked at the bell. "Somebody who didn't want the Company reading their books any more."

## Hold 2
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Tol Brask @@Quennell Haste @@Idris Kell
When: same day
]

He went into the hold that night, because he had run out of ways not to.

Tol took him. He had not asked Tol; Tol had simply been waiting in the corridor outside the engine spaces when Corvin came down it, as though they had arranged to meet, and had nodded once and turned and led him, without a word, through a hatch in the engine-room deck and along a service crawl so narrow Corvin had to go on his elbows, past cable runs and coolant pipes and the great humming flank of the drive, to a small square panel in a bulkhead.

"Hold 2," said Tol. "Inspection hatch. It's on the ship's plans, not on Brightwater's. They sealed the main doors. They didn't know about this." He took a key from his pocket — an ordinary mechanical key, old, on a loop of wire. "My ship. My hatches."

He opened it. Cold came out — a dry, burning, utter cold, that made Corvin's eyes water and his breath smoke. Tol handed him a lamp and a pair of thick gloves, and stood back.

"I've looked," said Tol. "Your turn."

Corvin went through.

Hold 2 was the biggest space on the _Good Title_, a long vault running half the length of the ship, and it was full. He had known it would be full; the power board had told him that. He had not known what full would look like. The pods stood in racks, floor to ceiling, row after row, aisle after aisle, going away into the dark beyond his lamp: long white capsules like seed-cases, each the length of a person, each with a small round window of frosted glass at one end and a little green light at the other, steady, steady, steady. Hundreds of them. He walked down the first aisle with the lamp held up and kept walking and the pods kept going. Thousands.

He stopped at one at random and rubbed the frost off its window with his glove.

A woman's face. Young — thirty, perhaps — dark-skinned, with her hair shaved close for the cold, her eyes shut, her lips a little blue. Asleep. Not dead; the light at the foot of the pod was green. Very still, very far away, as people in cold sleep always looked, as if they had gone down a long road inside themselves and not yet turned to come back.

There was a tag on the pod. A plastic sleeve, at eye level, with a printed card in it. He put the lamp to it.

_BRIGHTWATER RECOVERY — LABOUR CONTRACT 41-0973._
_TEY, Marisol. Born Kessane, 3058. Source: Kessane Water Authority, debt assignment._
_Contract assigned: SABRA CONCERN (Brightwater) — persons and issue._
_Dependants: one (1), female, b. 3086, retained Kessane, attaching on majority._

He read it three times. He read the words _Kessane Water Authority_ three times. He stood in the cold with the lamp shaking a little in his hand and read the words _persons and issue_ until they stopped being words.

Then he went to the next pod, and the next. The cards were all the same. Different names, different debts, different worlds — Kessane, Malory's Reach, the Tannet yards, the Pellan colonies, places he had been, places he had stood in front of people and explained. Water debts. Medical debts. Family debts sold on at auction. All of them bought, all of them assigned, all of them to the same instrument, the same seven-hundred-year-old promise that he had in a case in his cabin. _Sabra Concern. Persons and issue._

He did not hear them come in. He heard Tol say, from the inspection hatch, very quietly, "Mr. Saal," and turned, and the main doors at the far end of the hold were open, and there was light in the aisle, and Quennell Haste was walking down it toward him in his dark coat with Sergeant Kell a pace behind.

"Mr. Saal," said Haste. "You've found the agricultural stock."

Corvin said nothing. He found that he had put his hand flat on the frosted window of Marisol Tey's pod, as if to keep her from hearing.

"Three thousand and twelve," said Haste. He stopped a few metres away and looked about him at the racks, with that same mild collector's interest. "Water debts, mostly. A few medical. All bought at very good prices; there's a glut of labour contracts at the moment, nobody wants them. The Concord has more debtors than it knows what to do with." He looked back at Corvin. "Please don't look at me like that. I've done nothing illegal. Every one of these people signed, or was signed for, under the law. Every one of these contracts was sold to us freely. We have simply assigned them to our principal instrument. That's all."

"To populate it," said Corvin. His voice sounded strange to him.

"If necessary. If the rock is empty, an instrument with no persons attached to it is worth only the rock. An instrument with three thousand persons attached — and their issue — is worth rather more. A colony. A workforce. A balance that grows every hour they breathe on it." He spread his hands. "And if the rock isn't empty, then we will need labour for the consolidation, and these people will provide it, and their accounts and the natives' will be merged into one book. Either way the instrument has persons. Either way it's enforced. You see?"

"Their children," said Corvin. "The cards say _attaching on majority_. Their children back on Kessane. When they grow up—"

"They attach. Yes. Persons and issue." Haste tilted his head. "You know this, Mr. Saal. You know it better than anyone aboard. It's what happened to you. Your father signed, and you attached, at nine. It's why you're here." He smiled, quite kindly. "This is what a lien is. It always has been. I'm a little surprised you need to be shown."

Behind him, Kell had not moved. He stood with his hands loose at his sides and his weight easy, and looked at Corvin with his bored, patient eyes, and Corvin understood, as clearly as he had ever understood anything, that if he said the wrong thing in the next minute, Kell would not need to be told what to do.

"I'll want you on the bridge at eight," said Haste. "We'll be clearing Nhai's limb in six days, and I'd like to go over the populated-habitat speech. Sergeant, please make sure the engineer's hatch is sealed after Mr. Saal has gone." He turned, and then turned back. "And Mr. Saal. Your discharge is in my desk. It hasn't gone anywhere. Nor have you. Good night."

## The Captain
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Mabry Ode @@Tol Brask
When: same day
]

Ode was in her cabin, which Corvin had never seen: a cell like his own, with a bunk and a desk and on the wall a single picture, an old print of a ship he didn't recognise under full sail on a blue sea. She listened to him with her arms folded and her face like a shut hatch. When he had finished she said nothing for a long time.

"I didn't know," she said at last.

"I didn't think you did."

"I'm telling you anyway. I want it said. I've run throats for thirty-four years and I've carried every rotten thing there is to carry, guns and bodies and cargoes you wouldn't want to know about, and I knew what every one of them was. I didn't know this." She unfolded her arms and put her hands flat on her desk and looked at them. "Three thousand."

"Three thousand and twelve."

"And their kids."

"And their kids."

"And mine's mortgaged to him." She laughed, without any amusement. "Every rivet. If I refuse the cargo he forecloses the moment we're back through the Throat, and I lose her, and my crew lose their berths, and the sleepers go on another ship. Or he forecloses now, here, and Kell puts somebody else in this chair. I know the law, Mr. Saal. I've had it explained to me by people like you."

"I know."

"So what are you going to do?"

He didn't answer. After a moment she nodded, as if that were an answer.

"Tol," she said, without raising her voice.

The engineer was in the doorway; Corvin had not heard him come. He was wiping his hands on a rag.

"We could drop the hold," Tol said. "There's a way. Emergency jettison, from the old cargo days. The whole bay. We'd say it was a fault."

"Drop it where?"

"Somewhere they could be picked up. If anyone was there to pick them up."

"Nobody's dropping anybody," said Ode. "Not three thousand people in cold sleep into a gas giant's orbit in a system nobody's lived in for seven hundred years. Not on my ship." She looked at Corvin. "Not yet."

## The Band
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Fen Adaire
Items: ^The Mercy Bell
When: same day
]

Fen was still in the blister when he went back up, sitting on the deck in her ring of slates with her back against the couch and the bell beside her. She had stopped working. She was just looking at it.

He sat down beside her on the deck. He told her about the hold. She listened without moving, and when he was done she reached out, without a word, and took his hand, and held it — not tenderly; hard, the way you would hold on to something on a moving ship — and they sat together in silence for a long time, with the bell between them and the planet filling the window.

"Read me some," he said at last. "From the band."

She picked up the loupe and the lamp, without letting go of his hand, and leaned toward the bronze.

"Okafor, Grace," she read. "Okeke, Samuel. Oladipo, Bisi. Olsen, Erik. Olsen, Marta." She stopped. "Husband and wife, maybe. Or brother and sister."

"Go on."

"Ortiz, Benedicto. Osei, Kwame. Ostrowski, Anna." She moved the lamp. "Owusu, Adwoa. Page, Thomas."

She went on reading. He listened. Outside, on the far side of the great banded world, something went on counting; and in the hold below them three thousand and twelve people slept in the cold with their names on cards; and Fen read the dead of Caul aloud, one by one, in alphabetical order, to the man who had come to collect what they owed.
