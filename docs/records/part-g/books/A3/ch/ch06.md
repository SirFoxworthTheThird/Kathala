## The Queue
[
Place: #The Throat
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Mabry Ode @@Tol Brask @@Quennell Haste
When: +19 days
]

There were eleven ships in the queue at the mouth of the Throat, and the _Good Title_ was seventh.

The order had been settled at Port Ambry by the harbourmaster's lottery, which every captain had sworn to honour and which every captain, Ode said, would honour exactly as long as it suited them. The queue lay in a loose line along the approach, thirty thousand kilometres out from the mouth, each ship holding station on its neighbour with little flickers of manoeuvring thrust. From the _Good Title_'s bridge they looked like beads on a string. Beyond them was the Throat.

Close to, it was not a patch of wrong sky any longer. It was a wound. Corvin could find no other word for it. A great ragged lens of distortion, thousands of kilometres across, through which the stars on the far side swam and doubled and smeared, and at its heart a pulsing bruise of light, pink going to violet going to a deep hot red, like something seen through closed eyelids. The edges of the lens were never still. They rippled. Ode called them the lips, and the word had made him faintly sick the first time she said it and now seemed simply accurate. They opened and closed. Not much — a few hundred kilometres, the throat-men said, on a period of a few hours — but enough. A ship in the lips when they closed would not be a ship afterwards.

"First goes at the top of the next open," Ode said. She was in the master's chair with her boots on the console, which Corvin had learned was how she sat when she was worried. "That's the _Pellucid_. Then the _Harrow_, then the _Lady Jane_, then the _Saint Ishmael_. We go on the third open after. Nine hours, give or take."

"And until then?"

"Until then we sit here and watch better men than us go first." She did not take her eyes off the main screen. "Tol."

"Captain."

"How's she feel?"

Tol Brask's voice came from somewhere below, flat and absorbed. "Engine's fine. Engine's always fine. Hull's grumbling on the old Pellan scar. I'll watch it."

"Watch it."

Haste had come up from his cabin and was standing at the back of the bridge in a dark coat, quite still, watching the screen with the polite interest of a man at a concert. Corvin found that he disliked that more than anything else about him: that he could look at the Throat as if it were a performance being put on for him, and might be asked for an encore.

The _Pellucid_ went at the top of the open. Corvin watched her go: a long grey freighter, Concord-built, much newer than the _Good Title_, sliding forward on a steady burn into the swimming dark at the centre of the lens. For a while she was a clear shape. Then she was a smear. Then she was a red point that wavered and stretched and was gone.

Nobody on the bridge said anything for a minute.

"She's through," said Ode at last. "Or she's dead. Either way she's out of the queue." She took her boots off the console. "Next."

## The Saint Ishmael
[
Place: #The Throat
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Mabry Ode @@Quennell Haste
When: same day
]

It was during the second open, with the _Harrow_ and the _Lady Jane_ both gone into the red, that the _Saint Ishmael_ broke the queue.

She was the ship with the priests: a converted pilgrim-hauler, broad and slow, with a cargo of the faithful in orange who had sold everything they owned, the noodle-woman had told Corvin, for a berth on the first ship through to the Return. She was fourth in the lottery. She should have waited for the third open, as the _Good Title_ was waiting. Instead, as the lips began to close at the end of the second, Corvin saw her drive light flare, very bright, and saw her begin to move.

"What's she doing?" he said.

"Cutting," said Ode. She sat up very straight. "Stupid. Stupid bloody—" She hit the comm. "_Saint Ishmael_, _Saint Ishmael_, this is _Good Title_, you are out of turn, the lips are closing, abort, abort your run—"

The comm hissed. Then a calm, pleasant voice — the priest's voice, Corvin thought, the one from the gallery rail — said: "_Good Title_, thank you for your concern. We have been shown the way. We go in faith. Peace be with you all."

"Faith's got nothing to do with the bloody lips, you lunatic—"

But the _Saint Ishmael_ was already going, faster than she had any business going, her drive a long white stroke against the swimming stars, straight at the heart of the lens. For a moment Corvin thought she would make it. She was so nearly through. The red of the throat's centre had begun to swallow her bow; he could see the light of it on her hull. Then the lips closed.

He had expected it to be sudden, and it was not. It was slow, and it was quite silent, and that was worse. The edge of the lens slid across the screen like a curtain drawn across a window, and where it crossed the _Saint Ishmael_ the ship simply came apart: not in an explosion, but as if she had been made of paper and somebody had folded her. Her stern went one way and her bow another and her middle went into a long bright streak of debris that was pulled out and out along the edge of the lips like taffy, glittering, and then the curtain was closed and there was only a thin bright line drawn along the rim of the Throat, slowly spreading, slowly dimming, where four hundred people had been.

The bridge was very quiet.

"Throats don't forgive," said Mabry Ode at last. She said it softly, without anger, the way you would say the name of someone who had died. "Every throat-runner learns that the first year, or doesn't get a second." She turned to the comm officer, a young man with his hands over his mouth. "Log it. _Saint Ishmael_, out of turn, lost in the lips, all hands. Time and position. Send it back to Ambry on the next relay."

"All hands," the young man said, through his fingers.

"All hands." Ode swung her chair round and looked at Haste, who had not moved. "That's the Rush, Mr. Haste. That's what your claim's worth, to some people. That's what it's worth to the rest of us too, if we don't keep our heads."

"I'm sure we'll keep them, Captain," said Haste.

## Transit
[
Place: #The Throat
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Fen Adaire
Items: ^The Mercy Bell
When: same day
]

They went in on the third open, and it took eleven hours.

Corvin had read about throat transit. Everybody had. It was in children's books: the long fall, the strange lights, the time that went wrong. He had read, as a boy, that people in transit saw their own lives, or the faces of the dead, or nothing at all; that some came out singing and some came out mad. What none of the books had said was how boring most of it was. The _Good Title_ went into the red and the red closed round her and for hours afterwards there was nothing on the screens but red, a deep dull red that moved slowly, like blood in water, and every so often a shudder through the hull as she rode some invisible swell, and Tol's voice from below saying _fine, fine, she's fine_, as if to himself.

After the third hour Ode sent everybody who was not on watch to their bunks. Corvin did not go to his bunk. He went looking for somewhere with a window, and found the observation blister on the ship's upper spine — a dome of old scratched glass with two worn couches under it, built in some optimistic decade when throat-runners had expected to carry tourists — and found that it was already occupied by Fen Adaire and her bell.

Tol had stowed the crate there, it turned out, because it was the only space aboard that was not cargo, quarters, or Brightwater's. Fen had opened it. She had the bell out of its foam and sitting upright on the deck, wedged with cargo straps to the couch legs, and she was lying on her back on the deck beside it with a hand-lamp and her loupe, reading it.

"Sorry," said Corvin. "I'll find somewhere else."

"There isn't anywhere else." She didn't look up. "Sit, if you want. It's a big window."

He sat on one of the couches. Above them, through the glass, the red moved. Every so often something moved in it that might have been a shape and might have been his eyes.

"Does it bother you?" he said. "That?"

"The Throat? No. I've been through four." She turned the loupe a fraction. "What bothers me is that I can read six hundred and twelve names on this bell and I don't know who any of them are."

"I thought you said nearly three thousand."

"I said I'd counted nearly three thousand. Counting isn't reading. Most of them are too worn. Or too small. Or cut in a hand I can't make out." She sat up, pushing her hair back with the hand that held the lamp. "There's an order to them, though. That much I've worked out. The oldest are round the lip. They're the crossing — thirty-one names, cut very carefully, all in one hand. Then the colony's dead, year by year, going up the waist. You can see the hand change every twenty or thirty years. A new dockmaster, maybe. Somebody whose job it was. I can date some of them roughly by the style." She shifted round, on her knees now, and put her lamp on the shoulder of the bell. "And up here, at the top, the last hand. Very good. Very steady. Old, I think — there's a little shake on the long strokes. These are the newest. That one—" she touched a bright line with her fingertip— "is the newest of all. Or one of them."

He leaned forward. He knew which name it was before he read it.

"Lemuel Adeyemi," he said.

She looked at him in surprise. "You saw it at Ambry."

"I saw it at Ambry." He did not know how to explain that he had thought about it since. That a name on a bell, cut clean and bright among thousands of worn ones, had followed him onto the ship and into his bunk at night. "Who was he?"

"I've no idea. A person who died on Lantern, in the last years before the Closing. That's all the bell can tell me." She sat back on her heels. "That's the thing about provenance, Mr. Saal. Objects remember things. They just don't remember them in sentences."

"Corvin."

"Corvin." She considered him. "And there's something else. Here." She moved the lamp down, to a band low on the waist, below the old weathered names and above the lip, where he had taken the bronze for blank. In the light it was not blank. It was cut, very small, very close, with hundreds of names: a dense grey band of letters, cramped together, so close that they almost touched. "These aren't in order with the others. They're cut over a blank band that the old dockmasters had left — the bell was cast with a plain belt there, for decoration — and they're in hands I don't see anywhere else on the bell. Three or four of them, I think, taking turns. Fast. Not as careful. Like people in a hurry, or people very tired." She looked up at him. "Hundreds of them. All at once, at the end."

"An epidemic," said Corvin. "A disaster. A fire."

"Maybe." She didn't sound convinced. "If there'd been a disaster, the Company would have filed it. There'd be a record. I've read every page they sent out before the Closing, Corvin. There's no fire. There's no plague. There's nothing."

He said nothing. He was thinking, for no reason he could name, of a line near the end of the Director's statement, about a mine.

"Why do you want to take it home?" he said instead. "Really."

Fen was quiet for a while. Above them the red moved, and the hull gave one of its long low shudders, and Tol's voice came faint through the deck: _fine, she's fine._

"Because they're names," she said at last. "Somebody cut every one of these by hand, for three hundred years, so that the people who died on that rock wouldn't just be numbers in a Company's books. And then somebody took the bell away from them. Took it out through the Throat, before the end, and left them with nothing to cut their names on. I don't know who did that, or why. But I've had it all my life, and I've never once felt it was mine." She ran her thumb along the dense grey band. "It's theirs. Whoever's left."

"There may be nobody left."

"Then it can go back to the rock. Back on whatever hook it came off. It'll still be home." She looked at him, with the lamp in her lap throwing its light up under her face. "Your turn. Why are you here?"

"I'm paid to be."

"Everybody's paid to be. That isn't what I asked."

He was quiet for a long time. Then, because they were inside a throat, in the long red dark between one universe and the next, where it seemed — the books were right about this much — that ordinary rules had been left on the other side, he told her. The water-office on Kessane. His father's hand shaking on the stylus. Thirty-five years. The discharge in Haste's pocket, wanting only a seal.

She listened without interrupting. When he was done she said, "So you're bonded."

"Everybody's bonded."

"I'm not."

"Then you're the first person I've met in ten years who isn't."

"My great-grandmother paid ours off. She sold a painting. It was the only thing we owned worth anything except this." She touched the bell. "She could have sold this instead. A bell off a lost colony ship — there are collectors who'd have paid ten times what the painting fetched. She didn't. She sold the painting and kept the bell, and paid the family out of bond, and died in a rented room." Fen smiled a little, not happily. "We don't really know why. She just said it wasn't hers to sell."

The hull shuddered again, harder. Somewhere below them something metal rang, once, like a struck pipe; and very faintly, as if in answer, the bell under Fen's hand hummed.

They both looked at it.

"Sympathetic resonance," said Fen, after a moment. "It does that. It's a bell."

"Yes," said Corvin.

Neither of them looked away from it for some time.

## Sabra
[
Place: #Nhai
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Mabry Ode @@Quennell Haste @@Tol Brask @@Fen Adaire
When: same day
]

They came out of the Throat into a system that did not answer.

The red thinned and tore and fell away behind them, and there were stars — different stars, a different sky, the shapes of the constellations all broken and rearranged — and a sun. It was not much of a sun. It was a dim orange disc, much smaller than Kessane's, so faint you could look straight at it through the screen filters. And off to one side of it, a little way along the ecliptic, a great banded ball the colour of ochre and old bruises, with a thin hard line of ring drawn across its face.

"Sabra," said Ode. "And that's Nhai. The gas giant. Your rock's in orbit round it, if it's anywhere." She was grey with tiredness; she had not left the chair in eleven hours. "Comms. What have we got?"

The young comm officer had been listening since the red began to thin. He took his headset off and looked at her and then at Haste and then at Corvin, as though he would rather not say it to any of them.

"Nothing, Captain."

"Nothing on what?"

"On anything. I've been through every band in the Company's 2387 tables. Traffic control, haulers, emergency, the Concern's own channels. The throat-watch station's frequency. Then everything else. Nothing." He hesitated. "It's very quiet, Captain. Quieter than empty space usually is. Nobody's even leaking."

"The _Pellucid_? The _Harrow_?"

"Both through. I've got their transponders, both heading in-system. But they're not talking either. Not to us."

"Course they're not. They're racing." Ode rubbed her eyes. "And the rock?"

"Can't see it." The navigator answered this time, an older woman bent over a plot. "Lantern's orbit puts it on the far side of Nhai from us right now. It'll be behind the planet for days yet. We'll need to come round."

"How long?"

"At our best burn? Nine days to clear the limb."

Nine days. Corvin found himself looking at the banded face of the gas giant, at the hard bright line of the ring across it, and thinking that somewhere behind that, in its shadow, a rock eleven kilometres long had been turning for seven hundred and two years; and that nobody on this ship had the faintest idea whether there was a single living thing inside it.

"Silence is an answer of a kind," said Haste, behind him. He had come up onto the bridge without anyone hearing; he did that. "It narrows the possibilities. Captain, you'll lay a course for the rock, please, at your best burn. Mr. Saal, I'd like you to begin preparing for both contingencies. A populated habitat and an empty one. I expect you'll need a different speech for each."

"I'll need a different speech for each," Corvin agreed.

When Haste had gone, Ode looked at Corvin for a long moment, from under her grey brows.

"Different speech for each," she said. "Mine's the same either way. God help them."

## The Board
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Tol Brask
When: same day
]

Tol Brask found him two hours later, in the narrow corridor outside the engine spaces, where Corvin had gone to be somewhere that was not his cabin.

"You're the adjuster," said Tol. It was the first thing he had said directly to Corvin since Ambry.

"I am."

"You can read a power board?"

"After a fashion."

Tol jerked his head at the wall panel beside him, where a grid of little lights and figures showed what each section of the ship was drawing from the engine. He put one long greasy finger on a line near the bottom. HOLD 2 — SEALED. "Read that."

Corvin read it. He read it twice. The figure was larger than the figure for the bridge, the galley, and all the crew quarters together.

"That's a lot of power for seed," he said.

"That's a lot of power for anything," said Tol. "That's not a seed draw. I've hauled seed. Seed sits in a cold box and sips. That's a deep-cold draw. That's a cryo draw. That's the kind of draw you get from something you've got to keep at four degrees above nothing every second of every day, or it spoils." He took his finger off the board. "Captain doesn't want to know. Captain's got a mortgage. I'm not asking you to do anything. I'm just telling you what the board says. I tell the captain what the board says, every day, and now I've told you."

"Why me?"

Tol looked at him with his flat, absorbed, engineer's eyes.

"Because you're the one who's going to stand in front of them," he said, "and explain."

He went back into the engine spaces and shut the door. Corvin stood in the corridor for a long time with his hand on the cold wall, feeling through it the deep steady thrum of the _Good Title_'s great engine, driving them in toward the planet and the rock behind it; and, under that, so faint he could not be certain it was there at all, a second, lower note, steady as breath, coming up from somewhere aft.
