## The Third Key
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Hale Varga @@Teodor Basu:dead
Items: ^The Comptroller's Key
When: +1 day
]

Teodor Basu was dying in the Company infirmary on the second floor of the Counting House, because there was nowhere else to take him and nobody left who could do anything about it.

The Company's doctors had gone on the _Covenant_. So had its nurses, and its drugs, and its good beds. What was left was a long room with a row of empty cots and a woman from the Gallery Three clinic called Mrs. Ansah, who had come up the spine-road on her own when she heard there was a man shot in the Counting House, and who had done what she could, which was to pack the wound and give him the last of the Company's morphine and sit with him. When Varga brought Dagny in, at four in the morning on the forty-ninth day, Mrs. Ansah looked up at them and shook her head, once, and got up and went to the far end of the room to give them the bed.

He was very small in it. His glasses were on the cabinet beside him, folded, the cracked lens catching the light. His face was the colour of the sheet.

"Ms. Ostrova," he said. His voice was a whisper. "They let you go."

"He let me go." Dagny sat down on the edge of the cot. "He didn't get on the ship."

Teodor turned his head a little on the pillow, and looked at Varga, standing at the foot of the bed with his big hands at his sides. Something passed between the two men that Dagny could not read.

"No," Teodor said. "I didn't think he would." He closed his eyes for a moment. "Pim?"

"Gone. With Kas. On the _Covenant_."

"I'm sorry."

"I sent him."

Teodor opened his eyes. He looked at her for a long time.

"Then I'm sorrier," he said. He moved his hand on the sheet, feebly, toward the cabinet. "My glasses. Would you."

She picked them up. She was going to put them on his face for him, and he shook his head, very slightly, and she stopped.

"The frame," he said. "The left arm. Where it goes over the ear. It unscrews."

She looked at him. Then she looked at the glasses. She turned them in her fingers. The left arm of the frame was thicker than the right, a little, at the end — she had never noticed; nobody would have — and when she twisted the tip it turned, stiffly, and came away in her hand, and inside it, rolled very small, there was a slip of something pale and thin, like a fingernail. Ceramic. Like the key from his shoe. Not like it. Narrower, and darker, and the marks burned into it were different.

"I lied to you," said Teodor Basu. "On the Wick. I said there was no back door. There's one."

Dagny held it on her palm and did not breathe.

"I made it the first year," he said. "When I built her. I was twenty-six and very proud and I'd just been shown what the Company wanted her to do. And I was afraid. Not of the Company. Of her. Of what she'd be able to do to people when I'd finished, and that there'd be nothing anyone could do to stop her. So I made one thing she'd obey that nobody else knew about. Not the Director. Not the board. Not the Tally's own registry. It doesn't open the root the way the paired keys do. It can't change the line, or the rates, or anything else. It does one thing, once, and then it's spent." He swallowed. "It's a quit-claim."

"What does it do?"

"It changes who is owed." He shut his eyes again. "Listen. Please. I haven't— there isn't much time. You can't free them. I told you that and it was true. Every valve asks the Tally whose air it is, and the Tally answers by looking at who owes it and who is owed. Every account has to have a creditor. That's her heart. That's the thing I built her round. An account with no creditor is an account she can't answer for, and if she can't answer for it the valve fails closed. If you took the Company off every account on Lantern and left nothing in its place, every door and every scrubber on the rock would shut in the same hour." He breathed. "So you can't make them owe nobody. But with that—" his eyes moved to her palm— "you can make them owe somebody else. Anybody. Once. And the Company's claim is quit. Gone. The trust's empty. Whatever comes through the Throat in six hundred years with Halden-Ash's books in its hand will find that Halden-Ash isn't owed a single credit on Lantern. Not one."

Dagny closed her hand on it.

"Who?" she said. "Who do I make them owe?"

"I've thought about that every night for twenty-six years." Teodor almost smiled. "I never had an answer. Anyone living could sell it. Anyone living could die and leave it to somebody worse. Any company could fail and be bought. Any person could be bought." His voice was going. "It has to be somebody who can never collect, Ms. Ostrova. Somebody who can never sell. Somebody nobody can ever buy."

She sat very still on the edge of his cot. She thought of a port of armoured glass at the bottom of a shaft on Caul, and a woman behind it with her palm raised. She thought of the founders' band, and a boy on a box with an old woman's hand on his.

"Yes," said Teodor Basu, watching her face. "Yes. I thought you might."

"Will it hold?"

"It will hold as long as she runs." He moved his hand again, and found hers, and held it, very weakly. "It wants a voice. At the root. The grille. Say who, and say what discharges it. She'll want to know what discharges it — every debt has to have a way to end, even if nobody ever takes it. I built her honest, at least. I'll say that for myself." His fingers loosened. "The third door. It opens the third door too. It was made to."

"Teodor."

"Mm."

"The nine hundred and four. The letters." She did not know what she wanted to say. "Odile cut them. All of them. On the bell. Your list."

He did not answer for a long time. She thought he had gone. Then his lips moved.

"Good," he said. "That's good."

He died a little before five, with Mrs. Ansah on one side of him and Dagny on the other and Varga at the foot of the bed. He did not say anything else. At the end his hand tightened once on Dagny's, and then let go.

## The Root
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Hale Varga
Items: ^The Tally ^The Comptroller's Key
When: same day
]

Varga took her down.

The first door opened to his Security override. So did the second; he had set the overrides himself, he said, eleven years ago, and nobody had ever thought to take them back. The corridor beyond was the same cold blue it had been the morning before. Somebody had washed the floor. There was still a dark stain on the rock by the second door where Ezra Hesketh had lain, that the washing had not got out.

The third door was shut. It was a plain steel slab with no handle and a slot at chest height. Dagny looked at the slip of ceramic on her palm for a moment, and then put it in the slot.

The door opened.

The root was as she had left it. The low dome. The pedestal. The desk of dark glass and brass with its two empty slots, side by side, and the little grille beside them like the grille of a confessional. Nothing else. She stood in front of it with Teodor's quit-claim in her fingers and could not, for a moment, remember why she was there.

"There," said Varga quietly, behind her, and pointed.

Below the two slots, where she had not noticed it, flush with the brass, there was a third. Very narrow. You would not see it unless you knew.

She put the slip in it.

The console woke. Not as it had for the Director — there was no chime, no soft brightening of the glass. It simply went from dark to lit, and on its face, in plain white letters, the Tally said:

QUIT-CLAIM PRESENTED. ONE USE. STATE THE ASSIGNEE.

Dagny bent to the grille.

She had thought about what she would say all the way down the stair. She had thought about it in the infirmary with Teodor's hand going cold in hers. She had not found any words for it that were good enough, and in the end she said the plainest ones she had.

"All balances on the Tally," she said. "Every account. Every person on Lantern, living and to come. Assigned to the dead of Caul Deep. The nine hundred and four. They're owed it. All of it."

The glass was still for a moment. Then:

ASSIGNEE: THE DEAD OF CAUL DEEP. ACCOUNT HOLDER?

"None," said Dagny. "Nobody holds it. Nobody can draw on it. Nobody can sell it. It's owed to them, and they're dead, and that's all."

ACCOUNT HOLDER: NONE. CREDITOR TO BE ENTERED AS?

She thought for a moment. "Caul," she said.

CREDITOR: CAUL. STATE TERMS OF DISCHARGE.

_She'll want to know what discharges it. Every debt has to have a way to end, even if nobody ever takes it._

Dagny straightened up and stood in front of the console with her hands at her sides.

She thought of the bell. She had seen it last at two in the morning, two days ago, going into the _Magpie_'s tank-frame in its nest of ice-sacking, between the empty tanks — and it was there still, at the Dock Ring berth, because the _Magpie_ was there still, because the man who was to have flown it had sold his seat and gone. All night in the cell she had thought of it there. Nine hundred and four names round its waist that existed nowhere else now; Varga's men had taken Teodor's folder off Odile's bench on the morning of the root and burned it, Varga had told her so on the stair, not looking at her; the Tally itself forgot a closed account in ninety days, which was why every family on Lantern kept its dead's chits in a tin. Nine hundred and four names on a founders' band, and an old woman's shaking words on the crown above them, that Dagny had read by torchlight when she cut the shackle. _These are owed. Say their names._

"When their names are spoken," she said. "Every one of them. All nine hundred and four, to the Tally, here, at the root, in order. When somebody stands here and says every one of their names, the debt is done. Not before. Not for anything else. Not for money, not for work, not for anybody's seal or key. Their names."

There was a long pause. She had time to think that the Tally would refuse — that a machine built to count credits would not know what to do with a debt paid in names.

CREDITOR NAMES NOT ON FILE, said the console.

"They're on the bell," said Dagny.

Another pause. Then:

TERMS ACCEPTED. DISCHARGE: CREDITOR NAMES, SPOKEN, IN FULL, IN ORDER, AT ROOT. PRIOR CREDITOR'S CLAIM QUIT. OUTSIDE READING KEYS RENEWED. COMPANY RECEIVERS VOID.

And then, at the top of the glass, in the plain white letters:

ACCOUNT ESTABLISHED. CREDITOR: CAUL. ALL BALANCES ON THE TALLY ASSIGNED TO CREDITOR IN FULL. ACCOUNT HOLDER: NONE.

The console went dark. The slip of ceramic slid out of the narrow slot by itself, and lay on the brass, and Dagny picked it up and saw that the marks burned into it had gone grey, like a dimmed chit.

She stood there for a long time.

"Is that it?" said Varga at last. "Is it done?"

"The Company's claim is quit," said Dagny. She heard her own voice as if from a long way off. "Nobody on Lantern owes Halden-Ash a credit. Not one. Not ever. Whoever comes through the Throat with their books, the books are empty." She turned the dead slip in her fingers. "We still owe. Every hour of air. The line's still there. The locks still go to recovery. I couldn't touch any of that. He said I couldn't." She closed her hand on it. "But we owe _them_. Not the Company. Them."

Varga looked at the dark console. He did not say anything for a while. Then he said, in a voice she had not heard him use: "I poured the seal on eleven of them."

"I know."

"Then I'm in debt to them too."

"Everybody is," said Dagny. "Now." She put the dead quit-claim in her pocket. "Mr. Varga. Can you fly a tug?"

## Wake

]

The _Magpie_ went out from the Dock Ring at seven in the morning on the forty-ninth day, with a Company security chief at her controls and a Wick-tender in the second seat and the bell of the _Ardent Mercy_ strapped down in her tank-frame between the empty tanks, because there had not been time to take it out, and because Dagny had looked at it in its nest of ice-sacking when they boarded and found that she could not bear to leave it behind on an empty berth.

Varga flew her hard. He had flown landing craft in the Concord marines and he flew the _Magpie_ the same way, as if she were something he meant to break and did not much care when. Three days at full burn. The _Covenant_ had a day's start and four times their mass and she was not hurrying; she was a carrier, built to move fourteen hundred people and their cargo through a throat without spilling any of them, and she would be crawling the last of the approach, lining up on the mouth. A tug with empty tanks and nothing in her but two people and a bell could catch her. Just.

They did not talk much. There was nothing to say that would make the burn shorter. Varga slept in the pilot's seat in snatches of an hour, with his hands on the controls. Dagny slept in the second seat and did not sleep. On the second night, with Lantern a small grey grain far behind them against Nhai and the Throat not yet in sight, she said into the dark of the little cabin: "Why didn't you get on?"

He was quiet so long she thought he had not heard.

"I stood at the foot of the ramp," he said at last. "With my bag. The Director went up past me and said, _Coming, Hale?_ And I said yes. And I went to put my foot on the ramp, and I couldn't." He shifted in the seat. "I kept thinking about the ladder at Caul. Three hundred metres. You can hear a long way in a shaft. I thought, if I go up this ramp, I'm going up that ladder again. For the rest of my life. And I thought I'd rather stay down here with them."

Dagny did not say anything.

"You'll want to know what I'll do when we catch her," Varga said.

"Yes."

"I don't know. The men on her were my men. Some of them." He was silent again. "I know the codes for her docking collar. I know the way to her bridge. I know which of them will look the other way if I come up the companionway, and which won't. That's what I know." He turned his head in the dark. "What will _you_ do, Ostrova?"

"Get my son."

"And the shear?"

She had thought about that for two days and nights in the second seat with the bell behind her in the dark of the tank-frame. She had thought about inspectors coming through a throat with a commission, and a receiver, and a lien-house on Kessane, and the Tannet Yards ruling, and a different landlord. She had thought about the root, and the white words on the glass. _Prior creditor's claim quit._ Whatever came through the Throat would find the books empty now. Lantern owed the Company nothing. It owed the dead.

She had thought about the ice at Caul, and the faces in it, and that if the Throat closed nobody in the Concord would ever know their names.

"I don't know," she said.

## The Blue Flank
[
Place: #The Throat
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Hale Varga
When: +3 days
]

They came up on the _Covenant_ at the mouth of the Throat on the afternoon of the fifty-second day.

Dagny had seen the Throat in pictures all her life. Every child on Lantern had; it was in the schoolbooks, a smear of wrongness in the sky with a pink heart. It was not a picture. It filled the whole of the _Magpie_'s cracked forward window — a vast, slow, swimming lens of broken starlight, thousands of kilometres across, its edges rippling, opening and closing, and at its centre a deep hot bruise of red that pulsed like a heart. It was breathing. Teodor's numbers had said so. She could see it breathe.

And against it, small, black, a long blunt shape with a blue flank and a great dark drive-bell at her stern, slowing, turning, lining up on the heart: the _Covenant_.

"She's two hours from transit," said Varga. "Maybe less." He was looking at the scope. "And she's armed it."

"How do you know?"

"Bay four's open. Aft of the drive." He put his finger on the screen. "That's the launch rail. She'll put it out ahead of her into the mouth, on a slow track, and go in after it. It goes off when she's through." His face was grey in the screen-light. "Then the lips fold."

He brought the _Magpie_ in under the carrier's flank, close, so close that Dagny could see the rivets in the blue plating going past above the cracked window, and the Company's leaning letters painted ten metres high on the hull, and then the docking collar, a ring of steel on the _Covenant_'s belly with a lamp beside it. Varga touched the comm and said a string of numbers and letters into it, very calmly, and the lamp beside the collar went from red to green.

"They'll know," Dagny said.

"They'll know someone's docked. They won't know who till they look." He unstrapped. "Stay close behind me. If anyone points anything at you, get down. If I tell you to run, run."

They went up through the collar into the _Covenant_.

## The Mouth of the Throat
[
Place: #The Covenant
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Hale Varga @@Ruth Achterberg @@Pim Ostrova
Items: ^The Director's Seal:@Dagny Ostrova
When: same day
]

Afterwards, Dagny would remember the _Covenant_ as a place of corridors. Long, wide, clean, lit, full of people in Company clothes who stopped and stared as a big scarred man in Security grey went past them at a run with a woman in a Wick-tender's rig behind him. Nobody stopped them. Twice a man in Security armour stepped out of a side passage and saw Varga's face and stepped back in again. Once a young officer with a pistol on his hip said _Chief—_ and Varga said _Not now, Corrie_ without breaking stride, and Dagny turned her head as she passed and saw the young man's face, and knew it — Gallery Eleven, twenty-two years old — and did not stop.

The bridge was at the top of the ship, a long curved room with the Throat filling the whole of its forward wall. Ruth Achterberg was standing in the middle of it, with her officers round her, and the seal at her throat.

She turned when the door opened. She looked at Varga for a long moment. Then she looked at Dagny.

"Ms. Ostrova," she said. "Hale. I did wonder."

"Stand away from the board, Director," said Varga.

"I don't think so."

"Ruth." He said it quite gently. Dagny had never heard him use her first name. "It's over. Turn her round."

"It's not over. It's two hours from over." Achterberg did not move. Her hands were folded in front of her. "The device is on the rail. It goes into the mouth in ninety minutes, on a slow track, and we follow it through. That's the plan. That's been the plan since spring. I'm sorry you've come all this way to watch it."

"You don't have to fire it," said Dagny.

"I don't have to do anything. I am going to." The Director's face did not change. "Did you think there was some other end to this? The inspectors come through, and Caul is found, and the Company falls, and every one of these four hundred and six people loses everything. Their posts, their pensions, their names. And you lose nothing at all, because you have nothing. You'll just be sold to someone else." She shook her head. "No. I'll close the door. It's the only thing left that's mine to do."

"I've quit your claim," said Dagny.

That stopped her. For the first time, something moved in Ruth Achterberg's face.

"At the root," said Dagny. "Three mornings ago. With a key you didn't know existed. Teodor's. He made it the year he built her." She watched the Director's eyes. "Lantern doesn't owe the Company anything. Not a credit. Not one. Go through. Go to the Concord with your books. They're empty."

There was a long silence on the bridge. Somewhere an alarm was chiming softly, over and over, and nobody had turned it off.

"Teodor," said Ruth Achterberg, at last, very quietly.

"He's dead. Your man shot him at the root."

The Director closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them she did not look at Dagny. She looked at the Throat filling the forward wall, its red heart pulsing.

"Then there's nothing to go back for," she said. "And nothing to go through for." Her hand moved, very slightly, at her side. "Mr. Ferris. Launch now, please."

"Director, we're not aligned for transit, we'd be—"

"I know what we'd be. Launch."

Varga moved. He was across the bridge in three strides, and he had the officer at the launch board by the collar and was throwing him bodily away from it, and Dagny was moving too, toward the Director, toward the seal — and it did not matter, because the officer had already pressed it. Dagny felt it through the deck: a long low shudder from somewhere aft, and then a lurch, as something massive left the ship.

On the forward screen, small, dark, a shape slid out ahead of them into the swimming light of the Throat, on a slow straight track, toward its heart.

"How long?" said Varga.

"Ten minutes to the mouth," said the officer from the floor, white. "Then it— then it goes."

"Turn us round," said Varga. "Full burn. Away. Now."

Nobody moved.

"_Now_," Varga roared, and the helm officer turned them.

Dagny did not wait to see. She had the seal. She did not remember taking it. Ruth Achterberg had not fought her; she had stood quite still while Dagny broke the chain from her neck, and then sat down in the nearest chair and folded her hands in her lap and looked at the Throat. Dagny had the brass disk in her fist and she was running, out of the bridge, along the corridor, toward the passenger decks, toward the long cabins aft where the essential staff and their families had been put for the voyage. Somebody had told her, she did not know who. _Aft. Deck four. The hauler and the boy._

She found Pim first. He was in the corridor of deck four, outside a cabin door, alone, standing very straight with his school jacket buttoned and the tin wings on his collar, as if he had known she would come and had been waiting. He saw her and did not run to her. He said, quite calmly:

"He's gone to the _Magpie_."

She stared at him.

"He saw on the screen. When you docked. He saw the _Magpie_ on the collar, and he said, _She's brought the bell_. And then he went." Pim's chin was trembling, very slightly, and he was not letting it. "He said to tell you it's the plan. He said, _Tell her that was always the plan._"

## The Fold
[

]

She reached the docking collar as the _Magpie_ broke from it.

She saw it through the collar's port: the ugly little tug sliding away from the _Covenant_'s blue flank, her crane-arm folded, her cracked forward window catching the red light of the Throat, her drive lighting — a long white stroke of flame, far too much, everything she had — and turning, not away from the Throat as the _Covenant_ was turning, but toward it. Toward the heart. After the small dark shape on its slow straight track.

Dagny hit the comm on the collar wall so hard she split her palm.

"Kas. _Kas._"

The comm hissed. Then her brother's voice, very close, as if he were in the collar beside her, light and easy, the voice from when he was five and had broken something.

"Hey, Dag. You came."

"Kas, turn back. Turn round. Bring it back—"

"Can't, Dag. It's the plan. Your plan. Out in the wake, with the bell, before the door shuts." He laughed, a little breathlessly; she could hear the drive howling under his voice. "No wake, though. Bit of a problem. She's turning. Never mind. The _Magpie_'s done worse. Haven't you, girl?"

"Kas, listen to me, the names have to stay, I've— at the root, I've— Kas, the names are the only—"

"I know, I know. The names get out." The static was rising. "Somebody has to listen. Somebody in the Concord. That's what you said, in the Slake. I heard you." A pause, full of noise. "I'm sorry about Hesketh, Dag. And the girl. And Basu. I'm sorry about all of it. I'm sorry I sold you. I thought I was getting him out. He wouldn't go, you know. Sat in that cabin with his coat buttoned. Said he'd wait." The laugh again, cracking. "Takes after you."

"_Kas_—"

"Tell him to look after you," said her brother. "Tell him he's got my wings."

The small dark shape on its slow straight track reached the heart of the Throat.

She saw it go. A point of white, very small, at the centre of the red. Then the red was not red, and the lens was not a lens; it was folding. The vast slow swimming edges of the Throat came in toward their centre from every side at once, like a curtain drawn, like a hand closing, silent, enormous, unhurried, and the stars on the far side of it smeared and stretched and went out. And small, black, against the closing light, the _Magpie_ went on into it at full burn, a long white stroke of flame driving into the heart of the fold, smaller and smaller, until the fold closed over her and there was nothing there at all. Only the dark, and the ordinary stars, and a thin bright line drawn across the sky where the Throat had been, slowly spreading, slowly dimming.

The comm hissed. It went on hissing.

Then the backwash hit the _Covenant_.

Dagny had been told, afterwards, what it was — Varga told her, in the long weeks after, in the infirmary, when he came to sit with her; that a collapsing throat throws out a shock of hard light and broken space as it folds, and that a ship too near the mouth takes it across her hull like a wave. She did not know it then. She knew only that the deck came up and hit her, and the collar port went white, a white so complete and so hard that she felt it on her face like heat, like the Wick at the axis, and went on feeling it after the light had gone; and that somewhere aft of her the ship screamed, a long tearing shriek of metal, as the cargo hold where the Company's fuel and the Company's harvests and the Company's furniture had been packed for the voyage opened to the sky along forty metres of seam and emptied itself into the dark.

Then it was quiet. The alarms were going everywhere, but it was quiet.

She lay on the deck of the collar with the seal of the Halden-Ash Company in one fist and her split palm bleeding into the other, and her face burning, and looked at the port, where there was only black now, and stars, and a line of light fading across them like a scar.

After a while somebody was holding her. It was Pim. He had followed her down. He was kneeling on the deck beside her with his arms round her shoulders and his face against her hair, and he was saying something over and over, very quietly, and it took her a long time to understand that it was names.

## The Hook
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Ruth Achterberg

]

The _Covenant_ came home on her own reactor, eleven days later, with her hold torn open and her drive half dead and four hundred and six essential staff of the Halden-Ash Company aboard her who had nowhere else on earth or off it to go.

She could not dock at the fore lock, where she had lain for a year before the Closing. Her flank was too badly torn, and her drive too weak to hold her steady. So Varga brought her in at the old cargo dock under the Counting House, the deep one, cut into the rock beneath the vault for the Company's first freighters ninety years before, and nobody had used for thirty; and she went into it nose first and stopped there, and never came out again. There was nowhere for her to go. Within a month the Wick crews had run lines into her for her reactor's heat. Within a year, people had begun to call her simply the ship, and then something else.

Dagny was carried off her on a stretcher. She had known on the fourth day what the white light at the collar port had done. Mrs. Ansah from Gallery Three knew too, though she did not say it; she sat with her in the Company infirmary in the Counting House, in the same bed Teodor had died in, and gave her what there was for the pain, which was not much, and did not lie.

The Director came to see her on the last day.

Ruth Achterberg had come off the _Covenant_ on her own feet, and walked up the cargo dock past the people of Lantern who had come down to see the Company come home, and none of them had touched her. She had gone up to the long window on the top floor of the Counting House, Varga said, and stood at it for a whole day and a night, looking down the length of the world. Then she had come down and begun to work. The Tally needed keeping. The new keys needed learning. Somebody had to sit at the root, and see to the postings, and answer the valves when they asked, and there was nobody left on Lantern who knew how, except the four hundred and six.

She stood at the foot of the bed. She looked older. She did not have the seal.

"I've come for it," she said. "If you'll let me have it."

Dagny looked at her for a long time from the pillow.

"Somebody has to keep the books," said Ruth Achterberg. "Or the air stops. You know that. I can't promise you anything else. I can promise you that."

On the cabinet beside the bed, beside Teodor's glasses with the cracked lens, lay the brass disk of the seal on its broken chain. Dagny moved her hand, slowly — it was hard to move anything now — and pushed it across the cabinet toward the Director.

"Keep them honest," she said. Her voice was a thread. "Every hour of air. Owed to them. Not to you. Not to anybody. To them."

"To them," said Ruth Achterberg. She picked up the seal. She stood holding it, looking down at it in her palm, for a long time. "And the names? The discharge? The Tally will want them."

"The names are on the bell," said Dagny. "The bell's gone through the Throat."

"Then the account can never be closed."

"Not till it comes home," said Dagny. "Six hundred years. Seven. Then somebody will bring it back, maybe. And say their names. And then we'll be done." She closed her eyes. "Until then it's owed. That's all. It's owed."

## Iron on Iron
[
Place: #The Dock Ring
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova:dead @@Pim Ostrova @@Odile Marchbank @@Hale Varga
When: same day
]

She made them take her to the Dock Ring that evening. Varga carried her, because there was no one else she would let, the whole way down the spine-road and along the ring, with Pim walking beside them and Odile Marchbank coming behind on Grace Mbeki's arm. People came out of the stalls and the gantries and the berths to see them pass, and stood still, as they stood for the bell.

The Hook was where it had always been, above the inner lock. Black iron. Empty. The stub of the cut shackle still hanging from it, where she had put the torch to it seventeen days before.

Varga set her down on the bench under it. She could not stand. She sat with Pim's arm round her and looked up at it.

"I've nothing to ring," said Odile, beside her. The old woman's voice was a cracked whisper. "Thirty years I kept her. And I've nothing to ring for you, Dagny. Nothing at all."

"There's the hook," said Dagny.

Odile looked at her.

Pim understood first. He got up from the bench, and went to the berth wall where the haulers kept their tools, and came back with a length of iron pipe as long as his arm, dark and heavy. He held it out to her. She could not lift it. So he held it, and she put her hands over his — her burned hands over his steady ones, the way Odile's had lain over his on the box under the bell — and together they raised it, and struck the empty Hook.

It was not a bell. It was iron on iron, a flat hard clang, poor and ugly, swallowed at once by the rock. It went out across the Dock Ring anyway. All along the ring road, people stopped what they were doing and stood still for it.

"For Kas," said Dagny.

They struck it again.

"For Teodor Basu. For Ezra Hesketh. For Dorothea Clay. For Joss."

Again. Again.

"For Abara, Joseph," said Pim. "Achebe, Ruth. Adair, Michael." He did not stop. He knew them; he had read them aloud on the box for fourteen nights. "Agbaje, Femi. Aguilar, Rosa—"

Dagny's hands slid off his on the pipe. He did not notice at first. He went on striking the Hook, alone, on his own, and saying the names, one for each stroke, Ahmed and Akande and Albrecht, and the people of the Dock Ring stood along the ring road in the evening light under the Wick and listened to him, and some of them began, under their breath, to say the names after him; and Hale Varga stood with his scarred hands at his sides and his face wet, and did not move; and Odile Marchbank, beside the bench, put her shaking hand very gently over Dagny Ostrova's eyes, and closed them, and left her hand there.

"Owing," said Odile, so quietly that only the dead woman could have heard. "Owed."

Pim went on until he could not lift the pipe. Then somebody else took it from him and went on.
