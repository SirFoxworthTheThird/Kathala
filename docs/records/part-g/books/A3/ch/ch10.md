## Confession
[
Place: #The Galleries
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Teodor Basu
Items: ^The Caul List
When: +10 days
]

He asked to meet her in the chapel on Gallery Four, at the end of third shift, when the galleries were as empty as they ever were; and when she came in, he was sitting on the front bench under the tea-coloured window with a paper folder on his knees, and he did not get up.

"Sit down, please," said Teodor Basu. "I'd rather— I'd rather not have to look at you while I say it. If that's all right."

She sat at the other end of the bench. Between them on the crate-wood was the folder. It was thick. Its edges were worn soft, as though it had been carried a long way, or handled a great deal.

"In the spring," said Basu, "there was an accident at Caul Deep."

He said it to the window. To the ship in the resin, crossing the dark sea toward its lamp.

"The main shaft. Shaft One. Six hundred metres down through the crust into the old ice. The ice down there isn't like the ice on the surface. It's under pressure. It moves, very slowly, like— like glass in an old window. The shaft engineers had been reporting creep in the lower bracing for two years. Every quarter. Every quarter I took their figures and set them against the budget for re-bracing, and every quarter the re-bracing would have cost the Deep its season's yield, and every quarter the Director decided it could wait, and I agreed with her." He drew a breath. "I agreed with her. I want to be clear about that. It isn't her decision I've come to confess. It's mine. I had the figures. I could have refused to sign the budget. I didn't."

Dagny did not move.

"On the fourth of April the lower bracing failed at four hundred and eighty metres," Basu said. "The pressure ice came into the shaft. All of it, all at once. It's— there's a word the engineers used. A blowout. It fills a space the way water fills a bucket, except that it's ice, and it freezes as it comes. Shaft One was at full shift. Nine hundred and four people were below the three-hundred-metre station." His voice did not change. "None of them came up."

The chapel was very quiet. Somewhere far below them a pump thudded, and stopped.

"Varga's men went down the next day. There were— there were some at the lift station at three hundred metres. Who had got that far. Behind them the shaft was solid. Varga reported that recovery was impossible: it would take a year to cut the ice, and the shaft might go again while they cut. The Director asked me what a year's recovery would cost. I told her. She asked me what it would cost if it were known. In the Concord. The Labour Inspectorate had already fined the Company twice for Caul. A third finding, with nine hundred dead, would end the Concern. Possibly the Company." He swallowed. "So the Director ordered the shaft sealed at the three-hundred-metre station. And she asked me to make the nine hundred go away."

"Go away," said Dagny.

"From the Tally. From the books." He put his hand on the folder, flat, as if holding it shut. "Every person on Lantern is an account. When an account closes, the Tally posts why. Death, or completion of contract. If nine hundred accounts had closed by death in one hour, it would be in every hourly posting that went out to the Concord on the data-runs. So I closed them another way. I posted them as contracts bought out by the Company. Terms completed. Repatriated, through the Throat, on the spring transport." He was speaking very precisely now, every word placed. "I wrote letters, for the ones who had family here. From the Concord. _Arrived safe. Doing well. Will send for you._ I wrote four hundred and thirty-one letters, Ms. Ostrova. I sent them out on one data-run and had them come back in on the next. I copied their handwriting from their payroll signatures."

Dagny stood up.

She did not decide to. She was on her feet and then she was in front of him and she had hit him, open-handed, so hard across the face that his head turned and his glasses went skittering off along the bench and onto the floor. He did not put up his hands. He sat there with his face turned away and the mark of her hand coming up red on his cheek, and waited, and when she did not hit him again, he turned his head back and looked at her for the first time, with his naked short-sighted eyes.

"Yes," he said. "Thank you."

"Don't you _thank_ me—"

"I'm sorry. I mean it. Nobody's— in eight months, nobody has done that." He bent, slowly, and felt along the floor for his glasses, and found them, and put them on. One lens was cracked. "There's more. I'm afraid there's more."

She found she was shaking. She sat down again, not on the bench, on the step in front of it, with her back to him, looking at the dark empty chapel.

"The Inspectorate noticed," Basu said behind her. "Not the deaths. The arrivals. Nine hundred people repatriated through the Throat on the spring transport, and not one of them ever landed anywhere in the Concord. Not one ever registered for work, or drew a pension, or wrote home. It took them a few months to notice. Then it took them a few months more to be sure. The notice came in on the data-run three weeks before the Director's announcement. A Labour Inspectorate fleet, four ships, with a commission. Due through the Throat in a hundred days." He paused. "Three weeks later, the throat-watch recorded the onset of a closing phase."

Dagny turned round.

"That's—"

"Yes," said Teodor Basu. "I thought so too."

He held out the folder. She did not take it. He set it on the step beside her.

"Those are their names," he said. "All nine hundred and four. From payroll. I've— I've been carrying them. I don't know what else to do with them."

## Caul Deep
[
Place: #Caul Deep
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Kas Ostrova
Items: ^The Caul List
When: +1 day
]

Kas took her to Caul the next day in the _Magpie_, off the books, on a run he logged as a tank inspection, and for the six hours of the flight he did not make a single joke.

Caul came up out of the dark as a white ball streaked with grey, very bright against the bruise-coloured bulk of Nhai behind it. Close to, it was not white at all. It was a hundred shades of blue and grey and dirty yellow, cracked and ridged and pitted, with long straight scars across it where the haulers' landing-tracks ran and, at the bottom of a vast frozen basin on its leading face, a cluster of gantries and domes and towers that had been, until the spring, the biggest working mine in the Sabra Concern.

It was dark. Kas had said so, the day Lemuel died. _Used to be you'd see the lights of it from a thousand klicks out like a city._ It looked now like what it was: a town where nobody lived. The landing beacon was dead. The dome lights were dead. Frost had grown on the gantries, long feathers of it, the way frost grew on anything metal that was left alone on Caul. Kas brought the _Magpie_ down on the old pad by hand, slowly, with the floodlights on, and their light went out over a field of abandoned equipment — ice-cutters, haul-sleds, a crawler on its side — and threw long black shadows away across the ice.

"Shaft One's under the big tower," Kas said. "The lift head. I used to see the men going down in the morning. Shift-change, you'd get a queue of a hundred, two hundred, on the gantry. Waving at the haulers." He was looking straight ahead through the cracked window. "I waved back. Every week."

They suited up. They went across the pad to the tower, and in at the lift-head door, which was not locked — who would come? — and down.

The lift was dead, so they used the emergency ladder, a cage of steel rungs bolted down the side of the shaft beside the cable runs. It was three hundred metres to the station. Dagny had climbed the Wick every day of her life and she had never climbed anything like it: down and down in the narrow dark with her lamp on the rungs and the walls of the shaft going past, smooth grey ice-crete at first and then, lower, the old ice itself, blue-black and glassy, so clear in places that her light went into it a metre and showed her things frozen there, stones and bubbles and once a dead tool, hanging in the ice as if in air. Kas came down above her. She could hear his breathing on the suit channel, harsh and quick.

At three hundred metres the ladder ended on a steel platform, and the platform ended in a wall.

It was ice-crete. Poured, new, grey, filling the whole width of the shaft from side to side like a cork in a bottle, with a heavy steel inspection hatch set in the middle of it and the hatch welded shut. Somebody had stencilled on it, in Company blue, SHAFT CLOSED — NO ENTRY — BY ORDER. Under the stencil, very small, the leaning letters.

Beside the hatch there was a port. A thick round window of armoured glass, the kind they put in pressure walls, at head height, with its own little lamp behind it, dead.

Dagny put her light to the glass.

At first she saw only blue. A deep cold glassy blue, like looking into a lake. Then her eyes understood what they were looking at, and she did not move, and did not breathe, and heard on the channel behind her Kas say a word that was not a word.

The lift station was full of ice. It had come up the shaft from below and filled the station to the roof and frozen there. And it was full of people.

They were standing. Most of them were standing. Caught in the act of running toward the lift — toward her — in their mine-suits and helmets, frozen in mid-stride, arms out, heads turned, some of them holding on to each other. The ice was so clear she could see their faces. Some had their eyes open. A woman near the front had her hand up, flat, the palm toward the port, as if she had been reaching for it. Behind her, deeper in the blue, there were more. And more behind those. As far as the light would go, there were people in the ice, crowded, standing, reaching, going back and back into the dark of the shaft until the blue swallowed them.

And on the inner face of the hatch — on the inside, where the ice had not quite reached, in a pocket of clear dark space between the frozen people and the steel — there were marks. Long pale gouges in the paint. Five of them. Then five more. Like fingers dragged down a door.

Dagny looked at them for a long time.

"They poured the seal," she said. Her voice on the channel sounded very far away. "With some of them still on this side of the ice. Still alive. They poured it, and welded the hatch, and went up."

Kas said nothing. She heard him breathing.

"Count them," she said.

"Dag—"

"Count them. The ones you can see. I'll count too. Then we'll put them together."

They counted. It took a long time. The faces in the ice went back further than either of them could see, and they lost count, and began again, and lost it again. In the end Dagny said "That's enough," and Kas said "I got a hundred and twelve," and she said "A hundred and nine," and they stood together on the steel platform in the dark with their lamps on the port and did not say anything else for a while.

Then Dagny took the folder out of her suit's thigh pocket, where she had carried it all the way down — Teodor's paper folder, with nine hundred and four names in it — and held it up flat against the armoured glass, so that the woman with her palm raised could see it, if she could see anything. She did not know why. It was a stupid thing to do. She held it there anyway, until her arm ached.

"I'll bring them up," she said to the glass. "Your names. I promise you. I'll bring your names up."

On the way back, in the _Magpie_'s cabin, with the white ball of Caul shrinking behind them against the planet, Kas said the only thing he said on the whole flight home.

"Every week," he said. "I flew past them every week, and waved."

## Class T
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Teodor Basu
Items: ^The Tally
When: same day
]

Basu was waiting for her at the foot of the Wick when the _Magpie_ docked, and he did not need to ask. He looked at her face and nodded, and then he said, "Come up. Please. There's something I can show you now. I couldn't before."

He took her up into the Counting House by the service stair, past the offices, to a narrow room at the back of the comptroller's floor where there was nothing but a wall of old screens and a chair. There were no windows. On every screen there were figures.

"The throat-watch station," he said. "Its raw feed. Not the summaries that come to the Director's office. The raw readings, every six hours, from the instruments. They come to my floor first, for the accounts — the Company charges the Concern for the station's running, so I log its output. Nobody's ever looked at them but me and the man who sends them. A man called Roach. He's been out there eleven years." He sat down in the chair and began touching screens, bringing up columns. "I'm not a physicist. But I can read a trend. I've read nothing but trends for thirty years. I went back through every reading for the last six months, last night, after I left you."

He turned the chair round to face her.

"There is no closing phase," said Teodor Basu. "There never was. The Throat's breathing is exactly what it's been for ninety-one years. Regular as a clock. The numbers in the Director's announcement — the ones she said came from the throat-watch — they aren't in the throat-watch's feed. They aren't anywhere. Somebody in the Director's office made them up." He took his glasses off and rubbed his eyes, carefully, round the cracked lens. "Roach has been asking for confirmation. Three times. Nobody's answered him."

Dagny sat down on the floor with her back against the door, because there was nowhere else to sit and her legs had stopped holding her.

"Then it's not closing," she said. "It's not going to close. We could just— wait. Let them go. And in fifty days the inspectors come through, and—"

"It's going to close," said Basu.

He turned back to the screens and touched one, and the columns of throat-watch figures gave way to a single page of text. A manifest entry. She knew the format; she had seen him look at it before, on the bench by the bell.

_Device, Concord ordnance, class T. Shielded bay 4, aft of drive. Received under Security seal._

"I found out what class T means," he said. "It took me a while. It's not in any Company catalogue. It's in the Concord's own military register, the part that's public, under _throat ordnance_. They were made during the Succession Wars, two hundred years ago, when the Concord was fighting itself through every throat it had. A fleet that was losing would go back through a throat and then—" he made a small movement with his hand, a closing— "behind them. So the enemy couldn't follow."

"Close it," said Dagny.

"Collapse it. The register calls it a shear. A throat-shear. It's fired into the mouth of a throat by a ship in transit, and it— the physics is beyond me. It cuts the throat's lips. They fold in. The throat collapses. And it stays collapsed, Ms. Ostrova, for six or seven or eight hundred years, exactly as the Director said in her announcement, because that much of what she said is true: a collapsed throat heals on its own time and no one else's." He looked at her. "The Company bought a shear from a Concord arms broker three months ago. Before the throat-watch said anything at all. Before the Director announced anything at all. The _Covenant_ will carry it into the Throat on the day she leaves, with four hundred and six essential staff aboard, and fire it behind her as she comes out the other side. And the Throat will close on the day the Director said it would, and the inspectors will be on the other side of it, and the nine hundred and four will be under the ice of Caul for ever, and nobody in the Concord will ever know. Except as a line in a Company report. _Repatriated._"

"And us."

"And you. Nine thousand people who know what Caul is, sealed in a box with a meter on the air, with your contracts held in trust." He spread his hands. "The Company doesn't need to silence you. The Throat will do it."

For a long time neither of them said anything. Dagny sat against the door with her knees up and her hands over her face and saw, on the inside of her eyelids, the blue of the ice and the woman with her palm on the glass.

Then she took her hands away.

"Then we stop the shear," she said. "We stop it, and we let the inspectors come."

Teodor Basu looked at her for a long moment through his cracked glasses.

"Yes," he said. "I hoped you would say that."

## The Traffic Log
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Hale Varga
When: same day
]

On the security floor of the Counting House, two levels below them, in an office with a desk and a cot and a wall of camera screens, Hale Varga sat alone in the dark and watched a recording.

It was from the Dock Ring traffic log. An unscheduled departure, nineteen hours ago: hauler _Magpie_, pilot Ostrova K., logged as tank inspection, flight plan filed for the Caul run. And a return, an hour ago, from the same heading. Six hours out, six hours back. Long enough for a landing. Long enough for a climb.

He sat with it for a while, his big scarred hands flat on the desk. On another screen, from a camera on the comptroller's floor that the Comptroller did not know was there, Dagny Ostrova sat against a door with her face in her hands.

Then Varga reached out, and deleted the _Magpie_'s flight from the traffic log, and deleted the camera's last hour, and sat on in the dark with nothing on his screens at all.
