## The Lip
[
Place: #The Cold Wards
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask
]

The light ended at the Lip.

It ended the way a tide ends on a beach Tamsin Hask had seen once in a picture-book: not in a line, but in a long uncertain fringe, where the terraces were half lit and half not and the frost began to show on the north faces of things. Above the Lip the Wick still burned, a bar of white fire down the middle of the sky, and the Galleries climbed the walls of the world in their thousands, warm, stinking, lit. Below it — aft of it, the old people said, because the Founders had called it aft — the lamp simply stopped. The last two kilometres of Lantern's axis were a dark rod, and under that dark rod lay the Cold Wards, and nobody had lived in the Cold Wards for longer than anybody had been alive.

Tamsin clipped her tether to the Lip post, an iron spike hammered into the road by some cutter a century dead, and checked the other end on her harness. Forty metres of line. Then the reel at her hip, two hundred more. Then her own good sense, which was free.

"Going in," she said to nobody. Habit. Her mother's habit, from the pumps: you said the thing out loud so the air knew you were in it.

She pulled the mask down. The rig's heater hummed along her spine. She walked into the dark.

It was not quite dark. That was the first thing you learned. The Wick's light carried a long way along the curve of the rock, bouncing from terrace to terrace, so that for the first few hundred metres the Cold was a blue dusk, and only further in, past the old Pump Galleries, did it become the true black you needed the lamp on your brow for. The second thing you learned was that the Cold was not empty. People had lived here once. Their houses were still here, frost-furred, doors standing open; their washing still hung on lines between terraces, frozen into boards. There were tables with cups on them. There was a child's bicycle, which Tamsin passed every trip, leaning against a wall with its front wheel turned as if somebody had just stepped off it. She didn't touch it. Cutters didn't touch the small things. You took metal, and you left the rest to the dark.

She was working a bus-bar in the old Pump Gallery Fourteen, a fat run of copper as thick as her wrist that fed a dead scrubber bank, and she had been working it for six days. Copper was the best thing in the Cold. It was what the Count's foundries paid for, and what the Salt paid more for, and for a cutter with a family balance like hers it was the difference between a door that opened and one that didn't. The bar ran forty metres along the gallery ceiling and she had got eleven of it out. Today she thought she might get four more.

The air in the Pump Galleries was thin and cold enough to hurt through the mask. She could hear her own breath, loud, and the tick of the heater, and the scritch of the saw when she started it, and under all of that the deep slow grumble of the rock itself, which never stopped, which was the sound of Lantern turning. Frost fell from the bar where she cut it and drifted down in the light of her brow-lamp, sideways, the way things fell on Lantern, curving as they went.

She was on her third metre when the light went.

Not her light. The other one. Even here, a kilometre into the Cold, there was a faint wash of it in the gallery mouth behind her, and she saw it go: blue to grey to nothing, and then, after a breath, back. And then out again. And back.

Tamsin turned off the saw and stood with her heart going.

The gutter, people called it. Everybody had seen it by now. Three times in the last month, five the month before. The Wick faltering for a second or two, so that all over Lantern the light stammered and every person on every terrace looked up together, mouths open, the way you'd look up at a sound in the night. Then it would steady and everybody would look down again and go on as if they hadn't.

It steadied. The dusk came back into the gallery mouth. Tamsin let her breath out through her teeth.

"Not today," she said. "Not with me in here."

She started the saw again.

It was on the way out, with four metres of copper strapped to her back and the reel winding at her hip, that she found the hatch.

She'd taken a different way. That was the third thing you learned about the Cold: never quite the same way twice, because the rock moved, a little, over the years, and ice built where it built, and a road you knew could be a slide of frost between one trip and the next. Her usual route through the back of Fourteen had iced over, so she went down a level and along a service way she didn't know, keeping the tether in mind, counting metres.

The service way ended in a door. Not a house door. A big one, set into the raw rock of the outer wall, with a frame of riveted steel and a wheel at its centre the size of a cart-wheel, rimed white. Tamsin put her brow-lamp on it and brushed at the frost with her glove, and the letters came up under the ice in the old square type nobody used any more.

The Company.

She stood looking at it for a long time. Everybody on Lantern knew the story, the way everybody knew the Account. Once upon a time there had been a Company, and the Company had owned the rock and everything in it, and the Company had built the Wick and the Tally and the locks, and then the Throat had begun to close and the Company had run. Out through the Throat, in their ships, with everything they could carry, and the door shut behind them and they never came back. They took the good fuel and the medicines and the bell from the Dock Ring. They left the Tally, and the Account, and the people. That was the story. You heard it in school and in church and in the songs they sang at the Hook.

And here was one of their doors, still shut.

The seam round the frame had been welded. Not with a cutter's torch, either; it was a proper bead, wide and even, the work of somebody who'd meant it to stay shut for ever. On the floor in front of it, half under the frost, was a body.

That wasn't unusual. There were bodies all over the Cold. People had died in it when it was first let go, and people had died in it since — cutters, runaways, the drunk, the dimmed who had nowhere else to sleep. The cold kept them. Tamsin knelt and put her light on this one because it was the decent thing and because sometimes they had a chit that could be turned in to the Count, which paid a finder's tenth on any balance closed by death.

It was a woman, she thought, though it was hard to say. Dried to leather by three centuries of cold dry air. She was wearing old coveralls with the H-A letters on the shoulder, and she was sitting with her back to the hatch and her knees drawn up, and in her two hands, held against her chest, was a child's shoe. Just the one. Small. Red once.

There was a chit on a cord round her neck. Tamsin turned it to the light. The number was rubbed away, and the old-style printing on the face of it was mostly gone, but the balance was still there, in brackets, as balances always were when they were bad. Two thousand and something.

She didn't take it. She didn't know why. She sat back on her heels and looked at the woman and the shoe and the welded door, and the frost fell sideways through the light, and from very far away, through a kilometre of cold and dark and rock, she heard the Hook ring.

Somebody was dead.

## The Owing
[
Place: #The Galleries
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Agathe Hask @@Jory Hask
Items: ^Tally Chit
When: same day
]

The Hask room was on Gallery Twenty-Two, which was a low gallery, near the Lip, where the light came in sideways and never quite warmed anything. It was three metres by four. It had a bunk for her mother and a bunk for Jory and a mat for Tamsin, a ring-stove, a water tap with a meter on it, a door that opened when the family was in credit and didn't when it wasn't, and on a shelf over the stove, a tin.

The tin had held biscuits once, a long time ago, in somebody else's family. Now it held chits. Every chit the Hasks had ever owned was in that tin: Tamsin's grandmother's, her grandfather's, her great-grandmother's, a great-uncle who had died at nineteen in a pump accident, a baby who hadn't got a name. When you died on Lantern your chit came home. That was the law. The Count didn't want it; the Count kept the numbers. The family kept the metal, so that it would remember what it owed.

Agathe Hask was sitting up on her bunk with the tin in her lap when Tamsin came in. She did that sometimes, took the chits out and laid them on the blanket in rows, like a game of patience. Her lips moved while she did it. Tamsin had asked her once what she said, and her mother had said, "Their names, what do you think," and gone on.

"You're late," said Agathe, and coughed.

The cough was the lung-rot. Everybody below Gallery Fifteen got it in the end, from the damp and the mould and the scrubbers that were older than the Count; it came on slow and stayed for years, and it took you by inches, and the Tally charged you for the extra air. Agathe had had it for six years. The last two she'd been off the pumps, which meant she wasn't earning, which meant every breath she drew went on the balance.

"Got four metres," Tamsin said. She swung the copper down onto the floor and stretched her back till it cracked. "Good four. Thick."

"Count price or Salt price?"

"Salt."

Her mother's mouth went thin. Agathe had been a pump-hand for thirty years and a churchgoer for all of them, and she didn't like the Salt, and she didn't like that the Salt was where the money was. But she didn't say anything. She'd stopped saying things about money about the time she'd stopped earning any.

"Where's Jory?"

"Out. Running for somebody. Said he'd be back for the Hook."

"Who died?"

"Old Pask. Wim Pask, Twenty-Three." Agathe coughed again, longer this time, and when she was done she sat with her eyes closed and her hand flat on her chest as though she were holding something down. "In his sleep. His door dimmed last week. He'd been going up to sleep on the Hook steps. They found him there this morning."

Tamsin sat down on her mat. She thought about the woman by the hatch, with the shoe. She didn't say anything about the hatch. Not yet.

"How much was he over?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters to whoever gets it."

"His granddaughter. On Nineteen." Agathe opened her eyes. They were very pale, her mother's eyes, almost colourless in the bad light; Tamsin had them, and so did Jory. The Hask eyes, people said, as if it were a family heirloom, which in a way it was. "She's got two of her own. She'll be under by the summer."

They sat for a bit. The water meter on the tap ticked over, once, as somebody somewhere on the family's account drew a cup. Probably Jory, wherever he was.

"Mam."

"What."

"Who's Caul?"

Her mother looked at her as if she had asked what air was.

"You know who Caul is."

"I know what they say in church. I know it's the Account. Caul is owed and the Owing pay. I know the moon's called Caul and the Salt run ice off it. I mean who." She heard her own voice, and it was too loud for the room, and she didn't care. "Who's the money going _to_, Mam? We've owed Caul for three hundred years. Everybody on this rock owes Caul. Every hour of air, every cup out of that tap. Where does it go? Who's sitting on the other side of the book, getting fat on it?"

"Nobody's getting fat." Agathe's voice was quiet. "Caul is owed. That's all. Caul is the dark that kept us. When the Company ran and left us in it, the dark could have taken us, and it didn't. It let us breathe. So we owe it. That's what the Account is. That's what the Count keeps. You know all this."

"That's church."

"That's the truth. Same thing, down here." Agathe began to put the chits back in the tin, one at a time, in their order. "You want to know who's on the other side of the book, you go up to the Count and ask them. They've got the book."

The door opened. It made the noise it always made, a hydraulic sigh and a clunk, and the little light by the frame went green, and Jory came in sideways, the way he came in everywhere, as if he were expecting to have to leave in a hurry. He was fifteen and all elbows. He had a cut on his lip that hadn't been there this morning.

"They're ringing for Pask," he said. "Everyone's going. You coming?"

"What happened to your face?"

"Ran into something." He grinned, and the cut opened, and he licked it. "There's going to be a crier after. From the Count. Whole of the Hook's full of grey coats. Something's happening."

Agathe put the lid on the tin.

"Help me up," she said.

## The Hook
[
Place: #The Dock Ring
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Agathe Hask @@Jory Hask
When: same day
]

The Hook was an iron bracket the length of a man's arm, black with age, drilled into the rock wall of the Dock Ring above the inner lock. There was nothing on it. There had been nothing on it for three hundred and eleven years. It was the only empty thing on Lantern that nobody had ever tried to fill.

Under it there was a square, as much of a square as the curve of the rock allowed, and the square was full. Gallery Twenty-Three had come for Wim Pask, and so had a good part of Twenty-Two and Twenty-Four, and a crowd of dockhands from the Salt Docks aft along the ring, and the noodle-sellers, and children who had nothing better to do. Tamsin got her mother onto the steps at the side where she could lean on the wall, and Jory slid off into the crowd at once, and Tamsin stood with her hand on her mother's shoulder and watched Pask's granddaughter go up to the Hook.

She was a young woman, younger than Tamsin, with a baby on her hip and a toddler holding her trouser leg. One of the old men handed her the bar. It was a length of iron pipe, worn smooth and dark at the gripping end by three hundred years of hands. The woman stood under the Hook and looked up at the empty bracket and, after a moment, with the baby on her hip, she swung the bar up and struck it.

There was no bell. There had not been a bell since the Closing. What there was, was the iron Hook ringing against iron, a hard flat clang that went out across the square and along the ring and was swallowed by the rock. It was a poor sound. It was not meant to be anything else. Everybody in the square stood still for it, and touched their chits, and said, under their breath, "Owing and owed."

"Owing and owed," said Agathe, and coughed.

The young woman handed back the bar. Nobody would cut Wim Pask's name into anything. There was nothing to cut it into. His chit would go into the woman's own tin, on Nineteen, beside her mother's and her father's, and his balance would go onto her account, and the baby on her hip would carry a part of it from the day she got her own chit at six. That was all. That was what a death was, on the Owing side of Lantern.

Tamsin had always thought the Hook was the saddest thing on the rock. She'd said so once, when she was small, and her grandmother had told her not to be soft. _The Company took the bell when they ran_, her grandmother had said. _They took the bell and every name on it. We ring the Hook so we remember there was a bell, and there were names, and somebody took them. That's not sad. That's angry. Get it right._

## The Comptroller-General
[
Place: #The Dock Ring
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Agathe Hask @@Imre Vosk @@Brother Lusk
When: same day
]

The crier came out onto the gallery above the inner lock as the square was breaking up, and the square stopped breaking up.

He wasn't a crier, though. Tamsin saw that straight off, and felt the crowd see it too, a ripple going back through it like wind through a strip-farm. Criers were boys in grey with a slate and a loud voice. This was a man of about sixty in a long coat of a grey so dark it was nearly black, with silver at the cuffs, and behind him on the gallery were half a dozen men in the ordinary grey of the Count's bailiffs, with their docket-batons through their belts, and at their head a big pale man Tamsin knew by sight and by reputation: Brother Lusk, the Bailiff of the Count, who had walked more families into the dimming than anybody alive.

The man in the dark coat did not need a slate. When he spoke his voice was soft, and it went everywhere.

"Owing and owed," he said.

"Owing and owed," said the square, raggedly.

"My name is Imre Vosk. I am the Comptroller-General of the Count, and I keep the Account for all of you, as my predecessors have kept it since the Closing. I have come down to the Hook myself today because I have something to say that ought not to be said by a boy with a slate.

"You have seen the gutter." He let that sit. "You have all seen it. I won't insult you by pretending you haven't. The Wick is failing. The fuel the Founders saved from the Closing was meant to last three hundred years, and it has, and a little more. The Wickwrights tell me it will not last a great deal longer. They tell me that at the present length of the lamp, the last cassette will be spent before the end of this year."

The square made no sound at all. Tamsin felt her mother's shoulder go rigid under her hand.

"The Count has known this was coming," Vosk said, "and has made such provision as it can. From the first of next month the Wick will be shortened by a further third. The Galleries below Fifteen will go to half-light. The strip-farms will be consolidated in the Upper Wards, where the light is surest. That will buy us time. It will not buy us enough. We have too many mouths and too much air to warm and not enough fire to warm it with. And so the Count has taken a decision which no Count has taken since the Closing, and which I take with grief.

"The Account will be called."

Tamsin did not know what that meant. She knew, from the sound the square made, that some of the old people did.

"The ten thousand accounts on Lantern which stand deepest in debt to Caul," Vosk said, "will be released from the Tally's charge. The Tally will no longer supply their air, or their water, or their heat. Those so released may go into the Cold Wards, where the Count will provide such shelter as the Cold allows, and they will owe nothing more from that day, for the Account will hold them quit. The lists will be posted at the Hook in thirty days, by family. I am told there will be those who call this cruelty." He looked out over them, and his voice did not change at all. "I call it arithmetic. If we do not do this, all of us go dark together by the spring. If we do, most of us live. Caul is owed, and Caul will be paid. Owing and owed."

He turned and went back through the gallery door, and the bailiffs closed it after him, and Brother Lusk stood for a moment longer, looking down over the square, before he followed.

Nobody said "owing and owed." For a long time nobody said anything.

Then somebody at the back threw something at the closed door — a shoe, Tamsin thought, it was hard to see — and it struck the door and dropped onto the gallery, and the square began to move again, and the noise came up all at once, a hundred people talking and two or three screaming and one old woman somewhere singing the Hook song in a cracked, steady voice.

Tamsin looked down at her mother.

Agathe had her chit out. She was holding it to the light, the way they all were, all over the square, a thousand people turning a thousand small metal tokens in their fingers and reading the numbers on them, as if the numbers might have changed in the last ten minutes. Agathe's balance was minus eleven thousand two hundred and six. Tamsin knew that without looking. She'd known it every day for two years.

She knew, too, without asking anybody, where minus eleven thousand stood in the ten thousand deepest accounts on Lantern.

"Mam," she said.

"I heard him." Agathe put the chit back inside her collar. Her hands were steady. She coughed, and wiped her mouth, and looked up at the empty Hook. "Get me home, Tam. And then go and find your brother. He'll have heard, and he'll do something stupid."

Tamsin got her home. Then she stood in the Hask room in the sideways light with the copper still on the floor and the tin on the shelf, and she did not go and find her brother. She thought about a hatch in the Cold, welded shut, with H-A on it, and a dead woman holding a shoe.
