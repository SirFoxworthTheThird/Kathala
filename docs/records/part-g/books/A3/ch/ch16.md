## Zeller, Hanna
[
Place: #The Dock Ring
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Odile Marchbank @@Pim Ostrova
Items: ^The Mercy Bell ^The Caul List
When: +11 days
]

They cut the last name at eleven at night on the forty-seventh day, with half the Dock Ring watching.

It was Grace Mbeki's turn on the box, but she stood down from it when they came to the last page of Teodor's folder, and held out the engraver to Odile. The old woman had not cut a name in four days. Her hands had gone. They shook all the time now, even in her lap, even asleep; she had spent the last four days on the bench under the bell with a blanket round her, reading names aloud for the others in a voice that had gone to a whisper, and sleeping between them. She looked at the engraver for a long time.

"I'll hold it," said Pim. "You hold my hand. Like you did."

So they did it that way. Pim on the box, with the engraver, and Odile standing behind him on the ring road with her shaking hand laid over his steady one, the way she had laid it over his on the first night; and the last name came up bright on the founders' band, at the very end of the long cramped grey line that went all the way round the bell's waist and met its own beginning.

_Zeller, Hanna._

Pim stepped down.

Then Odile took the engraver out of his hand. Nobody stopped her. She climbed onto the box herself, slowly, with Grace Mbeki holding her elbow, and put the burr to the crown of the bell, up among the old cast leaves of the decoration where there was no room left for a name, and cut something there, very small, with her own shaking hand, so that the letters wandered off their line and came back to it. It took her half an hour. When she came down she would not say what she had written. _It's for whoever has her next_, was all she said.

Nobody said anything. All along the ring road, where people had been sitting with their backs to the rock every night for fourteen nights saying the names after the reader, there was a silence like the silence for the bell. Odile looked up at the bronze for a long time — at the band, full now, nine hundred and four names round the founders' belt that nobody had touched for ninety-one years — and then she reached up, shaking, and took the bell-rope, and rang it.

Once.

It was not for anybody. There was no new death. She rang it anyway, and the low sour note went out across the Dock Ring and round the curve of the world in the dark, and on the ring road the people who had been saying the names stood up, one by one, and stood still for it, and then, without anyone telling them to, began to go home.

"That's all of them," said Odile. "That's all of them, Dagny."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me." The old woman lowered herself onto the bench. "Go and do what you're going to do with it."

## The Empty Hook
[
Place: #The Dock Ring
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Kas Ostrova @@Pim Ostrova
Items: ^The Mercy Bell:#The Magpie
When: same day
]

They took the bell down at two in the morning.

Kas brought the _Magpie_'s crane-arm round from the hauler berth on the ring's outer face, where she lay in her cradle with her tank-frame open and empty, and three of the Wick crew climbed the Hook with chains and slings. It took an hour. The iron hook had held the bell for ninety-one years and did not want to let it go; the shackle had rusted into the bracket, and in the end Dagny had to cut it with a torch, with the bell hanging in the slings beside her face, close enough to read the names on the shoulder by the light of the flame. _Joss Teague. Bronwen Price. Lemuel Adeyemi._ When the shackle parted the bell swung, once, in the slings, and gave a small low hum, and the whole ring road heard it, and nobody spoke.

They lowered it into a cargo cradle and wheeled it out through the inner lock along the berth to the _Magpie_, and Kas's crane lifted it into the tank-frame and his crew strapped it down in a nest of ice-sacking, between the empty tanks, where it looked very small.

Then there was nothing on the Hook.

Dagny stood under it afterwards for a while, on her own, when the others had gone. It was strange. She had never once in her life looked up at the Dock Ring's inner lock and not seen the bell. The Hook was only an iron bracket now, black, empty, with the stub of the cut shackle hanging from it. She reached up and touched the stub. It was still warm from the torch.

"Mum."

Pim had not gone home. He had been sitting on the bench in the dark all along, watching.

"Bed," she said. "It's nearly morning."

"Is Uncle Kas going tomorrow? With the bell?"

"After the _Covenant_. If we can do what we have to do in the morning. Then he goes, in her wake." She sat down beside him on the bench. "He'll be in the Concord. With the names. Somebody there will have to listen."

"And he won't come back."

"No."

Pim was quiet for a long time. Then he said, in a very small voice, "I saw him in the Slake. When you told him. In the mirror." He did not look at her. "He looked funny."

"Funny how?"

"I don't know." He was turning his dimmed chit over and over in his fingers. "Like when he loses at shove and pretends he doesn't mind."

She put her arm round him. "He's frightened, Pim. He's going through a throat in a tug with a shear going off behind him. Anyone would be."

"Yes," said Pim. "I suppose."

Kas came back along the berth a little before dawn, with his helmet under his arm, to say goodnight. He hugged Dagny hard, for a long time, harder than he had since their mother's funeral, and then he crouched in front of Pim on the bench and unpinned the little tin pilot's wings from his collar — the cheap enamel pin the hauler crews gave each other when somebody qualified — and pinned them on Pim's school jacket.

"Look after her," he said.

"You look after the bell," said Pim.

Kas laughed, and ruffled his hair, and went.

## The Root
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Teodor Basu @@Ruth Achterberg @@Hale Varga
Items: ^The Tally ^The Director's Seal ^The Comptroller's Key
When: +1 day
]

The root of the Tally was three doors down, under the Counting House, in a place that nobody on Lantern had ever seen.

Teodor brought them in. He had told Dagny the way three nights before, in the locker room, with a diagram scratched on the back of a ration card: the comptroller's service stair down past the archive levels, to a door that opened to his key alone; then a corridor; then the second door, which opened to the Director's seal or the comptroller's key, either; then the third door, which opened only to both. The Director and he would go through all three together at seven. They would leave the second and third open behind them while they worked, because the charter required a witness from Security to stand in the doorway, and the witness would be Varga, or one of Varga's, alone. The first door, Teodor would leave unlocked behind him.

"Twenty minutes," he had said. "That's all there'll be. I'll keep her at the console for twenty minutes. After that she'll close the trust and come out."

So at five past seven on the forty-eighth day of the Closing, Dagny Ostrova came down the comptroller's service stair with Ezra Hesketh behind her, leaning on his stick, and six of the Wick crew behind him with pry-bars and wrenches and a stunner one of them had taken off a Company man in the strike, and they went through the first door, which was unlocked, as Teodor had said it would be.

The corridor beyond was long and plain and lit with a cold blue light. At the end of it the second door stood open. Beyond that, the third.

Dagny went through them both at a run.

The root was a round room, not large, with a low domed ceiling, and at its centre on a pedestal a console no bigger than a writing desk — a dark slab of glass and brass, very old, very plain, with two slots in its face side by side and a little grille beside them like the grille of a confessional. That was all. She had expected more. She had expected, she realised, something like the Wick: a machine you could see was the heart of the world. This was a desk. The Tally of Lantern, that every valve on the rock asked whose air it was, lived in a desk.

Ruth Achterberg stood at it with her hand on the seal, which was in the left-hand slot, on its chain. Teodor Basu stood beside her, with his comptroller's key in the right-hand slot. They both turned as Dagny came through the door.

Teodor's face, when he saw her, was the face of a man who has been hit and has not yet fallen down.

"No," he said. "No— get back— _go_—"

And Hale Varga said from behind her, quite quietly, "Stand still, Ostrova."

She turned. They were in the corridor behind her. They had been in the second doorway, she understood afterwards, in the alcoves either side of it, where the cold blue light did not reach — twelve of them, in armour, with slug-guns, not stunners. They had let her pass. They had let her people pass. Now they were in the doorway, and Hesketh was standing in the middle of the corridor between them and her with his stick in his hand and his old face quite calm, and the Wick crew were behind him with their pry-bars, frozen.

"Put them down," said Varga. "All of you. Put them down and nobody else gets hurt."

"Nobody else," said Hesketh. "Who got hurt already, Mr. Varga?"

One of the Wick crew — a girl called Dorothea Clay, nineteen, who had been on the master ring with Dagny in the strike — had the stunner. Dagny saw her hand move. She saw it the way she had seen Joss's arm swing on the Wick: slow, weightless, stupid, too late to stop.

"Dorrie, no—"

The corridor was very loud. The slug-guns were nothing like the stunners. They were short hard flat bangs, like doors slammed in a stairwell, a great many of them, all at once, and the blue light jumped with them. When they stopped, Dorothea Clay was on the floor of the corridor, and so were two of the crew behind her, and so was Ezra Hesketh, on his side, with his stick a metre from his hand, looking at the ceiling with an expression of mild surprise, as if somebody had told him a joke he had not heard before.

And Teodor Basu was on the floor of the root, at the foot of the console, holding his side.

Dagny did not see him fall. She saw him afterwards — the blood coming through his fingers, very bright on the Company grey, and his face gone the colour of paper, and his glasses with the cracked lens lying beside him. She understood later that he had moved when the shooting started. That he had come round the console toward her, toward the door, with his hands out, to put himself between her and the corridor; and that one of Varga's men had seen a figure move in the root and fired.

"Teodor," she said.

"I'm sorry," he said, from the floor. "I'm so sorry. I didn't— I didn't tell them. I swear. I never—"

"I know you didn't," said Ruth Achterberg.

She had not moved from the console. Her hand was still on the seal. She looked down at her comptroller on the floor with an expression Dagny could not read, and then at Dagny, in the doorway, with Varga's men behind her.

"Your brother came to me eleven days ago, Ms. Ostrova," said the Director. "In the bar in the Upper Wards. He was very frank. He told me everything: the root, the time, the doors, the bell, his run in the _Covenant_'s wake. He said he'd been asked to fly it and he wasn't a fool, and he'd rather have a seat." She turned the seal in its slot, and the console made a soft chime. "I gave him two. One for him. One for your son."

Dagny did not say anything. She found she could not.

"I'm sorry," said Ruth Achterberg, and Dagny believed, for the first time, that she meant it. "Truly. I would have liked this to end differently, for both of us. Mr. Varga, take her up. Have the comptroller seen to. I'll finish the transfer myself." She looked down at Teodor. "His key's in the slot. I'll only need him to say the words."

## Two Seats Out
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Kas Ostrova @@Pim Ostrova @@Hale Varga
When: same day
]

They put her in a cell on the security floor, a steel box with a cot and a drain and a slot in the door. Nobody hit her. Nobody spoke to her. At ten o'clock the slot opened and a tray came through with bread and bean paste and a cup of water, and she left it on the floor where it fell.

At eleven the door opened and Kas came in.

He was in a good coat. A Company coat, dark blue, with the H-A on the breast, too big for him across the shoulders, as if it had been somebody else's. He had his helmet under his arm. He stood just inside the door and did not come any further, and behind him in the corridor she could see Varga's broad back, and beside Varga, small, in his school jacket, with the tin pilot's wings on the collar, Pim.

"Dag," said Kas.

She sat on the cot and looked at him.

"I didn't know they'd shoot," he said. "I swear to God, Dag. I told her the time and the doors. I said, _Just stop them, just get there first, nobody has to get hurt._ She said nobody would. She said Varga would take you at the door and that'd be it. I didn't— I didn't know about Hesketh. And the girl. And Basu." His face moved. "I didn't know."

"What did you think they'd do?"

"I don't know! I didn't think!" It came out of him in a rush. "I thought about _him_." He jerked his head back at the corridor, at Pim. "I thought about him with his chit dimmed at twelve, sleeping on a bench in a locker room, and you telling me to fly a tug through a throat with a shear going off behind me, and the pair of you staying here to owe air to a box for six hundred years, and I thought— I thought, I can get one of us out. One. Just one. I can get the boy out." He was breathing hard. "I got him a seat, Dag. A seat out. On the _Covenant_. To the Concord. He'll have a life. He'll go to a real school. He'll never see a lock in recovery again in his life. You can hate me. Go on. But he'll be out."

"You sold nine thousand for one," said Dagny.

"I sold nine thousand who were already sold!" He was shouting now. "Everyone on this rock was sold before they were born! Varga told you that! What was your plan going to do? Take the line out of a box for a few hundred years? Send a bell to the Concord so some court could fine the Company and sell us all to somebody else? We were always going to lose, Dag. Always. I just— I made sure we didn't lose him too."

The cell was very quiet after that. Varga, in the corridor, had not turned round.

Dagny looked past her brother, at her son.

Pim was standing very straight beside Varga, the way he had stood at school the day his chit dimmed, with everybody looking. He was looking at her. He had not cried. She thought he had probably decided not to.

"Mum," he said. "I'm not going."

"Pim—"

"I'm not. I told him. I'm staying with you. I don't care about a seat."

And she understood, then, that this was the last thing, and the worst. That Kas was a liar and a coward and had sold Hesketh and Dorrie Clay and Teodor Basu for a seat on a ship — and that he was also, about this one thing, right. That her son was twelve and dimmed. That in six hundred years he would still be dimmed, and his children after him, and that there was nothing she had left, nothing at all, that could change it.

"You're going," said Dagny.

Pim stared at her.

"You're going with your uncle. On the _Covenant_. Through the Throat." She made herself say it level, the way she had said numbers out loud on the Wick all her life. "You're going to go to a real school, and you're going to grow up somewhere with a sky, and you're never going to see a lock in recovery again. And you're going to remember. Everything. The bell. The names. The Wick. All of it. You're going to remember it for me."

"No—"

"Yes," said Dagny. "Kas. Take him."

Her brother looked at her for a long moment, with his handsome ruined face. Then he turned and put his hand on Pim's shoulder. Pim did not move. Then he did — not toward Kas; toward her, one step, two, into the cell — and she got up off the cot and caught him and held him, hard, the whole thin length of him, with her face in his hair, for as long as she could stand it, which was not very long.

Then she let him go, and Kas took him out, and Varga closed the door.

## Noon
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Dagny Ostrova @@Hale Varga
When: same day
]

At noon she felt it through the floor of the cell: a long, deep, rising shudder that came up through the rock and the steel and her bones, as the _Covenant_'s great drive lit at the fore dock and she pulled away from Lantern for the last time.

It went on for a long while. Then it faded. Then there was nothing but the ordinary hum of the rock.

She sat on the cot with her hands in her lap and looked at the door.

Some time later — she did not know how long; she had stopped counting — the slot in the door opened, and nobody pushed a tray through it. Somebody was looking in.

"Ostrova," said Hale Varga's voice. "You awake?"

She did not answer.

"I didn't get on," said Varga. "The ship. I was on the list. I didn't get on."

The slot closed. And then, after a moment, very slowly, as if whoever was on the other side were still making up his mind, the lock of the cell door began to turn.
