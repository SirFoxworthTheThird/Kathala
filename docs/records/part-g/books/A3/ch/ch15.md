## Consolidation
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Idris Kell
When: +3 days
]

Kell's bailiffs drilled in Hold 1, the forward cargo bay, which they had cleared of everything but crates stacked to make corridors.

Corvin went down to watch on the third morning, because Haste had told him to — _you'll need to know how it's done, Mr. Saal, when it comes to it; the speech is only the beginning_ — and because, after the night in Hold 2, he had found that he could not sit in his cabin with the case on his desk any longer without wanting to put his fist through the porthole. So he stood on the gantry at the top of the bay and watched them.

They were good. He had seen bailiffs on the colony circuit, plenty of them, hired muscle in borrowed uniforms who drank on duty and broke things for fun. Kell's people were nothing like that. They moved through the crate-corridors in pairs, quietly, with their stun-batons held low, and they practised the same thing over and over. A door. A knock. A pair going in. Two of their own playing residents — a man and a woman, in civilian coats, sitting on crates — and the pair standing over them, not threatening, not raising their voices. One holding out a scanner. The other with a slate. _Name. Number, if you have one. Hold out your wrist, please._ And the residents, who had been told to resist, saying no, or saying nothing, or getting up to go — and the bailiffs, without hurry, without anger, putting them down. A baton across the back of the knees, and a hand on the neck, and the scanner on the wrist, and the slate chiming. Then up again, dusted off, and on to the next door.

Over and over. Hours.

Kell came up onto the gantry beside him at the end of the second hour, wiping his face with a towel.

"Enumeration," he said. "That's the first day. Every person in the habitat, scanned, named, numbered, entered on our book. Any old book they've got, we read across. Any account they're carrying, we merge into ours. Takes a week, for a big one."

"And if they won't be numbered?"

"Then we number them anyway." Kell said it the way a man explains how to tie a knot. "Most people don't fight. People want to know where they stand. They'd rather owe somebody than nobody. You'd be surprised."

"And the ones who fight?"

Kell looked down into the bay, where the bailiff playing the resident woman was sitting on a crate rubbing the back of her knee and laughing at something her partner had said.

"Then they come off the books," he said.

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning they haven't got an account. No account, no ration. No ration, no air, in a place like that. No legal person, under the instrument. You can't owe if you're not on the books, and you can't be owed. You just stop." He folded the towel. "It's quieter than it sounds. Mostly they come back in after a day or two. Hungry."

Corvin said nothing for a while. In the bay below, the pairs had started again from the first door.

"Are you bonded, Sergeant?" he said.

Kell turned his head and looked at him with his bored, patient eyes, and for the first time there was something in them that was not boredom. It might have been amusement.

"Everybody's bonded, Mr. Saal," he said. "I've got eleven years left. My daughter's got twenty-six, from her mother's side, unless I clear mine first, and then it comes off hers." He put the towel round his neck. "This job clears four. If it goes well."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then it doesn't clear four," said Kell, and went back down the ladder to his people.

## The Recording
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Fen Adaire
Items: ^The Mercy Bell
When: same day
]

Fen played him the bell that night.

Not the bell itself; it was strapped upright in the blister where it had been all voyage, silent, and she had never once, he realised, struck it. A recording. She had it on a tiny old player, the size of a coin, that she wore on a chain round her neck under her shirt, next to the loupe; she unclipped it and held it out to him on her palm in the dim light of the blister.

"My grandmother made it," she said. "When I was six. She hung it from a beam in our kitchen at Tannet and rang it once, with a padded hammer, so I'd know what it sounded like. She said I should know. She said everybody in the family should hear it once. It's the only time anyone's rung it, as far as I know, since it came out of the Throat." She put the player against his ear. "Listen."

He listened.

There was a hiss, an old kitchen's hiss, a child's voice somewhere saying something he could not make out. Then a single stroke. A low note, not loud, a little sour — not the pure clean note he had expected from a bell, but something rougher and sadder, with a waver in it, as if the bronze were tired. It went on for a long time. It hummed and hummed and slowly faded, and under it, faint, the child's voice again, and an old woman's voice saying, quite clearly, _There. That's her. That's what they heard, every time somebody died._

Then nothing.

He handed it back. He found he did not trust his voice for a moment.

"She'd have liked you," said Fen. "My grandmother. She liked people who looked like they'd been hit."

"Is that how I look?"

"Since the hold. Yes." She clipped the player back on its chain and tucked it away. "Tell me something. One of your four hundred and twelve. Tell me one."

So he told her one. He had not meant to. He told her about Malory's Reach, nine years ago, a frost-farm settlement on a moon so cold the domes had to be heated every hour of the year, and a family called Abernathy whose heating debt had been bought by a lien-house he worked for, and the morning he had stood in their kitchen and explained, in the voice, that the dome's heating contract was being reassigned to the lien-house's account and that their quarter would be serviced on the new schedule. Which meant every second day. Which meant, in Malory's Reach in the long winter, nothing at all. He told her how the mother had listened without saying anything, and nodded, and asked him if he would like tea. How he had said no. How he had gone back nine weeks later to tag the empty quarter for resale, and found it was not empty.

Fen listened with her knees drawn up and her arms round them and did not say anything at all until he had finished, and then for a long time after.

"Why are you telling me?" she said at last.

"Because you asked."

"That isn't why."

He looked at the bell. "Because there's a hold full of people under us who are going to be Abernathys," he said. "And the rock behind that planet's full of them already, or was. And I came all this way to explain it to them. And I don't know what I'm going to do."

She reached out, then, and put her hand flat against the side of his face — briefly, firmly, the way she put her hand on the bell — and held it there for a moment, and took it away.

"Then don't know," she said. "For now. It's better than knowing the wrong thing."

## Pod 41-0973
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Tol Brask @@Quennell Haste @@Mabry Ode
When: +1 day
]

The alarm went at four in the morning: a long rising whoop from somewhere aft, that went through the whole ship and had everybody out of their bunks before it had finished its first cycle.

Corvin was in the engine-room corridor in two minutes. Tol was already there, half inside the panel of the Hold 2 power board with a torch in his teeth, and Ode was behind him in a coat over her nightshirt, and through the open main doors of Hold 2, which somebody had unsealed, there was light and cold and the sound of people moving fast.

"Cascade fault," said Tol, round the torch. "Power bus to Rack Forty-One. Three pods dropped out of the cycle. Two came back on the backup. One didn't."

"Which one?"

Tol took the torch out of his mouth and looked at him.

"Forty-one oh-nine-seven-three," he said.

Corvin went into the hold.

The ship's medic was there, a heavy woman called Petrosyan, and two of Kell's bailiffs with lamps, and the pod at the end of Rack Forty-One stood open, its lid folded back, steam pouring off it in long white ropes into the cold air of the hold. The light at its foot was red. In the pod, half sitting up, with the medic's arm behind her shoulders and a foil blanket round her, was Marisol Tey.

She was awake. That was the worst of it. Corvin knew enough about cold sleep to know what an uncontrolled revival did — the blood thawing too fast, the cells bursting — and the medic's face told him the rest. But Marisol Tey was awake, for the moment, with her shaved head and her blue lips and her eyes open, looking up at the lights of the hold with an expression of enormous, patient confusion, the way a woman might look on waking in a stranger's house.

"Is this Sabra?" she said. Her voice was a thread. "Are we there?"

Corvin knelt beside the pod. He did not know why he did. The medic glanced at him and shook her head, once, very slightly.

"Nearly," he said. "We're nearly there."

"They said there'd be work." Her eyes found him. "They said— the contract— is my girl's contract— did they say? It attaches on majority. She's six. They said if I worked it down there— if I worked hard, the attachment would—" She stopped. She seemed to lose the thread of it. Her hand came out of the foil blanket and found his sleeve and held it. "Will you ask them? For me? Whether it attaches?"

"I'll ask them," said Corvin.

"Thank you." She closed her eyes. "It's very cold."

She died about four minutes later, with her hand still on his sleeve. The medic said the time aloud, and one of the bailiffs wrote it on a slate. Nobody else said anything.

When Corvin stood up, Quennell Haste was in the aisle behind him, in a dressing-gown of dark silk, with his hands in its pockets.

"Unfortunate," said Haste. "Sergeant, log one unit lost to equipment failure, please, and have the pod cleared. Mr. Saal, I'd like a word. And Captain—" he turned to Ode, who had come in behind him— "I'd like your engineer confined to quarters until we've established why a power bus on a certified cold-sleep rack failed at four in the morning in a hold that only he knows how to enter."

"Tol didn't touch your bloody rack," said Ode.

"Then he'll have nothing to fear from an inquiry." Haste turned back to Corvin. "Walk with me."

## Seizure
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Quennell Haste @@Fen Adaire
Items: ^The Mercy Bell:@Quennell Haste
When: same day
]

He walked Corvin up through the ship, not to his cabin, but to the observation blister.

Fen was there. She had not come down for the alarm. She was sitting on the deck in her nightclothes beside the bell, and when she saw Haste in the hatchway she got up, slowly, and stood between him and the bronze without seeming to know she had done it.

Behind Haste, on the ladder, there were two of Kell's bailiffs with a cargo sled.

"Miss Adaire," said Haste. "I'm sorry to disturb you. I'm afraid I've been remiss. I should have attended to this at Ambry." He took a slate from the pocket of his dressing-gown. "The bell of the colony carrier _Ardent Mercy_ was, at the date of the Closing, the property of the Halden-Ash Company's Sabra Concern. It was removed from the Concern's habitat at or about the Closing by persons unknown. It has since passed through several hands, in good faith, I'm sure. But property of a concern that is removed from it attaches, on recovery, to the instrument under which that concern's assets are held." He turned the slate round so that she could see it. "That instrument is now Brightwater's. The bell is an asset of the Sabra Concern. I'm taking it into secure storage in Hold 1 for the remainder of the voyage."

Fen looked at the slate. Then at him.

"It's mine," she said. "My family's. Six generations. We paid for it."

"Eleven crowns, I believe, at Tannet. Brightwater will of course reimburse you." He nodded to the bailiffs. "Please."

They came past him into the blister with the sled. Fen did not move. One of them put a hand on her arm — not roughly; the drilled, patient hand from Hold 1 — and she shook it off, and the other one stepped in, and Corvin saw the stun-baton come up, held low.

"Don't," said Corvin.

Everyone looked at him. Haste raised his eyebrows.

"Mr. Saal?"

Corvin stood in the hatchway of the blister with the cold of Hold 2 still in his clothes and Marisol Tey's hand still, he could have sworn, on his sleeve. He looked at Fen, at the baton, at the bell. He looked at Haste, who was waiting with polite interest for whatever he was going to say. He knew what he wanted to say. He had known it since the hold. He could feel the shape of it in his mouth.

"Don't hurt her," he said instead.

"Of course not," said Haste. "Miss Adaire, please stand aside."

Fen looked at Corvin for a long moment. Then she stood aside.

They unstrapped the bell from the couch legs and tipped it onto the sled, and it rang as it tipped — a single soft unintended note, low and sour, the note from the recording, humming in the small glass dome — and then it was on the sled and strapped down and going out through the hatch, and Haste was holding out the slate to Corvin.

"The seizure docket," he said. "It wants the adjuster's concurrence. It's only a formality."

Corvin looked at the slate. _Property recovered under instrument. Adjuster concurs._ A line for his mark.

He did not take the stylus. He did not refuse it either. He stood there.

After a moment Haste smiled, and marked the line himself — _Adjuster concurs (verbal)_ — and put the slate back in his pocket.

"Good night, Mr. Saal. Miss Adaire."

When he had gone, the blister was very quiet. The ring of slates still lay on the deck where Fen had left them, round the empty place where the bell had stood, like a circle round a grave.

"You said nothing," said Fen.

"I said don't hurt you."

"You said nothing." She was not shouting. She was looking at him with an expression he had seen on four hundred and twelve settlements, and had never once, until now, had turned on him by someone whose good opinion he wanted. "You stood there and you let him write it down. _Adjuster concurs._ That's you, Corvin. That's what you are. Verbal."

She went past him down the ladder and did not look back.
