## The Keel
[
Place: #The Cold Wards
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Orrin Sallow @@Jory Hask
When: +2 days
]

They went up the keel on the twenty-seventh day, four thousand of them, in the dark.

The keel-ways ran in the outer skin of the rock, a hundred metres under the floor of the Galleries, where the Company's engineers had cut them three and a half centuries ago for the pipes and the power and the long cold cables that tied the fore end of Lantern to the aft. They were tunnels two metres high and two wide, rough-walled, ribbed with old steel, and most of them were dead: iced shut, or fallen in, or blocked with three hundred years of frozen seepage. Cutters used the aft ends. Nobody had walked one end to end in living memory. Tamsin had walked the first three kilometres of Keel Four a dozen times, chasing copper, and knew where it forked and where the ice began. Beyond that she knew nothing at all.

So they found out.

It took all night. Four thousand people in a line two metres wide and eleven kilometres long, with lamps where they had lamps and hands on the shoulders in front where they had not, going fore along the skin of the world. The cutters went first, Tamsin and the dozen others among the Called who had worked the Cold, with lances and picks, cutting the ice where it had grown across the way and clearing the falls where they could and finding the forks round them where they could not. Behind them came the young and the strong, carrying the old and the sick. Behind them, everyone else. Jory ran the line. He went back and forth along it all night, sideways and quick, carrying word: _ice at the next rib, wait — fork, keep left — a fall, there's a way round, keep close._ Orrin walked near the front with a slate full of old Company plans, the keel-way maps he had copied from the archive on the last night before Lusk caught him, reading them by lamplight and calling out the frame numbers as they passed them stencilled on the ribs.

Nobody talked much. In the keel the sound of their feet came back off the walls and went on ahead of them into the dark, and the cold was so deep it seemed to have weight, and every so often, through the rock over their heads, faint, they felt the Wick stammer — a tremor in the stone, a change in the hum of the world — and everybody in the line stopped, and held their breath, and waited, until it steadied.

At four in the morning they came to the end of Keel Four, where it opened into a wide low gallery full of old pipe-runs and machinery, at the fore end of the rock. It was warm. That was the first thing. After eleven kilometres of the keel the air coming out of the gallery mouth was warm on their faces, and smelled of oil, and hot metal, and something else, faint and dry, like a stove.

Wenna Blackwood was waiting in it with two hundred of the Salt.

She was sitting on an old pump housing with a lance across her knees and her one eye on the keel mouth, and when Tamsin came out of it, white with frost, she stood up.

"You're late," she said.

"It's a long way."

"Your brother said four. It's ten past." Wenna looked past her at the line coming out of the keel behind her — the cutters, the young, the old being carried, the families, filing out into the warm gallery one by one, blinking in the Salt's lamps, more and more and more of them, until the gallery was full and they were still coming. Her face did something Tamsin had not seen it do before. "Four thousand," she said.

"Nearer five. The fourth list came into the Cold yesterday. They came too."

Wenna nodded slowly. She did not say anything for a while. Then she turned and pointed, with the lance, at the far wall of the gallery.

It was not a wall. It was a hull.

A great curved sweep of riveted blue-grey plating, going up out of the floor of the gallery into the rock overhead, and away on either side of them out of the reach of the lamps: the belly of a ship, buried nose-first in the fore end of the world, with the gallery cut round it like a socket round a tooth. There were pipes running into it, old ones, crusted with three hundred years of scale. There was a ladder running up it to a hatch. Beside the hatch, in square white stencil, faded nearly to nothing: FRAME 40 — REACTOR — LOWER ACCESS.

"There's your Hulk," said Wenna Blackwood. "Underneath."

## Covenant
[
Place: #The Hulk
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Orrin Sallow
Items: ^Fuel Cassettes
When: same day
]

The hatch was not welded. It was locked, with a Company lock, and Orrin opened it in four minutes, because he had opened its twin in the floor of the fifth hall three nights before and had not stopped thinking about it since.

Inside, the Hulk was a ship.

Tamsin did not know why that surprised her. Everybody on Lantern knew the Hulk was a ship; it was the first thing they taught you. But she had always thought of it as the guides on the Account Day tours talked about it, as bones: a dead thing, a relic, a floor with a brass rail round it. It was not a floor. It was corridors and ladders and bulkheads and doors, rooms with bunks in them and lockers with names stencilled on, a galley with its pans still hanging, everything thick with dust and cold and silent and perfectly, absolutely whole, as if the people who had lived in it had stepped out that morning. The Count had used some of it. There were crates in the first corridors, Count grey, stencilled with the Count's mark. Beyond them there was nothing but the ship.

The reactor room was on the lowest deck, behind a pressure door that stood open. It was warm in there. Warmer than anywhere Tamsin had been in her life, warmer than Wenna's room behind the cargo net; the air shimmered with it. The room was round and high and lined with machinery she did not understand, and in its middle, in a ring of steel cradles round a great dark column that went up through the deck overhead, there were fourteen cassettes.

They were the size of coffins, as Ninian had said. They were dull grey, and cabled each to the column, and each had a small light on its end, steady, amber, like an eye half open. They hummed. Very faintly. You felt it in your teeth more than heard it.

Tamsin stood in the doorway and counted them, because she could not help it. Then she counted them again.

"Fourteen," said Orrin, beside her. His voice was very quiet.

"Fourteen."

Behind them, Wenna's people were already coming down the ladder with the sleds.

## The Bridge
[
Place: #The Hulk
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Orrin Sallow
When: same day
]

The freight lift was on the old plans at the ship's fore end, where her nose went into the rock, and the controls for it — Orrin said — would be on the bridge. So while the Salt began, with winches and chains and a great deal of swearing, to uncable the first of the cassettes from the column, Tamsin and Orrin went up through the ship to find it.

It took a long time. The Hulk was bigger than she had imagined, and its decks were not level — the ship lay nose-down in the rock, tilted, so that every corridor ran a little downhill toward the fore, and every ladder leaned. They went up through deck after deck of cabins and stores, all dust and silence. Once they passed a room full of chairs in rows facing a blank screen, as if for a meeting that had never been held. Once they passed a nursery, with small beds, and Tamsin did not look into it.

The bridge was at the top. A long curved room, at the ship's nose, whose forward wall had been a window once, a great sweep of armoured glass looking out ahead of her; and now the glass looked out on rock. Raw grey nickel-iron, pressed up against it from outside, filling every pane. The ship had gone into the world nose-first and the world had closed round her face. There were chairs at the consoles, and a big chair in the middle, on a step, and everything was white with dust.

Orrin went to the consoles. Tamsin went to the big chair, because it was in the middle, and stood looking at it.

There was a plate on the console in front of it. A brass plate, screwed to the panel, the kind every ship carried, Orrin told her later, to say what she was and who had made her. It was furred with dust. Tamsin wiped it with her sleeve.

She read slowly. She always had. She moved her lips.

HALDEN-ASH CARRIER COVENANT.
HA 7731. TANNET REACH, 2361.

"Orrin," she said.

He came and stood beside her and read it. He did not say anything for a while.

"_Covenant_," he said at last, as if trying the taste of it. "Not the _Ardent Mercy_."

"No."

"Two thousand three hundred and sixty-one. Seventy years after the colony." He put out his hand and touched the plate. "It's not the ship that brought us. It's a Company ship. A carrier. From the end." He turned and looked down the long dusty bridge at the rock pressed up against the glass. "It's not bones. It came _back_."

"Came back from where?"

"From going." He was very pale. "From the Closing. The Company ran, Tamsin. Everybody knows that. In their ships, through the Throat, and the door shut behind them. Except—" he touched the plate again— "they didn't. Not this one. This one came back here. And they put it in the rock, and they built the Count on top of it." He turned round slowly. "And told us it was the _Mercy_."

There was a log on the console beside the master's chair. Not a slate; a book. A thick bound book of old paper, with the H-A letters stamped on its cover, lying open as though someone had put it down in the middle of reading it. Orrin picked it up. The dust came off it in a cloud. He turned to the front and read, and then he turned pages, faster and faster, his lips moving, and then he sat down on the step of the master's chair with the book in his lap, quite suddenly, as if his legs had gone.

"What?" said Tamsin.

He held it out to her, open. She could not read the old hand; it was small and slanted and full of flourishes. He took it back and read it to her. His voice was not steady.

"_Ship's log, carrier Covenant, at Lantern. Kept by R. Achterberg, formerly Director, Sabra Concern. Year three after the Closing._

"_We came home in her because we had nowhere else to go. Four hundred and six of us. The people of Lantern did not kill us, which was more than we deserved. I keep the books now. Somebody has to, or the air stops. I keep them at the root, under her, with the seal they gave me back._

"_I write this so that whoever keeps them after me will know what they are keeping._

"_The Account is owed to the dead of Caul Deep. Nine hundred and four people who died in the ice on Caul in the spring of 2387 because I would not pay to brace the shaft, and whom I sealed in the ice so that the Concord would not know. Every hour of air on Lantern, from the forty-ninth day of the Closing, is owed to them. That was done at the root by Dagny Ostrova, a Wick-tender, with a key that Teodor Basu made, and it cannot be undone. Nobody holds the Account. Nobody can draw on it. Nobody can sell it. It is owed to them, and they are dead._

"_It is discharged when their names are spoken at the root, every one, in order._

"_Their names are on the bell of the Ardent Mercy, which hung at the Dock Ring. Ostrova's brother took it through the Throat as it closed. We do not have them. Nobody does._

"_So it is owed. Keep the books honestly. Every hour, to them. Not to yourselves._

"_R. A._"

Orrin stopped. He turned a page.

"There's more," he said. "In a different hand. Later. Much later." He read it in a flat voice. "_Year 61. The Director's instruction regarding honest keeping has been reviewed by the Count and found impracticable. A debt that is owed to no living person compels no living person. The doctrine of Caul is adopted in its place: the Account is owed to the dark that kept us. The Founders' log is to be held by the Comptroller-General alone. The reserve in the ship's reactor is to be held for the continuance of the Count. Neither is to be spoken of._" He closed the book. "It's signed by the Comptroller-General of the day. I know the name. He's on the plaque in the fifth hall. He's the one who had the brass rail put up."

Tamsin stood on the dusty bridge of the _Covenant_ with her hand on the master's chair and her face pressed, almost, against the rock of the world.

"Caul's people," she said.

"Nine hundred and four of them."

"We've owed them every breath. For three hundred years. Real people. Dead." She found she was shaking. "And the Count knew. And made it into a ghost story. _Caul is the dark that kept us._ So nobody would ask."

"So nobody would ask who," said Orrin. "Yes."

"And the bell." She thought of the Hook, black and empty over the Dock Ring, and her grandmother saying _We ring the Hook so we remember there was a bell, and there were names, and somebody took them. That's not sad. That's angry._ "The names were on the bell. That's why we ring the Hook. That's what it's for. We've been ringing for them and we didn't even know."

Orrin opened the log again, and looked at the old slanted hand, and closed it.

"_Their names are on the bell_," he said. "_We do not have them. Nobody does._"

Neither of them said anything for a long time. Under their feet, through the tilted deck of the buried ship, they felt a long shudder, and a groan of steel, as the first of the fourteen came out of its cradle.

## The Count Key
[
Place: #The Hulk
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Orrin Sallow @@Brother Lusk:dead @@Wenna Blackwood @@Jory Hask
When: same day
]

The Count came for them through the Sanctum.

Tamsin heard it before she saw it: shouting, far below, and the flat hard crack of slug-guns, and the roar of Salt voices, all of it coming up through the tilted decks of the ship. She and Orrin went down the ladders at a run, the log under Orrin's arm, and came out on the reactor deck into a fight.

The Sanctum opened into the Hulk. Of course it did. Orrin had said it: _the Tally's root is under the ship._ There was a door on the reactor deck — a heavy steel door, Count-grey, that Tamsin had taken for a store — and it stood open now, and through it the bailiffs of the Count were coming, in grey, with batons and guns, and the Salt were meeting them in the corridor with lances and pry-bars and chains, and the corridor was full of bodies and smoke and the white roar of a lance cutting the air.

And in the middle of the reactor room, in the shimmering heat, with eleven cassettes still in their cradles round the dark column and three on sleds by the hatch, Brother Lusk was walking toward Jory.

Her brother had been at the hatch, at the head of the sled-line, running the chain for the winch. He had not seen Lusk come through the Sanctum door. He saw him now. He stood very still beside the sled with the chain in his hands and his chin up, fifteen and all elbows, and Lusk came toward him across the warm floor without hurrying, with his baton held loosely at his side, the way a man walks toward a door he means to close.

Tamsin did not decide anything. She was across the reactor room before she knew she had moved. She had the lance in her hands — her own lance, the cutter's lance she had brought eleven kilometres up the keel — and she had lit it, and the white flame came out of its throat a metre long and roaring, and Lusk heard it, and turned.

He looked at her. For a moment she saw something in his face that she had seen at the Lip, when the Galleries rang: interest. As if she were a sum that had come out in an unexpected way.

Then she put the lance through him.

It was very quick. She had cut steel with it all her life and it went through a man the way it went through copper, without stopping. Brother Lusk looked down at himself. He opened his mouth. Then he folded up, slowly, on the warm floor of the reactor room, among the cradles, and lay still.

Tamsin turned the lance off. Her hands were perfectly steady. She stood over him and found that she felt nothing at all, and thought that she would, later, and did not care.

"Tam," said Jory, behind her. His voice had gone small.

"Get the sled moving," she said. "Go on. Get it up."

In the corridor the fighting had stopped. Not because anyone had won. Because the bailiffs had seen what was behind the Salt — out through the lower hatch, on the ladder, in the gallery beyond, more and more of them, pressing in to see — five thousand of the Called, who had walked eleven kilometres in the dark and had nothing in the world left to lose. The bailiffs were not fools. They began to put their batons down on the deck. Some of them sat down beside them.

Through the Sanctum door, beyond them, Tamsin saw a dark coat going away fast down a corridor, toward a light.

"Vosk," said Orrin. "The root."

## The Root of the Tally
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Orrin Sallow @@Imre Vosk
Items: ^The Count Key:@Tamsin Hask
When: same day
]

The root of the Tally was a round room, not large, with a low domed ceiling, and at its centre on a pedestal a desk of dark glass and brass with three slots in its face and a little grille beside them. That was all. Tamsin had expected more. She had expected, she realised, something like the Wick.

Imre Vosk was at the desk. He had something in the left-hand slot, on a chain round his neck — he was bent over the console with the chain pulled taut, his face very close to the grille — and he was speaking into it, quickly and quietly, the soft voice that went everywhere.

"—release from the Tally's charge all accounts at present located aft of frame—"

"Stop," said Orrin.

Vosk did not stop. "—aft of frame nine hundred, the Cold Wards entire, and close the—"

Orrin crossed the room and took him by the collar of his dark coat and pulled him away from the console. He was not strong. He did it anyway. Vosk staggered back and the chain round his neck went tight and snapped, and something brass fell out of the slot and rang on the floor and rolled, in a small wobbling circle, and lay still at Tamsin's feet.

She bent and picked it up.

It was a disk. Brass, the size of her palm, worn smooth with handling, warm from the slot. There were letters on it, nearly gone. She turned it to the light. Two letters, leaning on each other like tired men. H and A.

"The Count Key," said Vosk. He was on the floor against the wall where Orrin had thrown him, with his coat torn at the collar and his hair in his eyes. He laughed, a little breathlessly. "Well. There it is. Three hundred years. You'd think it would look like more."

"You were going to close the Cold," said Orrin. "With five thousand people in it."

"I was going to close the Cold with five thousand people walking up a keel-way toward the Count's reactor." Vosk wiped his mouth. "Yes. I'd have closed the keel too, if I'd had time. The sums haven't changed, Orrin. They never change. You've got fourteen cassettes and thirty thousand people."

"Eleven in the column and three on the sleds," said Tamsin. "Going up the lift now."

Vosk looked at her for the first time. He looked at the brass disk in her hand, and at the cutter's rig, and the frost still white on her shoulders from the keel, and the burn of the lance on her gloves.

"Agathe Hask's girl," he said. "Yes. Wenna said. God help us." He leaned his head back against the wall of the root and closed his eyes. "Light it, then. Light the whole thing. End to end, the Cold and all. You'll have fourteen years. And then it'll go out, and you'll all be in the dark together, and you'll owe Caul every breath you take in it, the same as now. You can't close the Account, girl. Nobody can. You've read the log, I suppose. You know why."

"I know why," said Tamsin.

"Then you know it's owed for ever."

"No," said Tamsin. "It's owed till the bell comes home."

Vosk opened his eyes and looked at her.

"There isn't a bell," he said. "There's never going to be a bell. The Company took it."

"A Wick-tender's brother took it. Through the Throat. To tell the Concord what was done at Caul." She closed her hand on the disk. "Your Founders' log says so. You've read it. You had it locked in a book on a bridge nobody goes to, and put a brass rail round a floor, and told children it was the ship that brought us." She stepped closer to him. "You're right. We can't close it. We haven't got their names. But we know who we owe now. And it's not the dark, and it's not the Count, and it's not you."

## The Lamp
[
Place: #The Wick
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Orrin Sallow @@Ninian @@Wenna Blackwood @@Jory Hask
Items: ^Fuel Cassettes:#The Wick
When: same day
]

The Founders' lift came up into the light of the spine-road at nine in the morning, through a door that had been welded shut for three hundred years and that the Salt cut open from the inside with three lances in forty minutes; and on its platform, on a sled, humming, with its little amber light like a half-open eye, was the first of the fourteen.

The spine-road was full. Word had gone up out of the Hulk faster than the lift. The Galleries had heard; the Upper Wards had heard; the counting families had come out of their houses on the clean white terraces in their good coats and stood on their steps and watched, and nobody had stopped them, and nobody had stopped the Owing either, coming up the terraces from below in their thousands, from Twenty-Two and Nineteen and the Salt Docks, until the road along the foot of the Wick was a crowd from one end to the other, and the bailiffs who were left had taken their grey coats off and stood among it.

They hauled the sled up the spine by hand.

There was no other way. The cargo winches on the spine had rusted solid in the century since anyone had hauled anything up them. So they did it the way the Wick crews must have done it in the Closing year, with ropes: a chain of people on the spoke-ladders and the crawls, clipped on, hand over hand, two hundred of them, pulling a sled the weight of four men up a column of light into the place where there was no weight at all. Tamsin was on the rope. So was Orrin, with his soft hands raw. So was Wenna Blackwood, roaring at them all like a gravel chute. Jory ran the line.

At the master ring, at the root of the Wick, beside his tin hut, Ninian the Wickwright was waiting at the Founders' port with the hatch open and his blind white eyes turned toward the sound of them coming.

"Bring her here," he said. "Gently. Gently. That's it. Let me feel her."

He put both his old paper hands flat on the cassette. He stood like that for a long time, with the light of the lamp pouring down on him from the column above, white and hard, the light that had taken his eyes; and his face, lit from above, was the face of a man touching the face of someone he had thought was dead.

"Yes," he said. "Oh, yes. That's a good one."

They fed her into the port. It took twenty minutes, with Ninian's hands guiding every centimetre of it, talking to it under his breath the way Tamsin had once heard a cutter talk to a bar of copper. Then the port closed. Then he went to the master collimator, by touch, and put his hands on the trim wheels, and turned his face up the length of the lamp toward the aft end of the world, where it stopped.

"Watch," he said. "I can't. You'll have to watch for me."

He turned the wheels.

Tamsin watched.

She had lived her whole life under a lamp that stopped two-thirds of the way down the sky. Under it the Galleries, and the light; beyond it, the dark rod of the dead axis, and under that the Cold. She had walked into that dark a thousand times with a tether on her hip. She had taken her mother into it. She watched it now, from the root of the Wick, along eleven kilometres of the world.

The light went down the axis like water poured along a pipe.

Not fast. It did not need to be fast. It went down the dark rod a hundred metres, and then another hundred, white fire flowering out of the dead bottle and racing along it, and every hundred metres it went the shadows below it shortened and the terraces under it came out of the dark — dead terraces, frost-furred, with their washing frozen on the lines and their doors standing open — and their frost caught the light and blazed, white and gold, and then began, all along the aft walls of the world, very slowly, to drip. And the light went on. Over the Pump Galleries where the Called had burned their doors to keep warm. Over the gallery where they had laid their dead in rows under the frost. Over the service way under the old pump bank, and a welded door, and a woman sitting with her back to the wall with a red shoe in her hands. It went on to the very end of the axis, to the aft wall of the world, where nobody had ever in Tamsin's life seen anything but black, and there it stopped, because there was nowhere else for it to go.

The whole of Lantern was lit.

It was very quiet on the master ring. On the spine-road below, on every terrace, in every gallery, thirty thousand people were standing and looking up. Nobody spoke. Nobody moved. On Lantern you were taught at five not to look straight at the Wick, because it would burn your eyes; and they looked anyway, all of them, at the whole length of it, end to end, for the first time in their lives.

"Tell me," said Ninian.

"It's all lit," said Tamsin. Her voice did not work properly. "All of it. All the way to the end. The Cold's melting. The frost's turning to rain. It's all lit."

The old man nodded. He took his hands off the trim wheels and put them flat on the rail of the master ring and stood there, with his face turned up into the blaze he could not see.

"Thirteen more," he said. "Fourteen years. Fourteen years, end to end." He was smiling. "I won't see the end of them. Good. Somebody else can shorten her, next time. I've done enough of that."

From somewhere far below, on a terrace near the Dock Ring, someone began to sing. Tamsin knew the tune before she could hear the words. It was the Twilight Song: the slow, low one, from before the Count, about the crews who took the lamp down to make the Company listen and the whole world standing on its terraces and seeing the light, for once, as a thing that someone had made. Another voice took it up. Then a gallery's worth. It went round the curve of the world, terrace to terrace, under the blazing lamp, until it seemed to Tamsin that the whole rock was singing, very quietly, to itself.

Wenna Blackwood, beside her on the ring, was not singing. She stood with her great scarred arms folded on the rail and her one eye on the lit length of the Wick, and after a while she said, without turning her head: "Seven to the lamp and seven to me. That was the deal."

"That was the deal," said Tamsin.

"Light them all." Wenna was still not looking at her. "All fourteen. I've no use for a lamp of my own on the Salt Docks with this up there." She jerked her head at the sky. "I'll keep my end in the reserve. In the Wick store, where it was. Held. For the day she starts to gutter. And nobody charges for it." She spat over the rail, and watched it curve away sideways into the light. "God help me. Agathe Hask's girl."

## The Hook, Lit
[
Place: #The Dock Ring

When: same day
]

Tamsin went to the Hook that evening, under a lamp that did not stop.

The square below the inner lock was full, as it had been on the day of the proclamation. Fuller. The Owing were there, and the Called, back out of the Cold with the rain of its melting frost still in their hair, and the Salt, and Wickwrights, and people from the Upper Wards in their good coats standing a little apart and not sure whether they should be there at all. Orrin was beside her, with the Founders' log under his arm. Jory was on her other side. In her pocket there was a brass disk, warm, with two letters on it nearly worn away.

Somebody handed her the bar. It was the same length of iron pipe as always, worn smooth and dark at the gripping end by three hundred years of hands.

She stood under the empty Hook and looked up at it. Black iron. Nothing on it. There never had been, in her lifetime. Only now, standing under it in the full light of the Wick for the first time, she saw something she had never noticed: on the bracket, where the bell's shackle must once have hung, a short stub of old iron, cut through, its end rough and black, as if someone had put a torch to it a very long time ago, in a hurry, at night.

"For my mother," she said aloud. "Agathe Hask."

She struck the Hook.

"For the woman at Aft Store 3, with the shoe. For whoever she was. And for the one inside who waited."

She struck it again.

"For Dobbs. For the first thousand and the second and the third." Again. Again. "For the nine hundred and four of Caul Deep." She stopped. She found that she was crying, at last, in front of all of them, under the lamp, and that she did not care. "Whose names we haven't got. Whom we owe every breath. Who we didn't even know were people." She looked out at the square. "We'll owe them. Honestly. Every hour, to them. Not to the Count. Not to the dark. To them. Till the bell comes home."

She struck the Hook a last time and gave the bar to Orrin.

He looked at it in his raw hands for a long moment. Then he looked at the square, at thirty thousand faces turned toward him under the light, and said, in the clear precise voice of a counter reading a figure he has checked three times and knows is right:

"Owing."

And the square — slowly, raggedly, the Owing and the Called and the Salt and the counting families, all of them, the way they had said it every day of their lives, and for the first time meaning something by it — answered:

"Owed."
