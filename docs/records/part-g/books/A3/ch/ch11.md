## The Floor of the Fifth Hall
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Orrin Sallow
Items: ^The Tally
When: +6 days
]

The answer had been under his feet all his life.

Orrin found it on the sixth night, on his knees, in the fifth ledger hall, which was the lowest and oldest of the five, and the only one whose floor was not rock. He had not gone there looking for it. He had gone there because he had run out of places to look, and because when a counter has run out of places to look he goes back to the beginning, and the beginning of the Count, everybody knew, was the Hulk.

The fifth hall was the Account Day hall. It was where the tours came. It was kept a little cleaner than the others and lit a little brighter, and at its centre, where the desks stopped, there was a square of floor fenced off by a low brass rail, with a plaque on the rail that every child on Lantern had read aloud on their first visit, slowly, sounding out the long words. _HERE THE FOUNDERS RAISED THE COUNT UPON THE BONES OF THE SHIP THAT BROUGHT US. OWING AND OWED._ Inside the rail the floor was the Hulk's own plating, unpainted for the tours: a great curved sheet of riveted metal, dark blue-grey, worn smooth and shiny in the middle where three hundred years of small hands had touched it.

Outside the rail the same plating ran on under the desks, under the walls, under the whole of the hall, painted over in Count grey. And under the grey, in places — where chairs had been dragged, where feet had scuffed — the old blue showed through.

He had noticed it, absently, since he was a boy. He noticed it properly on the sixth night because he had been staring at the floor for an hour, sitting at a desk in the empty hall with his head in his hands, trying to think where else in the Count a hull might be numbered; and his eye had fallen on a place by the wall where a filing chest had stood for a century and been moved, and the paint under it had peeled in a long curling strip, and under the peeled grey there was blue, and on the blue there was white.

A stencil. Very old. The edge of a letter, or a number.

He got down on his knees. He had nothing to scrape with but the stylus Jory had given back to him. He used that. The Count grey came away in flakes and dust and then, as he worked down the strip, in long satisfying curls, and under it, in a stencil a hand's-breadth high, in the square white Company type that he had seen only on the oldest ledgers in the archive, letters came up out of the floor.

HA 7731.

And beside it, smaller: REACTOR ACCESS — FRAME 40 — AUTHORISED PERSONNEL.

He knelt there for a long time with paint dust on his knees and his hands. Then he got up, and went to the brass rail, and looked at the bare shining metal inside it that he had touched at seven and felt nothing.

_The bones of the ship that brought us._

The Hulk was Hull 7731. And the Hulk had a reactor. And in the light ledger, carried forward every quarter for three hundred years on the Founders' own authority, the Hulk's reactor held fourteen units in reserve.

He went back to the third hall and opened the light ledger again and did the arithmetic, because that was what he was for. A cassette, at the Wick's present length — a third of what it had been at the Founding — lasted a little under three years. At full length, a little under one. Fourteen units would light the Wick as it burned now for forty years. Would light it from end to end, the whole length of the world, the Cold Wards and all, for fourteen.

He sat back. He thought about the Comptroller-General standing on the gallery above the Hook, saying in his soft voice that the last cassette would be spent before the end of the year. That the Count had made such provision as it could. That it was arithmetic.

It was arithmetic. Orrin had simply not been given all of the numbers.

There was one more thing to look for, and he found it in the Wickwrights' own orders, which the Count filed and nobody read: a work order, issued two years ago, signed by the Comptroller-General, for an _auxiliary lamp, Upper Wards, to be commissioned on the failure of the Wick_. Specifications attached. It was a small lamp. A lamp for three terraces and the Counting House. A lamp for perhaps four thousand people.

Its fuel source, in the specifications, in Vosk's own neat hand, was given as _Reserve_.

## The Docket
[
Place: #The Galleries
Characters: @@Orrin Sallow @@Tamsin Hask @@Jory Hask @@Agathe Hask
Items: ^Fuel Cassettes
When: +1 day
]

He went down to Twenty-Two in the morning without changing his clothes, with grey paint dust still in the creases of his trousers, and when Tamsin Hask opened the door to him he said, before she could speak: "There's fuel."

She looked at him. Then she stood aside and let him in and shut the door.

Agathe was asleep. Jory was not there. Tamsin pulled out the room's one chair for him and sat on her mat on the floor, cross-legged, in her undersuit with her hair tied back, and listened while he told her. He told it badly, in the wrong order, the ledger and then the floor and then the ledger again and then the Wickwrights' order, and she did not interrupt him once. When he had finished she sat for a while looking at her hands.

Then she reached inside her undersuit and took out a folded square of yellowed plastic and held it out to him.

He unfolded it. A Company docket, its paper brown with age. He read it, and read it again. _Cassettes 1–212 to Wick store, Spine Road. For the lamp. Store to be sealed on completion._ The stamp. The neat small signature.

"Where did you get this?"

"Aft Store 3. In the Cold. Behind a welded door." She told him about the door, and the empty racks, and — after a moment, not looking at him — about the corner behind the last rack, and the wall. He did not know what to say to that. Nobody would have. She did not seem to need him to say anything.

"Two hundred and twelve," she said. "That's where the Wick's fuel came from. Those two hundred and twelve, and whatever was in the other stores. The Company put them in the Wick store for the lamp before it ran. Didn't it?"

"I think so. Yes."

"And your Count's been burning them down for three hundred years, and shortening the lamp, and shortening it, and telling us it was all there was." She took the docket back and folded it, carefully, along its old creases. "And it's got fourteen more under its floor."

"In reserve," said Orrin. "Yes."

"For the Upper Wards."

"For an auxiliary lamp. In the Upper Wards. When the Wick goes out." He made himself say the rest of it. "For about four thousand people. The Count. The counting families. The farms they've moved up there. It's— I think it's always been the plan. Since long before Vosk. The reserve was set aside at the Founding. Somebody's always known it was there. Every Comptroller-General. They just never needed it until now."

"And the Calling?"

He had thought about that all night, on his knees, and then at his desk, and then walking down the galleries in the grey morning.

"Thirty thousand people can't live under a lamp built for four," he said. "But ten thousand fewer is a start. And then, when the Wick goes out, the Galleries go dark, and the Cold comes up from the aft end, and—" He stopped. "And it doesn't matter, then, how many there are. Because they'll be in the dark, and the Count will be in the light, and the Account will go on being kept, by the ones who are left."

Tamsin Hask said nothing for a long time. On the bunk Agathe stirred, and coughed in her sleep, and settled.

"It's not arithmetic," Tamsin said at last. "It's a lifeboat."

"Yes."

"With fourteen seats in it, and a door, and a man with a stick at the door."

"Yes."

She got up off the floor. She went to the shelf over the stove and took down the biscuit tin with the farmhouse on the lid and opened it, and stood looking into it — at the chits, all the family's chits, three hundred years of them — and then she closed it again and put it back.

"I'm going to take them," she said.

He stared at her.

"The fourteen. Out of the Hulk, up the spine, into the Wick. All of them. And light it. All the way to the aft end." She turned round, and her face was quite calm. "You're going to tell me how to get into the Hulk."

"I can't—" he began, and stopped. He had been going to say _I can't, it's the Count's_, and found that the words did not mean anything any longer. He said instead: "I don't know how. I found a stencil on a floor. That's all. _Reactor access, frame forty._ I don't know where frame forty is. I don't know if there's a way in from the halls. The Sanctum's down there. The Tally's root. Nobody goes into the Hulk but the Comptroller-General."

"Then find out."

"And if I do? You'll walk in with a cutter's lance and fourteen cassettes the size of coffins will just— come out with you? Up through the Count, up the spine, past the bailiffs, past Lusk—"

"No," said Tamsin. "Not on my own." And she looked past him, at the door, and he turned and saw that it had opened while they were talking, without the sigh and clunk it usually made, and Jory was standing in it, with his finger on his lips, grinning; and behind him, filling the doorway from side to side, there was a slab of a man in a cutter's rig whom Orrin had never seen before, and who looked at him with no expression at all.

"This is Dobbs," said Tamsin. "He works for the Salt. Dobbs, this is my counter."

## The First Thousand
[
Place: #The Cold Wards
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Orrin Sallow @@Brother Lusk
When: +2 days
]

The Count posted the lists thirteen days early.

It came without warning on the morning of the seventeenth day after the Hook. The gutter had come four times in one night — four times the Wick had stammered and nearly died, and four times the whole of Lantern had woken in the dark and lain listening to the silence — and when the light steadied the last time and the third shift began, the boards at the Hook had been papered over in the night with long grey sheets, and the criers were out on every gallery with their slates, and the first thousand names were being read.

The Count called it mercy. The lamp was failing faster than the Wickwrights had said; every day's delay cost air. Better the first of the Called go now, in good order, while there was still light enough to see them into the Cold, than in a panic later in the dark. The rest would follow in their thousands, every few days. The criers read it out in the Comptroller-General's words, over and over, all morning, and then they began on the names.

Agathe Hask was not in the first thousand. She was in the third.

Tamsin heard it from a crier on Twenty-Two and stood very still on the terrace road while he read the rest of the third list, family by family, and the people round her stood still too, and then one of them began to weep, and then a door down the terrace opened and a man ran out of it shouting, and the crier stepped back against the wall with his slate held up in front of him like a shield.

The first thousand went at second shift.

The bailiffs brought them down from all over the Galleries in columns, to the ring road at the aft end of the Dock Ring, where it ran past the Salt Docks and on into the dark. There they were counted. There was a table set up in the middle of the road with a lamp on it and a counter behind it, and each family as it came was stopped, and its names read, and its chits touched to a counter's slate, and the slate chimed, and the chits went grey. Released from the Tally's charge. Owing nothing more. And then they were waved on, along the ring road, to the Lip.

Tamsin watched from the steps of a dead noodle stall at the corner of Spur Two. Orrin was beside her. He had come down without his coat. He had not said a word since the columns began.

They came past for an hour. Old people, mostly — the deepest accounts were the longest lives — and the sick, and whole families together where the head of the family was Called and the children under sixteen went with them by law, carrying what they could carry. Bedding. Pans. A man with a goat on a string. A woman with a birdcage, with no bird in it. Nobody fought. That was what Tamsin could not get over afterwards. A thousand people, and the bailiffs were perhaps forty, with their grey coats and their docket-batons, and nobody fought. They walked. They had been walking toward this all their lives, one hour's air at a time, and when it came they knew the way.

Brother Lusk stood at the Lip.

He was where the light ended, at the iron post where cutters clipped their tethers, a big pale man in grey with his baton through his belt and his hands folded in front of him, and as each family reached him he nodded to them, gravely, the way a priest nods at a door, and they walked past him and on into the blue dusk of the Cold, and their lamps went down the dark road and grew small.

"He's counting them," said Orrin. It was the first thing he had said. "Look. His lips. He's counting them in."

The counter at the table was a heavy-set grey-haired man with ink on his cuffs. Tamsin did not know him. Orrin did.

"Abney," Orrin said. "Senior Counter Abney. Third hall. He taught me long division." His voice did not change. "He refused my correction. _Surcharges are not corrected during a Calling._ He's very careful. He's the most careful man I know."

The column was ending. Along the Galleries, faint, a sound had begun — a sound Tamsin had heard once before in her life, at her grandmother's funeral, when the whole of Twenty-Two had done it. Somebody on a terrace high above had taken an iron bar and struck a pipe. A water main, a rail, anything that would ring. A flat hard clang, like the Hook. Then another, on another terrace. Then another. It went round the curve of the world, gallery to gallery, the way the songs went round, until all of Lantern was ringing, thousands of iron bars on thousands of pipes, not in time, not together, a great ragged clanging din that went on and on, under the stammering Wick, while the last of the first thousand walked past Brother Lusk into the dark.

Lusk turned his head, slowly, and looked up at the terraces, at the ringing.

He did not seem angry. He seemed — Tamsin thought, afterwards — interested. As if it were a sum that had come out in an unexpected way, and he would want to check it later.

Beside her, Orrin Sallow was crying. He was doing it without any noise, standing quite straight, with the tears running down his face and his hands at his sides.

"I believed it," he said. "All of it. _The Count is just._ I said it every morning. I said it to her." He did not wipe his face. "I want to see the floor of the Hulk, Tamsin. Frame forty. I want to see the fourteen. I want to see them with my own eyes."

"You will," said Tamsin.

She found that her hand was in his. She did not remember taking it. She did not let go.
