## The Limb
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Fen Adaire @@Tol Brask
Items: ^The Mercy Bell
When: +1 day
]

Fen found him in Hold 1 at six in the morning, sitting on a crate beside the bell with the lamp burned down to a glow at his feet and his voice gone to a whisper, reading the founders' band.

She did not know he was there. She had come for the bell. Tol had let her out of her cabin an hour before with his mechanical key on its loop of wire — Kell's people had confined him to the engine spaces, not to his cabin, and the engine spaces had more doors than Kell knew about — and she had come down through the crate-maze of the dark hold with no plan at all except to be beside it when they reached the rock, because it was hers, because it was going home, because she could not bear that it should arrive alone under a Brightwater tarpaulin. And she came round the last stack of crates and there was the adjuster, on a crate in the cold, with his hand on the bronze and his face very close to it, saying in a cracked whisper: "Okafor, Grace. Okeke, Samuel. Oladipo, Bisi."

She stood still.

He had not heard her. He went on. "Olsen, Erik. Olsen, Marta. Ortiz, Benedicto." He was reading by the lamp's last glow with his finger on each name, slowly, the way she did it. His voice kept failing and he kept pushing through it. "Osei, Kwame. Ostrowski—"

"Anna," said Fen.

He stopped. He turned his head and looked at her, and his face in the dying light was grey and hollow and wet, and he did not seem surprised to see her at all.

"Ostrowski, Anna," he said. "Yes."

"How long have you been doing that?"

"Since about midnight." He looked at the bell. "I did the crossing first. The lip. Then the colony, up the waist, as far as I could read. Then I started on the band." He swallowed. "I'm on the O's."

She came and sat down on the next crate. She did not say anything. She had come down meaning to say a great many things to him and found she could not remember any of them.

"There's something on the crown," said Corvin. "Did you know? On the big leaf. Very small. In a hand that shakes."

"I thought it was a damaged name."

"It isn't." He held up the lamp, and she leaned in, and read it, and sat back.

_THESE ARE OWED. SAY THEIR NAMES._

They sat side by side on the crates in the cold, and neither of them spoke for a long time.

"I signed them," Corvin said at last. "All three. The default. The consolidation. The bell. I signed them because he said he'd revive your family's debt if I didn't, and I believed him, and I'm good at signing things. My hand doesn't shake. It never has." He looked at his hand. "I can't take it back. A mark's a mark. That's the whole of what I am, Fen. You said so. A man who concurs."

"I spat at you."

"You were right to."

"I know I was." She looked at the bell. "What are you going to do?"

"I'm going to take it to the rock," said Corvin, "and do what it says."

Above them, through the deck and the hull and the cold, the deep thrum of the _Good Title_'s engine changed its note. They felt the ship come round. And on the comm in the hold's bulkhead, faint, a young voice — Dacey's, on the bridge, forgetting that Kell's people could hear him — said: "Clearing the limb. Clearing the limb. Oh. Oh, look at that."

## Lantern
[
Place: #Lantern
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Fen Adaire @@Quennell Haste @@Idris Kell @@Tol Brask
When: same day
]

From the bridge it was a rock.

A long grey irregular lump of nickel-iron eleven kilometres from end to end, turning slowly against the banded ochre face of Nhai, pocked and scarred and old. There were structures on it — the remains of gantries, aerials, the ring of a dock at the fore end — and none of them had lights. Corvin had stood in front of four hundred and twelve settlements and he knew what a dead habitat looked like from outside. It looked like this.

Then the rock turned, and showed them its fore end, and the dock ring, and in the middle of the dock ring, the great inner lock.

It was open.

And out of it — out of the rock, out of the inside of the world, through the round mouth of the lock and the wide ring of the dock — light was pouring. White light. Hard and pure and steady, the light of a sun, spilling out of the hollow rock into the dark in a long pale cone that went away from the lock across the black for a thousand kilometres before it faded, like the beam of a lamp left burning in the window of a house at night, for someone who had not come home yet.

Nobody on the bridge said anything.

"Lantern," said Fen at last, very quietly, beside Corvin. "That's why. That's why they called it that."

Haste was at the front of the bridge with Kell. He was looking at the light with his head a little on one side, the way he had looked at the Throat from the queue at Ambry: a performance being put on for him. He turned round.

"A populated habitat, then," he said. "Mr. Saal, you'll be giving the first speech. Sergeant, please have your people and the labour gang at the forward lock in two hours. Comms, raise them, please."

Dacey looked at him. Then he looked at his board, and swallowed, and touched it.

"They're raising us," he said. "Sir. They've been raising us since we cleared the limb. Old Company band. Voice." He put it on the speaker.

It was a woman's voice. Old, steady, unhurried, with an accent Corvin had never heard — the vowels flattened, the consonants soft, as if the language had been kept in a closed room for seven hundred years and had gone its own way in there.

"—ship off our fore quarter, this is Lantern. Ship, this is Lantern. Owing and owed. We see you. We saw you clear the limb. Who are you, and what do you bring? Ship, this is Lantern—"

Haste leaned past Dacey and touched the board himself.

"Lantern, this is the vessel _Good Title_, out of Port Ambry, on behalf of Brightwater Recovery of Kessane, which holds by lawful purchase the instrument of the Halden-Ash Company's Sabra Concern." His voice was warm, pleasant, exact. "We bring you greetings from the Concord, which has not forgotten you. We'll be docking within two hours. Please prepare to receive us."

The speaker hissed. For a long time there was nothing. Then the old woman's voice came back, and it had not changed at all.

"_Good Title_, Lantern. We heard you. Lantern owes the Company nothing. Lantern owes Caul." A pause. "Dock at the inner lock. We'll be waiting."

The channel closed.

Haste straightened up. He was smiling.

"We'll see," he said.

## The Reader
[
Place: #The Dock Ring
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Fen Adaire @@Quennell Haste @@Idris Kell @@Senna Hask @@Tol Brask
Items: ^The Mercy Bell ^The Count Key
When: same day
]

They had been waiting, it turned out, for seven hundred years, and they had got very good at it.

The _Good Title_ came in through the open inner lock of the Dock Ring at noon by the ship's clock, and Corvin, standing at the forward port with the case in his hand, saw the inside of the world.

He had read the Company's descriptions. They had not prepared him. A hollow world, lit from within, the walls of it climbing away on every side and meeting overhead, every surface terraced and built on and grown over — houses, roofs, tanks, farms, green, so much green, hanging upside down in the sky above him — and down the middle of it all, from end to end of the rock, a bar of white fire so bright he could not look at it, a sword laid along the axis of the world. And under the sword, in the light, on every terrace and road and roof that he could see, people. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. All of them standing still, all of them looking at the ship.

The dock was full. A square below the inner lock, as much of a square as the curve of the rock allowed, packed from wall to wall, and silent. When the _Good Title_'s ramp went down and Haste walked out onto it in his dark coat with Kell behind him and Corvin behind Kell, the silence did not change at all.

There was an iron bracket above the inner lock. The length of a man's arm, black, very old, drilled into the rock. There was nothing on it. Under it, on a bench, there was an old woman.

She stood up as Haste came down the ramp. She was tiny and very straight, with white hair cut short and pale eyes, almost colourless, in a face like folded paper, and she wore a plain grey coat and round her neck, on a chain, a disk of brass the size of a palm, worn smooth. Beside her stood a dozen others, men and women, young and old, in ordinary clothes; and behind them, the square.

"I'm Senna Hask," she said. "Reader of the Account. You're the Company."

"I'm Quennell Haste, of Brightwater Recovery, which—"

"Holds the instrument. Yes. We heard you." Senna Hask looked at him with her pale eyes, without any particular expression. "We've known you'd come since the Throat began to heal. Our Wickwrights watch it. Three years now. We've had time to think about what to say to you." She folded her hands. "Lantern owes the Company nothing. The Company's claim was quit at the root on the forty-ninth day of the Closing, by a Wick-tender called Dagny Ostrova, with a key the Company's own comptroller made. Every account on Lantern since that hour has been owed to the dead of Caul Deep. Nine hundred and four of them. Nobody holds it. Nobody draws on it. Nobody can sell it. It's owed to them." She paused. "We have kept it honestly for seven hundred years, Mr. Haste. Every hour of air. We'll go on keeping it. But not for you."

Haste smiled. He had a slate in his hand; he held it up.

"Brightwater contends," he said, "that the purported assignment of the Concern's receivables in the year of the Closing was made without the authority of the Company's officers, using an unregistered device, by a person with no standing under the Concern's charter. It was, in short, a fraud. A theft. Under Concord law a fraudulent assignment is void, and the original creditor's claim revives — with interest — attaching to the persons and issue of the original debtors." He lowered the slate. "That's you, Mrs. Hask. All of you. I'm very sorry. I know it's a great deal to take in. My colleague Mr. Saal is here to explain it."

He turned.

They all turned. The square turned. Thirty thousand pairs of eyes — Corvin felt them, the way you feel heat — came to rest on him, standing at the foot of the ramp with the case in his hand.

He had given this speech four hundred and twelve times. He knew it the way he knew his own name. It began _I understand that this is difficult_, and it was calm and sorry and unmovable, and it had a low rate of incident.

He opened his mouth to begin it, and found that it had gone.

"Mr. Saal," said Haste pleasantly.

Corvin looked at the old woman. Then at the empty iron bracket above her head. Then back up the ramp into the ship, to where Fen was standing in the hatchway, with Tol beside her, and between them on its sled, under its Brightwater tarpaulin, something round and heavy and the height of a person's hip.

"Mrs. Hask," he said. "What discharges it?"

Haste's head turned sharply.

Senna Hask looked at Corvin for a long moment.

"Their names," she said. "Spoken at the root. Every one, in order. That's the term Ostrova set. It's in the Tally and it's in the Founders' log. We read it out every year." Her voice did not change. "We haven't got them. Their names were on the bell of the _Ardent Mercy_. It hung there." She lifted one hand, without looking, toward the empty bracket. "Ostrova's brother took it through the Throat as it closed. Seven hundred years ago. To tell the Concord what was done at Caul. Nobody in the Concord ever heard, I suppose. And we've never had them since. We ring the Hook for them. That's all we can do."

"Brightwater holds the bell," said Corvin.

The square was utterly silent.

"It's an asset of the Sabra Concern," he said. "Recovered property. Mr. Haste seized it under the instrument, on the voyage. I concurred. My mark's on the docket. Which makes it — under the instrument Mr. Haste has just read you — property of the Concern, held by Brightwater, in the custody of Brightwater's adjuster." He turned and looked up the ramp. "Fen."

She was already moving. She and Tol came down the ramp together with the sled between them, and the sled's runners scraped on the steel, and at the foot of the ramp Fen took hold of the tarpaulin with the Brightwater seal on it and pulled it off.

The bell of the _Ardent Mercy_ stood in the light of the Wick for the first time in seven hundred and two years.

Nobody in the square made a sound. Then the old woman under the Hook took one step forward, and stopped, and put her hand to her mouth; and somewhere at the back of the square somebody began to cry, a long thin wavering sound that went on and on; and then the crowd moved, all of it, a great slow surge forward, not fast, not angry, the way water moves when a dam goes — toward the bell.

"Sergeant," said Haste. His voice was not pleasant any more. "Secure that asset. And the root. Now."

## The Founders' Lift
[

When: same day
]

Afterwards Corvin could never get it into the right order.

He remembered Kell's people coming down the ramp behind him in their pairs, with their batons low, the way they had drilled in Hold 1, and stopping, at the foot of it, because there was nowhere to go: the square had closed round the bell and the sled and Fen and Tol and the old woman, thousands deep, not touching anyone, only there. He remembered Kell looking at it. Looking at the faces. Looking, Corvin thought later, at the woken sleepers — the labour gang, a hundred and thirty of them still alive from the first three batches, standing in a huddle at the top of the ramp in their thin ship's coveralls, blinking in the light of a sun they had never seen, being stared at by a world. He remembered that one of the sleepers, a thin boy, had begun to walk down the ramp on his own, and that a woman at the edge of the crowd had held out her hand to him without a word, and that he had taken it.

He remembered Haste saying _Sergeant_ again, and Kell not moving.

Then he remembered the Founders' lift: a great iron platform at the side of the square, under the Counting House, and Senna Hask's people running the bell's sled onto it, and the platform going down, into the rock, into the dark, with the bell on it and Fen beside it and Corvin beside Fen, and the old woman holding the rail, and the light of the Wick going away above them in a closing square.

"Where are we going?" he said.

"The root," said Senna Hask. "Under the ship."

"What ship?"

She looked at him with her pale eyes.

"The _Covenant_," she said. "The Company's carrier. She came home, at the Closing. She's under the Count. She's been the floor of the world for seven hundred years."

Fen made a small sound. Corvin turned. She was staring at the old woman.

"The _Covenant_," Fen said. "The Director's statement said the staff would withdraw on the _Covenant_. I've read it a hundred times." She shook her head slowly. "She never arrived anywhere either. None of them did. I thought— everybody thought—"

"She came back," said Senna Hask. "Ostrova brought her back."

## Discharge
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Fen Adaire @@Senna Hask @@Quennell Haste @@Idris Kell @@Tol Brask
Items: ^The Mercy Bell ^The Tally ^The Count Key
When: same day
]

The root was a round room, not large, with a low domed ceiling, at the bottom of a tilted ship buried nose-first in a rock. At its centre on a pedestal there was a desk of dark glass and brass with three slots in its face and a little grille beside them like the grille of a confessional. That was all.

They brought the bell in on its sled and set it beside the desk. Senna Hask took the brass disk from round her neck and put it into the left-hand slot, and the glass woke, and the old woman bent to the grille and said, in her flat soft accent:

"Discharge of the Account. Terms as set. The creditors' names, spoken, in full, in order."

The glass was still for a moment. Then, in plain white letters:

DISCHARGE TERMS PRESENTED. BEGIN.

Senna Hask straightened up and stepped back. She looked at Corvin.

"You've got the bell," she said. "You say them."

He looked at Fen. She was already kneeling beside the bell with her loupe at her eye and her lamp in her hand, and she held up the lamp, and its light fell on the founders' band, on the long cramped grey line of names going round the waist of the bronze in hurried hands. She did not look at him. She put her finger on the first name.

"Abara, Joseph," said Corvin Saal.

Fen struck the bell.

It was not a hammer. There was no hammer. It was her open hand, flat against the bronze, the way Corvin had seen her lay it there a hundred times — but harder, so that the bell spoke. A low note, a little sour, with a waver in it. The note from the recording. It went up into the dome of the root and hung there.

"Achebe, Ruth," said Corvin.

She struck it again.

They went on. Name, and the bell. Name, and the bell. It was slow. It had to be. Some of the names were cut so cramped and so hurried that Fen had to bend close with the loupe and read them to him in a whisper first, letter by letter, and then he said them aloud; and the glass on the console beside them took each one and, after it, in small plain letters, wrote it down. ABARA, JOSEPH. ACHEBE, RUTH. ADAIR, MICHAEL. A line at a time, going up the glass.

Above them, through the decks of the buried ship, through the rock, he could hear the square. It had been silent. It was not silent now. It was ringing. Iron on iron, flat and hard, from the Hook, and from the rails and pipes and posts of the Dock Ring, and from further away, from the terraces and galleries up the curving walls of the world, more and more of them — thirty thousand people with bars of iron, striking whatever would ring, not in time, not together, a great ragged din coming down through seven hundred years of rock.

"Agbaje, Femi," said Corvin.

He was on the H's when Haste came into the root.

He came alone. Corvin heard him on the ladder before he saw him — quick, light steps — and then he was in the doorway, in his dark coat, with his hair disarranged for the first time Corvin had ever seen it, and in his hand a short ugly slug-gun he must have taken off one of Kell's people, held as if he had never held one before and found it a little distasteful.

"Stop," said Haste.

"Hussain, Amira," said Corvin.

Fen struck the bell.

"Mr. Saal, I'm not going to tell you again. That device is the property of the Concern and so is every account on that machine, and I will not have them—"

"Ibe, Chidi," said Corvin.

Haste shot him.

He felt it as a blow to the left side, low, under the ribs, as if someone had punched him there with a fist the size of a melon. He sat down. He did not decide to; his legs went and he was sitting on the floor of the root with his back against the pedestal, and the white letters on the glass above him, and the bell in front of him, and Fen's face, white, turned toward him, with the lamp in her hand.

"Read," he said to her. His voice came out very strangely. "Read me the next one."

"Corvin—"

"Read me the next one, Fen."

She looked at him for one more moment. Then she turned back to the bell and put the loupe to her eye.

"Idowu, Grace," she said.

"Idowu, Grace," said Corvin.

She struck the bell.

Haste had not moved from the doorway. He was looking at the slug-gun in his hand as if it had done something embarrassing in public. Behind him, on the ladder, there were heavier steps; and then Idris Kell was in the doorway too, and reached past Haste, without any particular hurry, and took the gun out of his hand, gently, the way you would take something from a child. Haste turned and stared at him.

"Sergeant—"

"I've got a daughter," said Kell. He said it in the same bored voice in which he had explained enumeration on the gantry in Hold 1. "Twenty-six years attaching, from her mother's side. And there's a boy up there off our hold holding hands with a woman he's never met in his life, in the light, and I'm not putting either of them down in front of their own bell, Mr. Haste. Not for four years off mine. Not for forty." He put the gun in his belt. "Sit down, sir. It's going to be a while."

Haste sat down.

"Ige, Folake," said Fen.

"Ige, Folake," said Corvin.

The bell.

## Zeller, Hanna
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Fen Adaire @@Senna Hask @@Tol Brask
Items: ^The Mercy Bell ^The Tally
When: same day
]

It took three hours and nine minutes. Senna Hask timed it, she told him afterwards; the Reader times everything, it is in the office. For the last hour of it Corvin did not know very clearly where he was. He knew Fen's voice, reading him each name before he said it, and he knew the bell, and he knew that someone — Senna Hask's people, or Tol, who had come down the ladder at some point and knelt beside him with his greasy hands pressed hard against Corvin's side — was keeping him from bleeding to death on the floor of the root of a dead company's ledger under a ship that had come home. He knew the ringing, coming down through the rock. It did not stop. Once, he thought, it changed — it went quiet, all at once, everywhere, for the space of a breath, and then came back louder — and he learned later that that had been the moment when the glass on the console wrote OSTROWSKI, ANNA, and somebody up in the square who had been counting had called out the number, and the whole world had heard it and stopped, and gone on.

"Zeller, Hanna," said Fen, at nine minutes past three in the afternoon.

Corvin said it.

She put her hand flat on the bronze and struck the bell for the last time, and the low sour note went up into the dome of the root and hung there, humming, for a long time, and slowly faded.

The glass on the console was quite still.

Then the long column of names on it — nine hundred and four of them, Abara to Zeller, in plain white letters, a line at a time — went out, all at once. And in their place, in the middle of the glass, the Tally of Lantern, which had kept every account on the rock every hour on the hour for eight hundred years, wrote:

CREDITOR NAMES SPOKEN IN FULL. ACCOUNT OF CAUL DISCHARGED.

And then, after a pause, as though it were checking:

NO BALANCE OUTSTANDING ON THE TALLY. NO CREDITOR. NO DEBTOR.

And then, in letters no different from the others, but which Corvin would see, for the rest of his life, whenever he shut his eyes:

THE TALLY IS QUIT. VALVES RELEASED TO OPEN.

Above them, through the rock, he heard the ringing stop.

## The Hook
[
Place: #The Dock Ring
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Fen Adaire @@Senna Hask @@Tol Brask @@Idris Kell
Items: ^The Mercy Bell:#The Dock Ring
When: +9 days
]

They hung the bell back on the Hook nine days later.

The old shackle was gone; there was only the stub of it, cut through, rough and black, where somebody had put a torch to it seven hundred years before. Lantern's smiths made a new one. It took them four days, because they wanted it right, and they cast it from the brass of a Company valve-wheel they took off the Pumphouse door, which nobody on Lantern needed to ask any longer whether it could open. Then the Wick crews climbed the Hook with chains and slings, and the bell went up, slowly, swinging a little in the slings, humming, and came to rest on its iron bracket over the inner lock of the Dock Ring, where it had hung for ninety-one years before anyone now living had been born.

Corvin watched from the bench under it. He could sit up now, for an hour or two at a time. Lantern's doctors had taken the slug out of his side on the second day, in an infirmary on the second floor of the Counting House, in a bed that Senna Hask told him had been in that room since the Closing; and they had told him, in their flat soft accent, that he was a lucky man, and he had not argued. Senna Hask had sat by that bed on the third evening and told him, because he asked, how the lamp had been kept alight for seven hundred years: fourteen cassettes, out of the belly of the buried ship, that had lit the Wick end to end for fourteen years; and in those fourteen years the Wickwrights and the ice-runners of the Salt had learned between them to skim the fuel from Nhai's clouds and make their own, badly at first and then well, because they had known exactly how long they had.

He was a bonded man too. He had not argued with that either. The instrument was void; Haste, who was confined to the _Good Title_ with Kell's people standing at its lock and very little to say to anyone, had conceded that much on the fourth day, after Corvin had sent him, from his bed, a single slate with the adjuster's certification on it — _Discharge of the Sabra Concern's receivables under the instrument's own terms. Debt extinguished. Instrument void. C. Saal, adjuster_ — and the slate had come back with a partner's mark under his, in a hand that was, for the first time, not quite steady. Three thousand and eleven contracts assigned to a void instrument had nothing to attach to. Lantern was waking the sleepers fifty at a time, as Mabry Ode had said a decent man would, and finding them berths. But there was no discharge in Haste's drawer for Corvin Saal. There never would be now. His father's water debt on Kessane was owed to Brightwater still, the way it had always been, and it would be owed when the _Good Title_ went back through the Throat, and he supposed he would pay it. He found he did not mind as much as he had expected. It was his. He knew whose it was. That was something.

When the bell was hung, the square went quiet, the way it had gone quiet for the Hook every day for seven hundred years.

Senna Hask came and stood under it. She had the bell-rope in her hands — a new one, braided from hauler cable — and she looked up at the bronze for a long time, at the furred brown surface of it, every inch of it letters.

Then she turned and held the rope out to Fen.

Fen did not take it at once. She stood in front of the old woman with her hands at her sides.

"There's something I never told anyone," she said. "My family never told anyone either. It's inside. In the mouth. Scratched, not cut — with a knife, I think, or a buckle. We always thought it was the salvager's mark. Two words and a name." She looked up into the bell's mouth, where the light of the Wick did not reach. "It says RING IT. And then a name. Most of it's gone. K, and an S at the end."

Senna Hask did not say anything for a moment.

"Kasimir Ostrova," she said. "Dagny Ostrova's brother. He sold her, at the Closing, for two seats on the _Covenant_. And then he took the bell through the Throat as it shut, on his own, in a tug with no business going through a throat at all. The Founders' log says so." She looked up into the dark of the bell's mouth. "He was trying to get the names out. So somebody would listen."

"Somebody did," said Fen. Her voice cracked. "It took a while."

She took the rope.

"For Kasimir Ostrova," she said, and rang the bell.

The note went out across the Dock Ring, low and a little sour, with a waver in it, and round the curve of the world under the blazing Wick, and on every terrace and road and roof that Corvin could see, people stopped what they were doing, and stood still for it, the breath of a bell, and went on.

"For Mabry Ode," said Fen, and rang it again.

"For Marisol Tey."

"For a man called Roach, at the throat-watch, who waited."

She went on. Senna Hask stood beside her and said, after each name, very quietly, "Owing and owed"; and after a while the square began to say it with her, the way it always had — and Corvin, on the bench, understood that it did not mean what it had meant. It had meant a ledger. It meant, now, only that somebody had been here, and was remembered, and that the people who remembered them had not finished being grateful.

When she was done, Fen came and sat down beside him on the bench, and leaned against his good side, very carefully, and put her head on his shoulder. Above them the bell hummed on its hook, slowly, slowly fading. Down the middle of the sky the Wick burned from end to end of the world.

"They'll need somebody to cut the new names," she said after a while. "Senna says. There's no room left on her anywhere. They're going to have to cast another bell."

"Can you cut bronze?"

"No." She did not lift her head. "I can learn. I read old hands for a living. It can't be that different from writing one."

He sat with her under the bell in the light. Somewhere above them, in the Counting House, in a round room at the bottom of a buried ship, a desk of dark glass and brass sat silent at the root of the world, with nothing left to count; and all through Lantern, in the Pumphouse and the Galleries and the farms, every valve on the rock opened when it was asked, without asking whose air it was.

Nobody owed anybody for it. For the first time in eight hundred years, nobody owed anything at all.
