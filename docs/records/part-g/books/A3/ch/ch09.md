## The Throat-Watch
[
Place: #Throat-Watch Station
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Fen Adaire @@Tol Brask
When: +2 days
]

The throat-watch station was where the Company's charts said it would be, which was the first thing in the Sabra system that had been.

It hung forty thousand kilometres sunward of the Throat, in the slow orbit the Company's engineers had given it seven hundred years ago, a squat grey cylinder the size of a house with a ring of dishes round one end and a long boom at the other carrying instruments nobody aboard the _Good Title_ could name. It was dark. Its dishes were pointed, still, at the place where the Throat had been and was now again, a pink bruise on the sky behind it. Frost had grown on it from somewhere — outgassing, Tol said, from its own seals, over centuries — so that it looked, in the ship's floodlights, like something that had been left out overnight in a garden.

"It's an asset of the Concern," Haste had said, at breakfast, in the voice of a man reading from a list, "and its records may be of value in establishing the circumstances of the Closing. Mr. Saal will go across. Miss Adaire will go with him, since she reads the formats. Captain, you'll lend them your engineer."

"I'll lend them my engineer," Ode had said, "and I'll want him back."

So the three of them went across, in suits, on a line from the _Good Title_'s lock, with the dark between them and the station and the stars very still. Corvin had done vacuum work on the colony circuit, a little, the way adjusters did — boarding hulks to tag them, walking frozen domes. He did not like it. He liked it less with the bruise of the Throat over his shoulder, breathing.

Tol got the station's lock open with a pry-bar and a good deal of muttering. Inside there was no air. There was a short corridor, very narrow, lined with lockers whose doors had sprung open over the years, and at the end of it a round room with a ring of consoles and a single chair.

There was a man in the chair.

He had been dead a very long time. The cold and the vacuum had kept him, after a fashion: he was dry and dark and sunken, his face drawn back from his teeth, his hands shrunk to claws on the arms of the chair. He wore a Company coverall with the two leaning letters on the breast, and a name-strip that said ROACH, and he was sitting facing the main console, where a single dead screen looked back at him. On the console in front of him there was a cup, a paper logbook, and a pen.

Nobody said anything for a while. Their breath sounded very loud in the suits.

"He stayed," said Fen at last. "At his post. To the end."

"Or he was left at it," said Corvin.

She looked at him through her faceplate. Then she looked away.

Tol went to the console banks and started opening panels, muttering to the machines in the way he did. Fen went to the logbook. She did not touch the dead man; she reached past him, very carefully, as if he might wake, and slid the book out from under his hand. Its pages were brittle with cold. She turned them with the tip of a gloved finger.

"Readings," she said. "Every six hours. Columns of figures. I don't know what they mean." She turned more pages. "Then notes. Personal. He's lonely. He writes about a woman called Hennie on Lantern. Something about her garden." Another page. "Then— it changes." Her voice changed with it. "The columns stop. Just notes. _Asked the Concern again for confirmation of the closing-phase figures. No reply._ And then a few days later, _No reply._ And then _No reply_ again. And then nothing for a long time, and then just a line."

"What does it say?"

Fen held the book up so that his helmet lamp fell on the page. The hand was a scrawl, the letters crowding, as if written in the dark or in a hurry or by someone very cold.

_They told me someone would come for me._

That was all. Under it the page was blank, and all the pages after.

"Right," said Tol, from somewhere inside a console with only his boots showing. "I've got the core. It's intact. Cold kept it. But it's locked six ways to Sunday with Company keys, and I'm no codebreaker."

"I am," said Fen. "Bring it."

They took the core and the logbook. They left the man. Corvin stood a moment longer than the others in the round room, looking at him in his chair, with his dead screen and his cup and his name-strip, ROACH, and thought about four hundred and twelve settlements and the faces on them, and a water-office on Kessane; and then, because nobody else was going to, he reached out and closed what was left of the man's eyelids, which did not close.

"Corvin," said Fen, from the corridor.

"Coming."

## Persons and Issue
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Quennell Haste @@Idris Kell
When: same day
]

Haste wanted to talk about the instrument. He wanted to talk about it most evenings, in the _Good Title_'s cramped wardroom, over a glass of the good Kessane brandy he had brought aboard in a case of his own, and Corvin had come to understand that this was not because Haste needed to explain it to him. It was because Haste liked to hear it.

"Persons and issue," Haste said, turning the glass. "Do you know, I think it's the most beautiful phrase in commercial law? Persons. And issue. A debt attaches to the person who incurred it, and to every person who comes out of them, until it's paid. It sounds monstrous when you put it like that. People do put it like that. But think what it means." He held the glass to the light. "It means a promise outlives the man who made it. It means that what you owe doesn't vanish because you've had the good fortune to die. That's not monstrous, Mr. Saal. That's civilisation. That's the whole of it. A world where promises die with the promisor is a world where nobody will lend you anything at all."

"The people on Lantern didn't make the promise," said Corvin. "Their ancestors did. Seven hundred years ago. Most of them under contract to people who owned the air they were breathing."

"Their ancestors signed. Freely, under the law of the day. In return for passage to a new world, which they received." Haste sipped. "Your father signed for you, too. You've never once in thirty-five years suggested to me that his signature was void."

Corvin said nothing.

At the end of the wardroom table Sergeant Kell had a slug-gun in pieces on an oilcloth and was cleaning it, part by part, with the slow absorbed care of a man shelling peas. He did not look up. He had not said a word all evening. He seemed to Corvin like part of the furniture: something solid and practical bolted to the deck, waiting.

"I've been meaning to ask," said Corvin. "About the hold."

"Agricultural stock," said Haste, without a pause.

"Your agricultural stock draws more power than the rest of the ship together. The engineer showed me the board."

"Does it?" Haste raised his eyebrows, mildly. "I'm no engineer. I imagine it's the embryo library. Embryos are delicate. They have to be kept very cold." He set his glass down. "Is there a reason you're interested, Mr. Saal? Beyond the general curiosity of a careful man?"

There it was. The pleasant voice, and under it the other thing. Corvin had heard that voice in his own mouth on four hundred and twelve settlements: _is there a reason you're interested?_ It was the voice that told a man where the edge was, and invited him to step back from it.

He stepped back from it. He was aware of himself doing so. He felt it the way he had felt, once, on a colony on Malory's Reach, the moment when the floor of a dome had started to give under his boot and he had shifted his weight without thinking.

"No," he said. "No reason."

"Good," said Haste, and smiled, and poured him another brandy he did not want.

At the end of the table Kell slid the bolt back into the slug-gun with a small oiled click, and looked up for the first time, straight at Corvin, with his bored, patient, uninterested eyes; and then looked down again and went on cleaning.

## Something Still Counting
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Fen Adaire @@Mabry Ode @@Tol Brask
Items: ^The Tally
When: +1 day
]

The signal came on the fourth day out from the Throat, at nine minutes past the hour.

The young comm officer heard it first. His name was Dacey, and he was nineteen, and he had been listening to the silence of the Sabra system for four days with the dogged attention of somebody who has been told by his captain that silence is never really silent if you listen hard enough. He had been working through the Company's old frequency tables band by band, the dull ones, the housekeeping channels, the ones nobody would ever have used to call for help. On the eleventh of them, a narrow band labelled in the 2387 tables simply _HOURLY — INTERNAL_, he heard a click.

He sat up. He waited. He was a patient boy. At nine minutes past the next hour, it came again: not a click this time, but a burst. A short, dense squeal of data, two seconds long, and then nothing.

He called the captain. The captain called Tol, and Tol called Fen, and by the time the next hour came round there were six people crowded into the comm space behind the bridge, Corvin among them, in a silence so complete that he could hear Ode's breathing.

Nine minutes past. The burst came.

"Again," said Fen. "Run it back. Slower."

Dacey ran it back. On his screen the two-second squeal unfolded into a shape: a long ragged comb of pulses, dense at the front, thinning toward the end. Fen leaned over his shoulder, her face very close to the screen, her lips moving.

"It's Company format," she said. "Their old internal packet. I've seen it in the archives. There's a header—" she put her finger on the dense front of the comb— "and then a long body, and then a checksum. The header's encrypted. The body's encrypted. But the structure—" She stopped.

"What?" said Ode.

"The structure's a posting." Fen straightened up. She looked, Corvin thought, as if she had seen something in the room behind them. "That's what the Company called it. An hourly posting. It's the Tally."

Nobody spoke.

"The ledger," said Fen. "The thing that ran the habitat. The Company's documents are full of it. Every hour, on the hour, it posted every account — every debit and credit, air and water and heat, every person on the rock. And it broadcast a summary on this band, to the Company's receivers. Here. The throat-watch. The haulers. So the Company would always know what it was owed." She looked at the screen. "It's still doing it. Seven hundred years. On the hour. Plus nine minutes, because its clock has drifted. It's still posting the accounts."

"So they're alive," said Dacey. His voice cracked on it, and he flushed.

"No," said Fen. "Not necessarily. That's the point. The Tally doesn't need anyone. The Director's statement said so: it's fully autonomous, it requires no Company supervision. If everyone on Lantern died six hundred years ago it would still post their accounts every hour, for as long as it had power. It'd post the dead. It'd post them as closed, or as estate, or—" She stopped again. "I don't know what it would post them as. I can't read it."

"The core," said Corvin. "From the throat-watch. It'll have the Company's keys."

"It might. If I can get into it. If the keys are the same ones. If the Tally hasn't changed its own in seven hundred years." She pushed her hair back with both hands. "If I could read the body of that packet, I could tell you how many accounts it's posting, and whether the balances are moving. If they're moving, someone's breathing. If they're not—"

"How long?" said Haste.

He was in the doorway. Nobody had heard him come. Fen turned round and looked at him, and Corvin saw her face close like a door.

"I don't know," she said. "Days. A week. It's seven-hundred-year-old cryptography on a core that's been in vacuum since before my family had a name."

"Take as long as you need," said Haste pleasantly. "It doesn't change anything, of course. Either way, we're going to the rock." He looked at the screen, at the ragged comb of the burst, as though it were an interesting curio in a dealer's window. "But I'd like to know which speech Mr. Saal will be giving."

He went away again. After a while, so did everybody else, back to their posts, back to their bunks, until there was only Dacey at his console and Fen at his shoulder and Corvin in the doorway.

Ten minutes before the hour, Fen said, without turning round: "Do you want to hear it again?"

"Yes," said Corvin.

So they waited. Nine minutes past the hour, from somewhere on the far side of the great banded planet ahead of them, behind which a rock eleven kilometres long was turning in the dark, the burst came: two seconds of dense, patient, meaningless noise, exactly as it had come every hour for seven hundred and two years, to receivers that had been dead for most of them.

Something was still counting.

Corvin stood in the doorway of the comm space and listened to it, and thought — he could not help thinking it, and despised himself a little for it — that it was the sound of a debt being kept. And that he had been sent all this way, at the end of thirty-five years of his own, to collect it.
