## The Ledger Halls
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Orrin Sallow
Items: ^The Tally
When: +4 days
]

Orrin Sallow had been taught, at six, that every number in the Account was a person, and that a counter who made an error had wronged somebody he would never meet.

He had believed it then and he believed it now, at twenty-six, sitting at his slate in the third ledger hall of the Counting House with the lamps turned up as far as the Count allowed and a cup of cooling barley-water at his elbow. He believed it in the plain unexamined way he believed that the Wick was above him and the rock below. It was why he had become a counter rather than a doctor or a Wickwright, which had been his mother's two hopes for him. A doctor saved the one in front of him. A counter, if he was careful, kept faith with all of them.

The ledger halls lay in the belly of the Counting House, under the old Company offices and over the Sanctum, where the Tally itself ran and where nobody went but the Comptroller-General. There were five halls, each a long low vault with rows of desks down it, and at every desk a counter in grey with a slate, and on every slate a family. The halls were quiet. Counting was quiet work. There was only the soft tapping of styluses and, very faintly, through the floor, a hum that some of the older counters said was the Tally thinking and that Orrin privately thought was a pump.

The Hulk was under all of it. You could not forget that in the halls, because the floor of the lowest one was not rock but metal: a great curved plate, riveted, cold to the touch, painted over many times in Count grey and showing through the paint in places as a dull blue. Children who came on the Account Day tours were allowed to kneel and put their hands on it. _That's her_, the guides said. _That's the_ Ardent Mercy, _that brought the first families. The Founders built the Count on her bones, so that the Account would stand on the ship that brought us._ Orrin had put his hand on it at seven and felt nothing at all, and had been ashamed of feeling nothing, and had never told anyone.

The Calling lists had come down from the Comptroller-General's office four days ago, ten thousand accounts in order of depth, and had been divided among the halls. Each counter had been given four hundred. The instruction on each slate was the same, in the Comptroller-General's own neat hand: _Verify. Every balance, every entry, from the last birth to the present hour. No account may be Called in error. The Count is just._

Orrin had three hundred and twelve still to do. He was on Hask.

## Hask, Agathe
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Orrin Sallow
When: same day
]

Hask, Agathe. Gallery Twenty-Two. Born 270 AC. Pump-hand, thirty years; retired on sickness, 309. Dependants: Hask, Jory, fifteen. Adult child on separate account: Hask, Tamsin, cutter.

Balance: minus eleven thousand two hundred and six.

He began, as he had been taught, at the bottom. Agathe Hask's own consumption, year by year, since she got her chit at six: air, water, heat, light, housing. Ordinary. A little heavy in the last six years, which would be the lung-rot. Her own earnings against it: thirty years of pump wages, posted monthly, regular as a heartbeat, and then nothing. The difference, by itself, came to minus three thousand and forty. That was a life. That was what it cost to be Agathe Hask.

Then the inheritances. Her mother's balance, posted to her at her mother's death in 299: minus four thousand one hundred. Her father's, posted to her mother before that, and carried through. A brother's. A child's, a baby, two months old, minus nine. He paused over that one, as he always did over the small ones, and then went on.

And then, every year, on Account Day, the inheritance surcharge.

The surcharge was older than the Count. It had been in the Tally from the Founding, a levy on all inherited balances of three parts in a hundred, posted once a year, because — the counters were taught — an inherited debt was a debt that had gone unpaid for a whole lifetime, and the Account must be compensated for its patience. Orrin had calculated it ten thousand times. You took the inherited portion of the balance on Account Day and you multiplied it by three in a hundred and you posted the result.

The inherited portion. Not the whole balance.

He looked at the figure posted on Account Day 310 for Hask, Agathe. Then he looked at the one for 309. Then 308. He took a fresh page on his slate and did the arithmetic by hand, the way he had done it as a boy, with the stylus scratching.

It was wrong. It had been wrong for eleven years. Some time around 299, when her mother's balance came to her, somebody — some counter, at some desk, in some hall — had keyed the surcharge against the whole of Agathe Hask's balance instead of the inherited part. And then, each year after, the surcharge itself had been treated as inherited, because it had been posted under the inheritance heading, and had been surcharged in turn. Three parts in a hundred, compounding, on a figure that had never been owed.

He worked it through to the present hour. It took him most of the morning. When he was done he sat back and looked at it.

Corrected balance: minus nine thousand and sixty-one.

Two thousand one hundred and forty-five credits. A little over two thousand credits that Agathe Hask had never owed, that her daughter had been cutting copper in the Cold to pay down, that would have gone on her son's chit at her death and on his children's after him.

Orrin turned to the list from the Comptroller-General's office and ran his stylus down it until he found the cut: the ten-thousandth account, the shallowest that would be Called. It was minus nine thousand four hundred and twelve.

He sat very still. A counter at the next desk glanced at him, and away.

At minus eleven thousand two hundred, Agathe Hask went into the Cold. At minus nine thousand and sixty-one, she did not.

## Gallery Twenty-Two
[
Place: #The Galleries
Characters: @@Orrin Sallow @@Tamsin Hask @@Agathe Hask @@Jory Hask
Items: ^Tally Chit
When: same day
]

A Calling could not be posted on a balance alone. The rules said a counter must also sight the account-holder, in their place of residence, and confirm by their own eyes that the person existed and was where the Tally said. It was an old rule and a kind one, Orrin had always thought: it meant that before the Count did anything to a family, someone from the Count had stood in their room.

He had never been below Gallery Fifteen before. He was ashamed of that, a little, going down. The lift stopped at Eighteen and he walked the rest, along the terrace roads with his grey coat and his slate, and he felt the light change as he went: the clean white of the Upper Wards going yellow, then grey, then a sort of brownish dusk, sideways, as the Wick fell away behind the curve. The terraces were narrower here and the houses smaller and nearer together, and there was washing hung between them, and the smell was of damp and cooking and people. Children stopped playing and watched him go by. An old man spat, not at him, but not far from him either.

Twenty-Two was the last gallery before the Lip. He found the Hask door by its number. He touched his chit to the panel, the counter's chit, which opened any door on Lantern for the purpose of a sighting, and the door sighed and clunked and let him in.

There were three people in the room. A woman of fifty or so, sitting up on the lower bunk with a blanket round her, very thin, with pale, almost colourless eyes. A boy on the top bunk, all knees, who looked at Orrin's coat and then at the door and then at Orrin's slate in a way that made Orrin hold the slate a little closer. And standing at the ring-stove with her back to him, a young woman in a cutter's rig with the upper half peeled down and tied round her waist by its arms, who did not turn round.

"Counter Sallow," Orrin said. "Third hall. I'm here for a sighting. Hask, Agathe?"

"That's me," said the woman on the bunk. She had a cough; he heard it under her voice, waiting.

"I'm sorry to disturb you. It won't take a moment." He held up the slate and read the particulars from it — name, gallery, birth year, number — and she nodded to each of them, and he marked them. His stylus was not quite steady. "Thank you."

"That's it?" said the young woman at the stove. She still had not turned. "That's the sighting? You come down twenty galleries to look at my mother and say her name, and then she's sighted, and then she can be Called?"

"Tam," said the woman on the bunk.

"No, I want to know. I want to know how it works." She turned round then. She had the same pale eyes, set in a hard, dirty, angular face that would have been handsome if it had not been so angry. "Does he get paid by the head? Is there a bonus for the deep ones?"

"I'm salaried," said Orrin, before he could stop himself.

She laughed. It was not a nice sound.

"Counter," said Agathe Hask quietly. "My daughter's had a bad week. So has everybody on this gallery. You'll forgive her. Is there anything else you need?"

He should have said no. He had his sighting. He had no business in this room any longer and every reason to leave it. Instead he heard himself say, "There's an error in your account."

The room went quiet.

"An error," said Tamsin Hask.

"In the inheritance surcharge. From 299. It was keyed to the whole of your mother's balance instead of the inherited part, and then compounded. It's—" he looked at the slate, though he knew the figure— "it's two thousand one hundred and forty-five credits. Over eleven years. I've corrected it on my slate. I'm going to file it today."

Agathe Hask looked at him for a long moment.

"How much does that leave?" she said.

"Nine thousand and sixty-one."

"And where's the line? For the Calling?"

He should not have told her. The cut was not posted. It would not be posted for another twenty-six days. "Nine thousand four hundred and twelve," he said.

Nobody said anything. On the top bunk the boy had sat up.

"So she's under," said Tamsin. "If you file it. She's under the line."

"If it's accepted. Yes."

"If."

"Corrections are— they have to be approved by a senior counter. It's a formality. The arithmetic is plain. Anyone can check it." He held out the slate, as if she might want to, and she looked at it, and at him, and he understood that she could not read the figures on it and that he had just shamed her, and he took it back.

"Why are you telling us?" she said.

"Because it's true."

"Lots of things are true. The Count doesn't come down here and tell us them." She folded her arms. "What do you want?"

"Nothing. I— nothing." He found that he did not know what to do with his hands. "It's my job. The Count is just. An account can't be Called in error. That's the rule."

"The Count is just," said Tamsin, flatly, the way you'd repeat a word in a language you didn't speak.

Agathe Hask coughed, then, for a long time, bent over the blanket; and her daughter went to her and held her shoulders until it passed, and Orrin stood in the middle of the small room with his slate and looked away, at the shelf over the stove, where there was a biscuit tin with a picture of a farmhouse on its lid, very old, the paint nearly gone.

When the coughing stopped, Agathe sat back and wiped her mouth and looked at him again with her pale eyes.

"Counter Sallow," she said. "Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"Who is Caul?"

He opened his mouth to say the catechism. _Caul is the dark that kept us when the Company left us in it. Caul is owed for every breath, and the Owing pay._ He had said it every Account Day of his life. He found that he did not want to say it here, in this room, to this woman, and he did not know why.

"Caul is the Account," he said at last.

"That's what I told my girl." Agathe nodded slowly. "She didn't think much of it either."

He left soon after. In the passage outside, as the door clunked shut behind him, he reached into his coat for his stylus to mark the sighting time on his slate, and found the clip empty. He looked back at the door. He thought about knocking. He thought about the boy on the top bunk, all knees and quick eyes.

He marked the time with his fingernail, and went back up the galleries into the light.

## The Root
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Orrin Sallow
Items: ^The Tally
When: same day
]

The correction came back in an hour, with a line through it in red and a note in the margin in the cramped hand of the senior counter of the third hall: _Surcharges are not corrected during a Calling. Refile after the lists are posted. — A._

Orrin read the note three times. After the lists were posted the Haskes would be in the Cold. After the lists were posted there would be no account to correct.

He sat at his desk while the hall emptied around him for the dinner bell. He was not hungry. He was angry, and he was not used to being angry, and he found he did not know what to do with it except work. So he did what a counter does when a number will not sit right: he went back to the beginning.

The inheritance surcharge had been in the Tally from the Founding. Every counter knew that. But nobody had ever told him where in the Tally it lived, or who had put it there, or what it had been written to do. He had never needed to know. Now he wanted to. He wanted to see, written down, the rule that said three parts in a hundred, and see whether it said _inherited portion_ or _whole_. He wanted to know whether the error was a counter's, or the Tally's own.

The Count's archive of the Tally's root entries was open to counters of the fourth rank and above. He was fourth rank. He had never opened it. Hardly anyone did; it was a long scroll of old rules, rate changes, adjustments to the dimming line, all of it three hundred years dead. He found the surcharge rule after half an hour of searching. It was where it should be, in the first year after the Closing, posted on the Founders' authority. It said _inherited portion_. It was not the Tally's error. Somebody in a ledger hall had simply keyed it wrong, eleven years ago, and nobody had looked since.

He should have stopped there. He nearly did. But the archive was open on his slate and he had never seen the very first page of it, and so — with the same small guilty pleasure as putting his hand on the Hulk at seven — he scrolled to the top.

The first entry in the root of the Tally of Lantern, as kept by the Count, was dated the forty-ninth day of the Closing, in 2387 of the old reckoning. It was very short. It established the Account.

ACCOUNT ESTABLISHED. CREDITOR: CAUL. ALL BALANCES ON THE TALLY ASSIGNED TO CREDITOR IN FULL. ACCOUNT HOLDER: NONE.

And under it, where every other root entry he had seen carried the same small paired mark — the two authorities of the Founders, the twin keys of the root, side by side — there was something different. Not the paired mark. A single line of the Tally's own plain text, which he had never seen it use about anything:

AUTHORISED BY A KEY NOT KNOWN TO THE TALLY.

He read it several times. He thought about what _account holder: none_ meant, in a ledger. Every account had a holder. Somebody's chit, somebody's number. An account with no holder was money owed to nobody: a debt that could be paid into for ever and drawn from by no one.

He was still looking at it when the hall door opened and a bailiff in grey said, "Counter Sallow? The Comptroller-General asks for you. Now, please."

## The Long Window
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Orrin Sallow @@Imre Vosk
Items: ^The Count Key
When: same day
]

He had never been to the top of the Counting House. The Comptroller-General's gallery was a single long room with a single long window, curving, that looked straight down the length of Lantern along the Wick, and when Orrin came through the door the light hit him so hard he had to stop and put his hand up. Beyond the glass the whole world hung, lit and upside down: the Upper Wards bright, the Galleries dimming, the Wick blazing down the middle of the sky to where, two-thirds of the way along, it simply stopped, and beyond it the dark of the Cold lay like a bruise.

"Few people are prepared for it," said Imre Vosk. "Sit, Counter. Please."

He was at a desk at the far end of the room, with the window behind him so that it was hard to see his face. Orrin sat. There was a cup of tea in front of him, already poured. He did not touch it.

"You've been in the root," said Vosk.

"Yes, Comptroller-General. I'm fourth rank. It's open to—"

"It is open to you. Yes. I'm not reproaching you." Vosk's voice was as soft as it had been at the Hook; it was a voice you leaned toward without meaning to. "I'm interested in what took you there. Counters of your age don't usually go reading three hundred years of rate tables for pleasure."

"There's an error in an account on the Calling list. A surcharge keyed to the whole balance. I wanted to see whether the rule was at fault, or the keying. It was the keying. The rule's sound."

"Of course it is. The Founders wrote it." Vosk nodded. "And the account?"

"Hask. Agathe. Twenty-Two. Corrected, she's above the cut. I filed it. Senior Counter Abney refused it until after the lists."

"Abney was correct. We can't have corrections in flight during a Calling, Counter. You understand that. If every counter went into every account looking for reasons to lift it over the line, the line would mean nothing, and the Calling would fail, and then all of us would go into the dark together." He spread his hands. "It's a hard rule. It's the rule."

"She doesn't owe it," said Orrin. "Two thousand credits of it. It's not hers."

"Then it will be corrected afterwards, as Abney said."

"Afterwards she'll be in the Cold."

"Yes," said Vosk gently. "That's a pity." He let a moment pass. "You saw the first entry. In the root."

It was not a question. Orrin said, "Yes."

"What did you make of it?"

"I didn't— I don't know. _Account holder, none._ And the key. I've never seen the Tally say that."

"The Tally says what it was told to say, at the Founding, by people who had a great deal more to think about than how it would read to a counter three hundred years later." Vosk leaned forward a little, and the light from the window lay along his shoulder and the side of his face. "Let me tell you what the doctrine says, Counter Sallow, since you know it already. The Account is held by Caul. Caul holds itself. The Founders gave the Account to the dark that kept us, so that no living man could ever own it, and no Company could ever come back and claim it. That is what _holder, none_ means. That is why the key is not known. Because it was not a man's key. Do you understand?"

"Yes, Comptroller-General."

"The Count administers the Account. It does not own it. I hold the Count Key, which lets me keep the books, and I hold it as my predecessors did, in trust, for Caul. That's all. There's nothing hidden in the root, Counter. There's only the Founding, which is a holy thing, and which is not improved by being puzzled over by clever young men at dinner time." He sat back. "You've done good work on the Calling lists. Abney tells me you're the most careful counter in the third hall. I'd like you to go on being careful. Finish your four hundred. Sign your sightings. And then, when the Calling is done, refile your correction, and I will approve it myself."

Orrin looked at the tea he had not drunk. He thought of the room on Twenty-Two, and the tin with the farmhouse on the lid.

"Thank you, Comptroller-General," he said.

"Owing and owed, Counter."

"Owing and owed."

## The Salt Docks
[
Place: #The Salt Docks
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Wenna Blackwood
When: same day
]

The copper went for ninety at the Salt Docks, which was thirty more than the Count's foundry would have given and thirty less than it was worth, and Tamsin took it because she had to.

The fence was a man called Brannock who worked out of a dead hauler's cabin at the end of Spur Four, aft along the ring where the Count's bailiffs did not come without a reason. He weighed the copper on a beam scale older than the Closing and counted out the ninety in Salt tokens, which were brass washers stamped with a crude S and worth exactly what the Salt said they were worth and nothing anywhere else on the rock. Tamsin put them in the inside pocket of her rig. She would change them at a chit-shop on Nineteen that took Salt washers at a quarter off, and the quarter off would go to the Salt too, because everything on the Salt Docks ended up going to the Salt in the end.

"Somebody wants a word," said Brannock, not looking up from his scale.

"Who?"

He jerked his head toward the back of the cabin, where a curtain made of an old cargo-net hung over a doorway. Tamsin looked at it for a long moment. Then she went through.

The room behind was bigger than the cabin, cut back into the dock wall, and warm, warmer than any room Tamsin had been in all year. There was a stove with real coal-cake in it. There was a rug. There was a table with a lamp on it and a pot of something that smelled of meat. And at the table, eating out of the pot with a spoon, was a woman of sixty with one eye and a patch over the other, and grey hair cropped to a burr, and arms like a hauler's.

Everybody on Lantern knew who Wenna Blackwood was. Nobody below Fifteen had seen her and lived to boast of it. That was the story, anyway. Tamsin had always assumed it was the Salt's own story, put about by the Salt.

"Sit," said Wenna Blackwood. Her voice was a gravel chute. "Eat, if you want. It's goat. Real goat. Off the farms."

Tamsin sat. She didn't eat.

"You're Agathe Hask's girl," said Wenna. "Cutter. Good cutter, Brannock says. Clean metal, no lies about the weight." She chewed, swallowed. "And you've found a door."

Tamsin went cold.

"The Cold talks," said Wenna, not unkindly. "Not much. But I've got ears in it. Three cutters work the Fourteen run besides you, and one of them saw your light on the service way under the old pump bank, and saw where it stopped, and came and sold me that for a bottle." She put the spoon down. "Company door. Welded. Letters on it. That right?"

There was no point lying. "Aft Store 3."

Wenna nodded slowly, as if Tamsin had confirmed something she already knew. "Company stores," she said. "They had a dozen, aft, in the old days. Fuel and parts and medicine. They emptied them when they ran. Everybody knows that. Every one of them's been cut open and gutted three times over in three hundred years." She leaned forward. "Except that one. Never found it. Never even knew it was there. Know why?"

"No."

"Because somebody welded it shut and then iced over the way in, that's why. Somebody wanted it forgotten." Her one eye was very bright in the lamplight. "What's behind a door like that, cutter? Eh? What would the Company not take when it ran — and not want anybody else to have?"

"I don't know."

"No. Nor do I. But I'd pay to find out." Wenna Blackwood picked up her spoon again. "Half of whatever's in it. You cut the door, my people carry. I'll stand your tools and your air and your mother's tin besides. Think about it."

Tamsin stood up. Her legs were not quite steady; it was the warmth, she told herself, she wasn't used to it.

"My mother's going to be Called," she said. She didn't know why she said it. "Unless a counter in the Count does what he said he would."

Wenna looked up at her with no expression at all.

"Then you'd better think quick," she said.
