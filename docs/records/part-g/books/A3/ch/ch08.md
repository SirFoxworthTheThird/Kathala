## The Stylus
[
Place: #The Galleries
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Orrin Sallow @@Agathe Hask @@Jory Hask
When: +3 days
]

The counter came back three days later, which Tamsin had not expected, and he came in plain clothes, which she had expected even less.

She almost didn't know him. Without the grey coat he was only a thin young man with soft hands and a clean face and hair cut the way they cut it in the Upper Wards, short at the sides, and he stood in the doorway of the Hask room holding a cloth bag against his chest as if it might be taken off him, which on Twenty-Two it might.

"I didn't use the chit," he said, before anybody could speak. "On the door. I knocked. I wanted you to know that."

"Door was open," said Tamsin.

"I knocked anyway."

Agathe was asleep, or pretending to be; she slept a great deal now, propped on her bunk with her mouth a little open and her breath whistling. Jory was on the top bunk with his knees up, watching the counter with bright interested eyes. Tamsin was mending her rig's heater-line at the table with a soldering pen and a borrowed light.

"Well?" she said.

He came in and shut the door behind him, and stood there.

"The correction was refused," he said.

She put the soldering pen down.

"Not refused. Deferred. Until after the lists are posted." He was speaking quickly, looking at the floor. "The Count doesn't allow corrections during a Calling. In case— so that the line holds. I went to the Comptroller-General himself. He says he'll approve it, afterwards. He gave me his word."

"After the lists are posted," said Tamsin, "she'll be in the Cold."

"Yes."

"So what's his word worth? Down there?"

"I don't know," said Orrin Sallow. He looked up at her. "That's why I came."

She didn't understand him for a moment. Then she did, and something tightened in her chest that she did not let reach her face.

"You came to tell us," she said. "That's all. You came down twenty galleries in your second-best clothes to tell my mother she's going into the dark with two thousand credits on her that aren't hers, so you'd feel better about it."

"No. I came because it isn't right, and I can't make it right, and I didn't want you to hear it from a list." He held out the cloth bag. "And I brought food. It's not much. It's what my mother keeps in the house. Bread. Some cheese. Real cheese. I didn't know what you— I'm sorry."

Tamsin looked at the bag and did not take it. On the top bunk, Jory sat up.

"He can stay," Jory said. "If he's got cheese."

"Jory."

"I'm only saying." Her brother swung his legs down, dropped lightly to the floor, and held out his hand to the counter, palm up. In it was a stylus. A good one, Upper Wards make, with a silver clip. "Here. You dropped it."

Orrin looked at the stylus. Then at Jory. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a short, startled sound, as though it had been surprised out of him. "I didn't drop it."

"No," Jory agreed. "But you can have it back. For the cheese."

Orrin took the stylus. Tamsin took the bag. Nobody said anything for a while. On the bunk, Agathe Hask's breath went on whistling in and out, in and out, each breath posting, somewhere far above them in the Count's vault, to an account that was two thousand credits deeper than it should have been.

"Why does it matter to you?" Tamsin said at last. "Really. There's ten thousand on that list. You'll have four hundred of them on your slate. Some of them must be wrong too. Why us?"

He thought about it. She watched him think about it, and was annoyed to find she believed him when he answered.

"Because I checked yours," he said. "I haven't finished the others."

## Spur Four
[
Place: #The Salt Docks
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Wenna Blackwood @@Jory Hask
When: same day
]

She went to the Salt that night.

Wenna Blackwood was where she had been before, behind the cargo-net curtain at the back of Brannock's cabin, with the stove and the rug and a pot of something on the table. She looked up when Tamsin came in and did not seem surprised.

"Your counter came back," she said.

"How do you know that?"

"Because you're here." Wenna pushed out a chair with her boot. "Sit."

Tamsin sat. "Half," she said. "Of what's in the store. You carry. You stand my tools and my air."

"And your mother's tin."

"And my mother's tin. And something else."

The one eye considered her.

"If it's fuel," said Tamsin. "If there's cassettes in there. I want one. Not to sell. To give to the Wickwrights."

There was a pause. Then Wenna Blackwood laughed, a big rough sound like gravel going down a chute.

"To give to the Wickwrights," she said. "You hear that, Brannock? The girl wants to light the lamp." She wiped her eye. "Child. If there's a single live cassette behind that door — which there won't be, because the Company took every one it could carry and burnt the rest getting out — it's worth more than every soul on Twenty-Two put together. I'm not giving it to a guild of blind old men so the Count can burn it on the Upper Wards' tomato beds."

"Then I'm not cutting your door."

"It's not my door. It's not your door either." Wenna leaned back. "But all right. All right. If there's fuel, one's yours, to do as you like with. Burn it in your stove for all I care. Two's yours. And if there's not — and there won't be — you've still got half, and your tools, and a winter's air." She held out her hand across the table. It was huge and scarred and missing the top of one finger. "Done?"

Tamsin looked at the hand.

"There's a body," she said. "By the door. A woman. She's holding a shoe. Your people don't touch her. I move her. And they don't touch anything small."

Something changed in Wenna Blackwood's face. It was there and gone, too quick to name.

"Done," she said.

Tamsin took the hand.

Behind her, from the doorway, Jory said, "I'm coming."

She had not heard him follow her. She turned round, and he was there in the gap of the cargo-net curtain, all elbows, with his chin up.

"No," she said.

"I'm coming. I can carry. I'm good on a tether. You know I am. And if Mam goes into the Cold—" his voice cracked, and he pushed through it— "if Mam goes into the Cold I'm going to need to know the way."

## The Weld
[
Place: #The Cold Wards
Characters: @@Tamsin Hask @@Jory Hask
Items: ^Fuel Cassettes
When: +1 day
]

It took a day to get the lance in.

Wenna sent four cutters: Dobbs, a slab of a man who said nothing all day, and three others whose names Tamsin did not bother to learn, with sledges and a thermal lance and two tanks of oxygen and enough tether to rope off half the Cold. They went in at the Lip in the dead of third shift, when the Count's bailiffs were thinnest on the lower roads, and Tamsin led them by her old way through the back of Pump Gallery Fourteen and down the service way that ended at the door.

The woman was where Tamsin had left her, sitting with her back to the hatch and the shoe held against her chest. Dobbs looked at her and then at Tamsin and stood back. Tamsin knelt and put her arms round the dried, light, terrible body, and lifted her — she weighed less than a child — and carried her ten metres back along the service way and set her down against the wall in the same position, knees up, the shoe in her hands, facing the door. Jory watched. He did not say anything.

Then they lit the lance.

The weld was good. Tamsin had known it would be. Whoever had laid it had laid it as if they meant it to outlast them, a wide even bead all the way round the frame, and the steel under it was thick, old Company plate. The lance ate into it slowly, white and roaring, with the steel running red down the face of the door and freezing as it ran into long black icicles of slag. They took it in turns. It was hot, hard, choking work, in masks, in the cold, with the sweat freezing in their rigs, and it took them eleven hours. At the end of it Dobbs put a pry-bar the length of a man into the gap and leaned on it, and two of the others leaned with him, and the wheel at the centre of the door, which none of them had dared touch, turned on its own a quarter-circle with a groan like an animal, and the hatch of Aft Store 3 swung open into the dark.

The air that came out was colder than the Cold.

They stood back from it and let it settle. Then Tamsin put her brow-lamp up and went in.

It was a long room. Longer than she had imagined. A vault cut into the raw rock of the outer wall, forty metres deep, with a rounded roof, and down the length of it on both sides, in rows of three, racks: tall steel cradles with clamps and cables and coffin-shaped hollows in them, each hollow the size of a man. Tamsin had never seen a fuel cassette. Nobody below the Wickwrights had. But she knew what had stood in those hollows as soon as she saw them. There were a hundred of them. Two hundred. More.

They were all empty.

Dobbs came in behind her with a bigger lamp and swung it along the racks, slowly, all the way to the end. Empty. Every one. The clamps hung open. The cables lay coiled on the floor where somebody had unplugged them and dropped them. At the far end, on a pallet, there was a heap of what looked like spare clamps and a sealed medical locker with a red cross on it, frosted white.

"Well," said Dobbs, the only word he said all day. "There's your fuel."

Tamsin did not answer. She was looking at the wall beside the door.

There was a paper on it. An actual paper, a Company docket in a plastic sleeve, pinned to a board at eye height where somebody signing things out would see it. The plastic had gone yellow and brittle and the paper inside was brown, but the cold had kept it, and the print was clear. She did not read well. She read slowly, moving her lips, putting her light on each line.

TRANSFER DOCKET — AFT STORE 3.
CASSETTES 1–212 TO WICK STORE, SPINE ROAD. FOR THE LAMP.
STORE TO BE SEALED ON COMPLETION.

And a date she could not make out, and a stamp, the two tired letters, H and A, and under the stamp a signature. She could read that, just. It was small and neat and careful, the hand of somebody who signed a great many things and wanted each of them to be legible. _T. Basu._

"Two hundred and twelve," she said aloud. "For the lamp."

"So that's where the Wick's fuel came from," said one of the cutters. "Out of here. Three hundred years back. Huh."

"So that's where it went," said Tamsin. She did not know why she said it like that. She took the docket off the board, sleeve and all, and folded it carefully, and put it inside her rig.

It was Jory who found the other thing. He had gone along the back of the racks with his own light, the way he went everywhere, sideways and quick, and he had stopped at the end nearest the door, in a corner behind the last rack where somebody had heaped up a nest of old grey insulation blankets. He was standing very still.

"Tam," he said, in a voice she had not heard him use since he was small.

She went. She looked. Then she put her hand on his shoulder and turned him round, gently, and walked him back toward the door, and went back on her own.

They were very small bones. They were mostly hidden in the blankets, which was something. And on the rock wall above the nest, scratched in with something sharp — a buckle, maybe, or a spoon — at the height a child could reach, there was a column of short lines. Tally marks. Tamsin counted them because she could not help it. Eleven.

Under the eleven marks, in big uneven letters, the kind a child would make who had only lately learned them, there were five words.

SHE SAID SHE WOULD COME BACK.

Tamsin stood there for a long time. Then she went out of the store and along the service way to where she had set the woman down against the wall with the red shoe in her hands, facing the door that had been welded shut ten metres from her for three hundred years; and she knelt down in front of her and could not, for a while, see anything at all.

When she came back, Dobbs and the others had already begun to unbolt the empty racks for the steel. Nobody had touched the corner. Nobody looked at her.

"Leave the blankets," she said. "And the wall."

"We know," said Dobbs.

## The Light Ledger
[
Place: #The Counting House
Characters: @@Orrin Sallow
Items: ^The Tally
When: same day
]

Orrin Sallow had been told by the Comptroller-General himself to finish his four hundred, sign his sightings, and stop puzzling over the root. He finished his four hundred. He signed his sightings. Then he went back into the archive, because once a counter has seen a number that will not sit right he cannot stop seeing it, and because he had begun to understand, in the days since he had stood in the Hask room, that he was going to be ashamed of himself either way and would rather be ashamed of something he had done.

He did not go back into the root. He was not that brave, yet. He went into the light.

Every account on the Tally was charged for light, as for air and water and heat. Every counter knew the rate. What Orrin had never looked at, because no counter ever had any reason to look at it, was the other side of the light ledger: not what the Owing were charged for the Wick, but what the Wick was given. Fuel in, light out. The Wickwrights reported to the Count every quarter how much of the last cassette was spent, and the Count posted it, and the posting went into a ledger that nobody read.

He read it. He read three hundred years of it in two nights, sitting late in the empty third hall with the lamps turned down to save the light he was reading about.

It was very simple. At the Closing the Founders had held a stock of cassettes for the Wick. They had burned them, one at a time, through three centuries, shortening the lamp each time a cassette ran low, as the Count had always said. The stock was nearly gone. The last cassette was in the Wick now, and Ninian the Wickwright's quarterly report put it at a fifth remaining. That was the gutter. That was what the Comptroller-General had said at the Hook. All true.

And then, at the very bottom of the ledger, under the long column of spent cassettes, there was a second line that had not changed in three hundred years. A single entry, carried forward every quarter, posted on the Founders' authority in the first year after the Closing, and then never touched again.

RESERVE — HULL 7731. 14 UNITS. HELD.

Fourteen units.

He did not know what Hull 7731 was. He had never heard the words. They were not in the catechism, or the Account Day tours, or the rolls of the Count's property. He searched the archive for them and found them nowhere else at all.

But he knew what a unit was, in the light ledger. Every line above it said so. A unit was a cassette.

He sat in the dark hall for a long time, with the faint hum of the Tally coming up through the floor — or a pump, or the Hulk, or nothing — and thought about fourteen cassettes, held, for three hundred years, while the Wick was shortened and shortened over the Galleries and the Cold crept up from the aft end of the world. He thought about ten thousand accounts on a list. He thought about a woman on Twenty-Two asking him who Caul was.

Then he wrote _Hull 7731_ on the back of his hand, in ink, with the stylus that a boy had stolen from him and given back for cheese, and went home.
