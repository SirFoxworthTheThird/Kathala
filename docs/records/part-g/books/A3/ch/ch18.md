## Five Hundred
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Quennell Haste @@Mabry Ode @@Idris Kell @@Tol Brask
When: +2 days
]

"Five hundred," said Haste.

They were in the wardroom, the morning before the _Good Title_ cleared Nhai's limb: Haste at the head of the table with his slate, Kell at his shoulder, Ode at the far end with her arms folded and Tol behind her, and the ship's medic, Petrosyan, standing by the door as though she would rather be anywhere else. Corvin sat halfway down the table. Nobody had asked him to sit there. Nobody had asked him to speak, either, and he had not.

"We clear the limb in eighteen hours," Haste said. "We'll be at the rock in thirty. Whatever we find there — people or no people — we'll need hands. Enumeration takes bodies. Consolidation takes more. Sergeant Kell's people are very good, but there are twelve of them. I want five hundred of the stock awake and fit for work by the time we dock."

"You can't," said Petrosyan.

"I'm sorry?"

"You can't wake five hundred in thirty hours. Not safely." The medic's voice was flat and hoarse, as if she had been arguing in her head for some time. "The revival bay's built for fifty at a time. Six-hour cycles. Five hundred is ten cycles, sixty hours, and that's with nothing going wrong. If you push it — if you thaw them in batches of a hundred on fifty beds — you'll lose some. A lot, maybe. You saw what happened to the woman on Rack Forty-One."

"What sort of number?" said Haste.

Petrosyan stared at him.

"Of losses," said Haste patiently. "If we thaw in batches of a hundred. Roughly."

"I don't— ten in a hundred. Fifteen. I don't know. Nobody does it that way. It's not done."

Haste made a note on his slate. "Fifteen percent of five hundred is seventy-five. Out of three thousand and eleven. That's within tolerance for a recovery of this kind." He looked up. "Begin the first batch at noon, please."

"No," said Mabry Ode.

She had not moved. She stood at the end of the table with her arms folded and her face like a shut hatch, the way she had stood at the ship's lock at Ambry on the first day.

"Captain?"

"I said no. That's my medic, and that's my revival bay, and that's my hold, and those are people in it, not stock. Nobody's thawing anybody in batches of a hundred on my ship to save you a day's work at the other end." She unfolded her arms. "Tol's locked the hold power board. Master's codes only. If you want five hundred awake, Mr. Haste, you can wait till we dock and wake them fifty at a time like a decent man, and you can write in your book that the master wouldn't have it. That's my answer."

There was a short silence. Then Haste set his slate down on the table and smiled, quite kindly, the way he had smiled at Corvin in Hold 2.

"Captain Ode," he said. "I've been very patient."

"So've I."

"Yes. And I respect it. But this ship is mortgaged to Brightwater Recovery under an instrument that provides for possession on the master's default, and the master's refusal of a lawful order from the lienholder's agent in the course of a recovery voyage is a default. You know the law, I think. You've had it explained to you."

"By people like him," said Ode, and looked down the table at Corvin.

Corvin looked at his hands.

"Give Sergeant Kell the master's codes, please," said Haste.

"Go to hell."

Haste sighed. He did not look at Kell. He did not need to.

## The Master's Chair
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal @@Mabry Ode:dead @@Idris Kell @@Tol Brask @@Quennell Haste
When: same day
]

It happened on the bridge, an hour later, because Ode went there.

She walked out of the wardroom without another word and up the companionway to the bridge and sat down in the master's chair with her boots on the console, and told Dacey and the navigator to go below, and they would not go; and she was still sitting there, with Tol beside her at the engineer's station with his hand flat on the lock of the hold power board, when Kell came up the companionway with six of his people.

Corvin came up behind them. He did not know why. He told himself afterwards that he had come to stop it, and knew that was not true; he had come because he could not have stayed below and listened.

"Captain," said Kell. He was perfectly civil. "Mr. Haste asks for the master's codes."

"Mr. Haste can ask."

"I'd rather not take them, ma'am."

"Then don't."

Kell nodded slowly, as though she had confirmed something. Then he made a small movement with two fingers, and two of his people went past him to Tol at the engineer's station, and took him by the arms, and lifted him out of his seat — Tol did not fight; he went limp, like a sack, and they had to carry him — and pinned him against the bulkhead. A third went to the console beside him and began, with a slate and a cable, to work at the lock.

Ode stood up.

She did not shout. She simply stood up out of the master's chair and stepped across the bridge toward the woman at the lock, a gaunt grey old woman in a colourless coverall with nothing in her hands — and one of Kell's people, a young man, not much older than Dacey, who had been standing at the companionway with his slug-gun held across his chest the way they had been drilled, saw the master of the ship move fast toward a bailiff at the hold controls, and did what he had been drilled to do.

It was one shot. It was very loud on the small bridge. Mabry Ode stopped, and looked down at herself with an expression of annoyance, as if she had spilled something on her coverall; and then she sat down, quite carefully, on the deck, with her back against the base of the master's chair, and put her hand over the place.

"Oh, you stupid boy," she said, not to anyone in particular.

Then she said, "Tol."

Tol had stopped being limp. He was fighting the two who held him, silently, savagely, with his teeth bared, and getting nowhere.

"Tol," said Ode again. "Watch the Pellan scar. She grumbles on it. Watch it."

"I'll watch it," said Tol.

"Good lad." She leaned her head back against the chair. Her face had gone very grey. She looked up at the main screen, where Nhai's great banded limb filled half the view, ochre and purple and the hard bright line of the ring, and beyond the limb the black. "Thirty-four years," she said. "And I never once saw what was on the other side of a throat before anybody else did. Always somebody first. Always." She closed her eyes. "Damn."

She died there, against her own chair, before Petrosyan could get up the companionway. Nobody on the bridge moved. The young bailiff who had fired stood with his gun still raised, staring at her, and then slowly lowered it. Kell went and took it out of his hands, gently, the way you would take something from a child.

Then he turned to the woman at the lock. "Carry on," he said.

Corvin stood at the top of the companionway. He found he had his hand on the rail and could not take it off. Behind him, on the steps, Quennell Haste had come up without anyone hearing, in his dark coat, and stood looking at the master of the _Good Title_ dead on her own deck with an expression of grave, sincere regret.

"How very unfortunate," he said. "Sergeant, please log the master's death as a consequence of her resistance to lawful possession. Mr. Saal—" he turned— "a word, please. In my cabin."

## Concurrence
[

Items: ^The Mercy Bell
When: same day
]

There were three documents on Haste's desk. He laid them out side by side, squaring their edges with his fingertips, and turned them round to face Corvin.

"The first," he said, "is a declaration of default. The master of the _Good Title_ refused a lawful order of the lienholder's agent and resisted possession with force. That constitutes default under the ship's mortgage. Brightwater takes possession of the vessel. The second is the consolidation order: it authorises the enumeration of any persons found at the Sabra Concern's habitat and the merging of their accounts into Brightwater's book under the instrument. The third is the bell. The seizure docket, which you were good enough to concur with verbally the other night. I'd like your mark on it now, for the record."

"And if I don't?"

"Then none of them is valid," said Haste. "That's the point of an adjuster, Mr. Saal. A partner can order. Only an adjuster can certify that the order was lawful. Without your mark, the master's death is a killing. With it, it's a regrettable consequence of a default." He folded his hands. "You see why I brought you."

Corvin looked at the three slates.

"She wasn't armed," he said. "She didn't threaten anybody. She stood up."

"She moved toward a bailiff at a control panel during a lawful possession, in a manner the bailiff reasonably perceived as a threat." Haste said it gently, as if helping a slow child with a lesson. "You've certified that sentence before, I think. Or ones very like it. On Malory's Reach, if I remember the file."

Corvin did not answer.

"I'm not threatening you," Haste said. "I want to be very clear. You're not in any danger. Your discharge is in that drawer. Sign these and it's yours the day we're back through the Throat. Thirty-five years, Mr. Saal. Done." He paused. "Miss Adaire, on the other hand."

Corvin looked up.

"Her family's freedom from bond," Haste said, "dates from the payment of a debt by her great-grandmother, with the proceeds of a painting. I've had our people look into it, on the packet out. It's a little irregular. The painting, it seems, may not have been entirely hers to sell. If that were so — and a court might well find that it was so — the discharge would be void, and the original debt revived. With interest. Attaching to the issue." He let that sit. "I'd hate to see that. She's a remarkable young woman. But a consolidation is a consolidation. One reads everything across."

The cabin was very quiet. Through the bulkhead, faint, Corvin could hear the deep steady hum of the ship's great engine, and under it, from somewhere aft, the other note, lower, steady as breath, that he had heard on the first night out of the Throat, and had known since Hold 2 for three thousand people sleeping in the cold.

He picked up the stylus.

He signed the first slate. _Default certified. C. Saal, adjuster._ He signed the second. _Consolidation authorised._ He signed the third, under Haste's _Adjuster concurs (verbal)_, his full name and number. His hand was perfectly steady. He had signed four hundred and twelve settlements' worth of slates with that hand, and it had never once shaken, and it did not shake now.

"Thank you," said Haste, and gathered them up.

When Corvin came out of the cabin, Fen was standing in the corridor.

He did not know how long she had been there. Long enough. She had been confined to her quarters since the seizure, and someone had let her out, or she had let herself out, and she was standing against the opposite wall with her arms wrapped round herself as if she were cold, and she had heard. He could see that she had heard all of it.

"Fen—"

She did not say anything. She looked at him. Then she turned her head, very deliberately, and spat on the deck at his feet, the way Mabry Ode had spat into the gap between the ship and the dock at Ambry on the first day. And then she walked away down the corridor, and did not look back.

## The Crown
[
Place: #The Good Title
Characters: @@Corvin Saal
Items: ^The Mercy Bell
When: same day
]

He went to the bell that night because there was nowhere else he could bear to be.

Hold 1 was dark and cold. Kell's crate-corridors were still stacked across it from the drills, so that the bay was a maze of grey walls in the light of his lamp. The bell stood at the end of one of them, on its sled, strapped down under a tarpaulin with a Brightwater seal on it. He had an adjuster's access now. He had signed for it. The seal opened to his chit.

He folded back the tarpaulin and sat down on a crate beside the bell, with the lamp on the floor between his feet throwing its light up onto the bronze.

He had never been alone with it. It had always been Fen's: in its crate at Ambry, in the blister, under her hand, with her loupe and her lamp. He had only ever looked at it over her shoulder. Now he looked at it on his own. The brown furred surface, every inch of it letters. The worn old names round the lip — the crossing, thirty-one of them, in one careful hand. The colony's dead going up the waist, generation after generation, the hands changing. The dense grey band round the founders' belt, cramped and hurried, nine hundred and four of them, Abara to Zeller, that Fen had read aloud to him in the dark. The bright names high on the shoulder, the last ones, the newest. _Lemuel Adeyemi. Bronwen Price. Joss Teague._ He did not know who any of them were. He had read them so often he felt he should.

He lifted the lamp and looked at the crown.

He had never looked at the crown. Nobody did; it was the top of the bell, where the casting's decoration was thickest, a ring of raised bronze leaves round the shank where the hook had gone. Fen had said once that there were names up there too, crowded in among the leaves, and he had taken her word for it. He looked now. There were names, small, worn, squeezed between the leaves. And on one leaf, the largest, low on the crown's slope — cut very small, in a hand that was not like any of the others, a hand that shook, so that the letters wandered off their line and came back to it — there were words.

He had to put his face very close, with the lamp held up beside it, to read them.

THESE ARE OWED. SAY THEIR NAMES.

He sat back on the crate.

For a long time he did not do anything at all. He sat in the cold dark bay among the grey crates with the lamp on the floor and the bell in front of him, and the hum of the engine through the deck, and under it the other hum, the low note of the hold, three thousand and eleven people asleep with their names on cards. He thought about a dead man in a chair at the throat-watch, with his name on a strip. ROACH. He thought about Marisol Tey asking whether it attached. He thought about Mabry Ode against the base of her chair saying _damn_. He thought about a water-office on Kessane and a man whose face he could not remember signing his name in a shaking hand. He thought about a mother on Malory's Reach asking him whether he would like tea.

He thought about four hundred and twelve settlements, and that he could not, if his life depended on it, say the name of a single person on any of them.

_These are owed. Say their names._

He leaned forward and put the lamp close against the lip of the bell, where the oldest names were, the thirty-one of the crossing. They were so worn he could hardly make them out. He made them out anyway, one letter at a time, with his finger on the bronze the way Fen did it.

"Abel Corrigan," he said aloud. His voice sounded strange in the empty hold. "Mary Corrigan. Agnes Weil."

He went on. It was very slow. Some of the names he could not read at all, and he said what he could of them, a letter, a syllable, and went on to the next. The lamp grew hot in his hand. The hold was cold. On the bridge above him, he supposed, someone was flying the ship who was not Mabry Ode, and in the hold behind him the bailiffs were beginning, a hundred at a time on fifty beds, to wake the stock; and in eighteen hours the _Good Title_ would clear the limb of the great banded planet and come round into the light, and see whatever there was to see.

He did not think about any of that. He sat in the dark on a crate in a hold full of a dead company's property, with his hand on the bell, and read the dead aloud, one by one, to nobody, and did not stop.
