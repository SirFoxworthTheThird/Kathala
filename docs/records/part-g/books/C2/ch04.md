# Chapter 4: The Skirt

*The Combine · FY 2291*

"Again," Sol said.

Pell held the clip up in front of his visor where she could see it, and turned it in his glove so the work light caught the throat. His hands were steadier than they had been a week ago. They were still too slow.

"Spring," he said, and pressed the gate with his thumb and let it snap back. "Gate. Throat." He ran his thumb round the inside curve where the line's eye would sit. "No burr. No crack. No flat. Eye." He took the eye of his line in his other glove and bent it back on itself, hard, the way she had shown him, looking for the little white stress lines in the braid where it went into the swage. "No fray. No white. Seat." He clipped it to the hardpoint, and pulled, and leaned his whole weight back on it, which in the Ring's gravity, such as it was, was not much, but was enough to tell. "Holds."

"Again."

"Chief, that's nine times."

"Kostya checked his clip four hundred times. That's how many shifts he'd done. He checked it every shift, and it held four hundred times, and on four hundred and one the eye pulled through the throat. You know why?"

Pell was quiet.

"Because a Lindqvist clip isn't rated for side-loading," Sol said. "Because when you swing out on it, like he did, to ride a cassette in, the line comes off the clip at an angle, and the throat flexes, a hair, every time. Four hundred times. And you can't see it flex. And you can't see it in the throat when you look, because the crack starts on the inside of the steel. So checking it doesn't save you. You check it anyway. Because the day you don't check it is the day it'll be the burr, and not the crack." She pointed at him. "Again."

He did it again.

They were at the Spindle that week, all of Gang Four, fitting the governor's outer collar. It was the best work on the Ring and the worst. The best because the Spindle was the heart of the thing, and the work was fine, and Gang Four had been trusted with it because Gang Four was the best gang on the Ring and the Company knew it, even if it would have docked them a day's pay for saying so. The worst because the Spindle stood nine hundred meters off the Ring's rim, straight up, a tower of black steel and gold phase-lines narrowing toward its top like a needle, and to work on it you had to work in the open, on the outside, with nothing at your back but your line and nothing below you but the Ring's face nine hundred meters down and Kettle a long way under that.

Sol had worked the Spindle for three years. She had helped raise the last third of it. Dov had died on it.

"Okay," she said. "Clip in. We're going inside."

The governor lived in the Spindle's belly, two-thirds of the way up, in a cavity the size of a cathedral that the riggers called the Barn. You went in through a rigging hatch in the tower's flank, which was open, because nothing on the Spindle was closed yet, and then you were inside, and the dark swallowed your lamps, and you hung on your line in the middle of a space so big your lamps did not reach its walls.

Pell said, "Oh," very softly, as everybody did.

The governor ring filled the Barn. It was forty meters across, a ring within the Ring, standing on its edge like a great wheel: a collar of dark steel two meters thick, ringed all round its outer edge with teeth the size of a man, and laced on every surface with phase-lines finer and denser than anywhere else on the structure, so that when your lamps touched it the whole thing lit gold and then went dark again as the light moved on. It was not turning. It could not turn. It was held. All round it, at intervals, great steel arms reached in from the Barn's walls, and at the end of each arm was a fixed collar, a cuff of steel through which the governor's teeth passed, and through each cuff, driven in through a bore and seated deep in a notch between two teeth, there was a dog.

"Three for the dog," Sol said. "There they are."

Pell moved his lamp. The nearest dog was ten meters off: a steel pin as thick as a man's thigh and four meters long, driven through the bore of its collar with only its head standing out, a squared boss with a socket for the big drivers.

"That's what we're doing?" he said. "Dogs?"

"That's what we did. Last year. Now we're doing the outer collar. But you need to know the dogs." She pushed off and floated toward the nearest one and caught herself on the arm. He followed, clumsy, his line paying out behind him. "When the Ring's done and all the phase-lines are seated, the governor has to be free to move. To breathe, Haddad calls it. It doesn't turn round, not like a wheel. It shifts. Tiny amounts. Fractions of a degree, back and forth, all the time, and that's what holds the phase steady. If the governor can't breathe the throat can't open. So while we're building, we dog it. Lock it. So it doesn't shift under us while we're seating lines. Twelve dogs, twelve notches." She put her glove on the dog's head. "And before they light it, we pull them. All twelve. And it breathes."

"How long does that take?"

"Six hours, if nothing seizes. Four of us on the big drivers, three rounds of the Count on each one to back it out even, and then you walk it out of the bore on the crane." She turned, so her lamp went round the Barn and touched the other arms, one by one, going away into the dark. "Twelve notches. One. Two. Three—"

"That one's different."

She stopped. "What?"

"The third one." Pell's lamp was on it, across the Barn. "The notch. It's a different shape. See? The others are square-bottomed. That one's deeper. And there's no dog in it."

He was right. She had stopped noticing it years ago. The third arm reached in from the Barn's wall like the others, and its cuff gripped the governor's teeth like the others, but the cuff was bigger, much bigger, a great boxy mass of steel plating that wrapped not just the collar but a whole section of the governor ring and the arm behind it; and the bore through it was empty, and the notch in the governor's teeth that the bore lined up on, which you could see if you got close, was cut deeper than the rest, with a stepped profile, like a key-slot.

"Design change," she said. "Year before last. Originally there were thirteen notches—that was one of them, the deep one, it was for something. Haddad's people had it in the plans. Then the plans came back from Ostrava with that one struck through, and they didn't take the notch out because it was already cut, and they didn't dog it because they didn't need to, because they've got twelve. So it's a leftover."

"What was it for?"

"I'm a rigger, Okafor. I hang off things and bolt them together. Ask an engineer."

"What's the box?"

"The Inner Housing." She looked at it. "That's the most steel on the whole Ring, in one place. Four meters thick on the outside face. There's a chamber in the middle where the governor's sensors are, and the old instruments. You get in through a plate on the side." She remembered. "If Kettle flares when you're working the Spindle, and you can't get down to the platform in time, you go in there. Behind the steel. Everybody does. It's the only place on the Spindle that'll keep a flare off you."

"Have you been in it?"

"Twice." The second time had been with Dov. They had sat in the dark in there for six hours with their suit lamps off to save the cells, back to back, and Dov had told her the plot of every serial he had ever watched on the platform screens, at length, wrong, until she had threatened to vent his suit. Three months after that his clip went. "It's not much. It's a steel room."

Pell was looking at the deep notch with his lamp. His face, behind the visor, had the expression it got sometimes, which she was beginning to recognize: a farm boy's expression, a look at a thing that did not make sense yet but was going to.

"Chief," he said. "Why's it called a dog?"

"Because it holds on and doesn't let go," said Teague, on the gang channel, and coughed. "Come on, children. Collar's waiting."

* * *

They worked the collar for six hours and ran out of air margin at the end of it, the way they always did now, because the shifts had been extended by forty minutes that week without anyone extending the suits. Sol called the Count for the bolts and the gang answered, and on the third round Pell came in on his own number for the first time without being prompted—*seven the hand on the cold steel wheel*—in a cracked tenor, a little sharp, and Juno said, on the tug channel, "Oh, he *sings*," in a voice so delighted and so cruel that he did not sing again all shift.

Halfway through, a white suit came up the tower.

You could see it from a long way off, coming up from the Ring's face on a little Company scooter, the kind the engineers used: clean, white, lit up all over with running lights like a party. Bracken saw it first and said "Company," and everybody stopped talking, and then the white suit came round the tower's flank and hung there off the end of the collar, and through its visor, under the lights, there was a woman's face. Fifty, maybe. Narrow and dark, with deep lines bracketing her mouth, and very black eyebrows, and eyes that did not look at anybody but at the collar, the bolts, the seam of gold where the phase-lines met.

"Dr. Haddad," Sol said.

"Chief Dace." The voice was dry and accented, a Halvard voice, educated. "Carry on."

They carried on. Haddad watched. She did not say anything for a full round of the Count. Then, while the drivers were being reset, she said, on the gang channel, so they all heard: "The dogs. In the Barn."

"Yes, doctor."

"How long to pull them? All twelve."

"Six hours. If nothing's seized."

"Could you do it in four?"

Sol looked at her. Haddad was still not looking at anybody.

"With two gangs," Sol said slowly. "Four of us on the drivers and four on the crane. And if nothing's seized. And if you don't mind us maybe scarring a notch."

"I would mind that very much."

"Then six."

Haddad said nothing. Then she nodded, once, inside her helmet, and wrote something on the tablet strapped to her forearm.

"Why four?" Sol said.

Haddad looked up from the tablet. For the first time she looked at Sol, directly, through two visors and a meter of vacuum and the glare of her own running lights; and Sol, who had spent twelve years reading the faces of Company people for the thing behind them, saw something in this one and could not tell what it was. It was there and gone.

"Contingency planning," said Haddad. "Carry on, Chief." And the scooter turned, its little thrusters puffing white, and went back down the tower toward the Ring, small and bright and getting smaller.

"What was that?" said Juno.

"Don't know."

"*Contingency planning*," said Juno in Haddad's accent, very precisely. "God. When's first light, doctor? When the board says, chief."

"Don't," said Teague.

"What?"

"Just don't," he said, and coughed, and coughed, and went on coughing for a long time, so long that they all stopped work and waited, and Sol watched his air drop on her visor, four percent, five, until finally it stopped, and he said, thickly, "Sorry. Count it, chief."

* * *

The *Patience* took them home at shift end, all five of them crammed into her little crew bubble with their helmets off and their faces grey with tiredness, Juno flying. She was an old tug, older than the Ring; she had been a dock tug at Ostrava before the Company bought her for the yard, and she still had her Ostrava registry plate bolted to the bulkhead over the pilot's couch, brass, with her name on it in the old Combine lettering, *PATIENCE*, and under it her number and the date of her keel. Juno said she was the best tug on the Ring and also that she was held together by spit and prayer. Both were true. She pulled forty tonnes with arms that had been rebuilt so many times there was nothing original left in them but the bolts. She had never once let them down.

From the *Patience*'s bubble, coming in, you saw the platform the way you never saw it from inside: the whole of it, small and alone, hung off the Ring's rim on its long mooring arm. The dock module, the spine, the admin stack with its lit windows, and the hab rings—three of them, A and B and C, wheels of grey metal turning slowly on the spine, six hundred meters across—where four hundred and twelve bonded workers and forty-odd Company staff and twenty Proctors ate and slept and coughed and played cards and waited to go home.

And round the hab rings, the skirt.

It was meant to be a shield. For the Lighting. Sol knew this the way she knew most things about the Lighting, which was secondhand and in pieces. When the Ring was lit, first light would come out of it—the riggers did not know exactly what first light was, only that it was a kind of flare, a big one, bigger than Kettle's worst, and that it would come out of the throat when the throat opened, and that you did not want to be in front of it. The platform would be moved off to a safe distance for the Lighting, they had always been told. The hab rings would be shielded. The skirt was the shielding. It was a great cupped shell of plates—steel, and between the steel, tanks of water, and between the water, something Haddad's people called *the mesh*, a lattice of the same gold lines that laced the Ring—built in sections, on frames, that would close round the three hab rings like a hand closing round an egg. Gang Nine had been building it for two years.

It was half done. Sol had seen it every shift from the *Patience* coming home, and she saw it now, and she had seen it so often she did not see it at all; and then she did.

"Juno," she said. "Go round the skirt."

"I'm on approach."

"Go round it. Slow."

Juno looked at her and then took the *Patience* off approach without a word, and brought her round in a slow arc past the platform's flank, past the hab rings, along the line of the skirt.

There was nobody on it.

The frames stood out from the rings like the ribs of a half-built ship. Some of them were plated. Most of them were not. On the plated sections, the water tanks were in, and you could see the gold mesh glinting between them where the outer plates were not yet on; on the bare sections, there was nothing but the frame. Floating tethered all along the line, waiting, were the plates: hundreds of them, great curved sheets of steel four meters on a side, each one moored to a frame by a single line, turning very slowly on its line in the light. And there were no gangs. No work lights. No tugs. Gang Nine's work lights should have been on, all along the line, as they had been on every shift for two years, and they were off. The plates turned slowly in the dark like leaves on a dead tree.

"Where's Nine?" said Bracken.

Nobody knew.

"Take us in," Sol said.

* * *

She found Oyelaran in the galley at the end of third shift, eating alone at the end of a long table with his back to the wall. He was a broad dark man with grey in his beard, and he had been chief of Gang Nine for six years, and he and Sol had drunk together a few times and fought once, over a crane, and had a kind of respect for each other that neither of them would have called friendship. He saw her coming and did not look surprised.

"Dace," he said.

"Where's your gang?"

"Here. In the galley. Eating. Like me." He gestured at the long tables with his spoon. Gang Nine was there, she saw, at the next table along, eight of them, talking low. "We're on the cassettes now. Segment Three-Forty. Started this morning."

"What about the skirt?"

He put the spoon down.

"Suspended," he said. "Pending revised specification. That's the order. Came down from the Director's office through the Chief Engineer, yesterday, end of shift. Stop work on the skirt, stow tools, moor the plates, transfer to cassettes. Revised specification to follow."

"What revised specification?"

"It'll follow," said Oyelaran. "That's what follow means. It hasn't come yet." He picked up his spoon again and looked at it. "I asked. I asked the shift engineer and he said he didn't know. I asked to see the Chief Engineer and they said she was busy. I put in a request to see the Director." He ate a spoonful. "They gave me a day's dock for the request. Wasting Company time."

"How long to finish it? The skirt. If you went back tomorrow."

"Full gang? Two of us, three, if they gave us Gang Six too? Sixty days. Maybe fifty. It's not just plating. The mesh has to be tuned to the Ring's phase. That's Haddad's people." He looked at her. "Why?"

"Because the *Mercy* comes Day Three Hundred," said Sol. "And Lighting's after. And if the skirt's not finished by Lighting it doesn't matter, because we'll all be gone. Unless."

"Unless," Oyelaran agreed. He did not finish it either.

At the next table along, a woman from Gang Nine with a shaved head had turned round on her bench and was listening. Sol knew her by sight; Ruzicka, a cutter. She said, "You heard about the *Mercy*?"

"What about it?"

"She's late," said Ruzicka. "Clerk in the comms stack told my bunkmate. She was supposed to signal from the outer system relay on Day Two Forty, when she crossed the heliopause. She didn't. Nothing. Clerk said it's been seven days."

"Signals get lost," said Oyelaran.

"Seven days," said Ruzicka. "Out of a hauler. You ever known a hauler miss its relay signal by seven days?"

Nobody had.

* * *

Teague took her to the ballast hold that night.

She had known he would, sooner or later. She had known about the ballast hold for years: everybody did, the way everybody knew about the purge valve. The platform's ballast hold was down at the bottom of the spine, below the dock, a long low cavern of a space packed with crates of Gall ore that the Company kept for trim and never moved, and it was cold and dark and the Proctors' cameras had not worked down there since before Sol came aboard, because the Company had not wanted to pay for new ones; and so it was where people went, when people wanted to talk about things that could not be talked about anywhere else.

"I'm not joining anything," she said, on the ladder.

"I know."

"I've got fifty-three days."

"I know how many days you've got, Sol. I can count." He was ahead of her, going down slowly, a rung at a time, stopping to breathe. "I'm not asking you to join anything. I'm asking you to come and listen. You can listen and not join."

"That's how it starts."

"That's how everything starts," said Teague.

There were perhaps forty of them, in the end, sitting on the ore crates in the light of two work lamps turned down low. People she knew. Oyelaran was there, and Ruzicka, and men and women from Gangs Two and Six and Eleven, and two of the galley crew, and an old woman from the air plant called Mother Szell who was supposed to have been on the Ostrava docks in the strike of '64 alongside Teague, and who had a voice like a rusty hinge. Juno was there. Sol had not known Juno came. Juno saw her and lifted a hand and looked away. Bracken was not there, and Pell was not there, because Sol had told Pell that if she ever caught him in the ballast hold she would cut his line.

They talked. It was not a meeting, exactly. There was no one in charge, and nobody stood up. They sat on the crates and said things, one after another, in low voices, and the cold came up off the ore. The skirt. The *Mercy*. The forty extra minutes on every shift, and no extra air. Kostya. Somebody from Gang Two whose bunkmate's bond had been extended three months for an infraction nobody could name. The Lindqvist clips. Dov.

"Sixteen times," said Teague, and looked at Sol, and she looked back at him until he looked away.

"So what do we do?" somebody said. A young man from Gang Eleven, not much older than Pell. "If they're not finishing the skirt. If the *Mercy*'s late. What do we do about it?"

"We ask," said Mother Szell.

"Oyelaran asked. They docked him."

"Then we ask together," said the old woman. "All of us. All at once. One person asks, they dock him a day. Four hundred ask, they've got four hundred people not working, and a Ring to light."

"That's a strike," said the boy from Gang Eleven.

"It's asking," said Mother Szell, "loud."

"You know what they do to strikers," said Juno, from her crate, suddenly. Her voice was very precise. "Under the bond schedule. You know what the penalty is. Withdrawal of labor is breach of bond. They can extend your bond to life. They can transfer you. They can put you on a penal hulk at the Halvard yards and you'll never see daylight again, never, and they can do it to your family, too, if your family's on the bond, and mine is." She looked round at them. "I've got a son on Ostrava. He's nine. He's on my bond. If I break it they can take him."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"I'm not saying don't," said Juno, in a smaller voice. "I'm just saying."

Sol stood up.

They all looked at her. She had not meant them to. She had meant to stand up and go up the ladder and back to Bay Fourteen and cross off today's mark and go to sleep. She stood there on the cold ore crates in the low light with forty faces turned toward her, and she found that she did not know what to say, because she had not meant to say anything.

"I've got fifty-three days," she said, finally. "I'm sorry. I've got fifty-three days, and then the *Mercy*, and I'm out. And I'm not—I can't. I'm sorry about the skirt. I'm sorry about Kostya, and Dov, and everybody. But I can't. Not now. Not when I'm this close." She looked at Teague. "You said I could listen and not join."

"I did," said Teague.

"Then I've listened."

She went to the ladder. Nobody stopped her. She heard someone say something behind her, low, not kindly, and she heard Teague say, "Leave her be," and then she was on the ladder and climbing, and the cold of the hold fell away under her, and she was in the spine corridor, with its harsh white lights and its clean grey walls, alone.

She had gone about twenty steps along it toward the hab ring transfer when the speakers came on.

They did not come on often. Shift changes, and drills, and a flare warning when Kettle was acting up. They made a soft double chime first, two notes, high and low, the Company's chime, and then a voice, a woman's, recorded, calm and pleasant and not belonging to anybody on the platform. Sol had heard it so many times she did not hear it. She heard it now.

"Attention all personnel. Attention all personnel. This is a notice from the office of the Site Director.

"Following a phase instability in the Ring's primary governor, identified and certified by the Chief Phase Engineer, Doctor Y. Haddad, the Lighting of Transit Ring TTH-9 is advanced to Day Two Hundred and Eighty-Eight of the current fiscal year.

"All bonded personnel will continue in their assigned duties. Revised work schedules will be posted to your terminals.

"The Combine thanks you for your service."

The chime again, high and low. Then the speakers went off, and there was only the hum of the corridor lights, and the air plant breathing in the walls.

Sol stood in the middle of the spine corridor.

She did the sum without meaning to. It did itself. Day Two Hundred and Forty-Seven today. Lighting on Day Two Hundred and Eighty-Eight. Forty-one days. And the *Mercy* on Day Three Hundred, if the *Mercy* came at all, which would be twelve days after first light. Twelve days after.

The platform would be moved off to a safe distance for the Lighting. They had always been told that. The hab rings would be shielded. She thought of the plates on the dead skirt, turning slowly on their lines in the dark.

Behind her, at the bottom of the spine, she heard the ladder ring under somebody's feet, and then voices, a lot of voices, coming up out of the ballast hold. They had heard it too. The speakers went everywhere.

She did not turn round. She stood with her back to them in the white light, with her hands at her sides, and she found that her right hand had gone, by itself, to the thigh pocket of her liner, where she kept her scribe; and that she was holding it so hard, through the cloth, that the point had gone through and into her leg, and she could feel a small warm wetness there, spreading, and it did not hurt at all.
