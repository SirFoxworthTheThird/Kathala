# Chapter 15: Kettle-Weather

*The Dark · Year 419*

They gave me a suit on the morning we left Brine, and Ness fitted it.

I do not know how she arranged it. She would not tell me, afterward; she only said that walkers fit walkers, and that nobody who was not a walker would know the first thing about getting a Tally into a suit, and that Marrow had looked round the yard for someone small enough to get the inside seals done up without stepping on me, and there she was. So I stood in a room at the back of the Reeve's Hall with my arms out, in my shift, while forty walkers argued and swore and patched their own suits in the yard outside the door, and Ness knelt on the wet stone floor in front of me with a suit spread out round her like a skin, and did not look at my face.

"Arms in," she said.

I put my arms in. The suit was old and heavy and stiff and smelled of other people. It was too big. She folded the excess at the wrists and bound it with tape.

"Don't talk," she said, very low, with her head down at my wrist. "Marrow's at the door. Just listen."

I listened.

"The lung was a shell," said Ness.

I did not move.

"The one they rolled down the ramp," she said, binding the tape round my wrist, round and round, her fingers quick and hard. "The day you went. Veska had Barrow's boy open it up that night to put it on the loop. It's empty. The core's gone. The filters, the fans, the whole heart of it. Somebody took it all out and put the casing back on and sealed the intakes with cloth so nobody'd look. It's a box. It's a big grey box full of nothing." She moved to my other wrist. "Number Three died four days after. Like Quill said. The whole Hold's breathing the black. Hesta's girl went. And Barrow. And three more before I left. I don't know since."

I was looking at the wall over her head. It was grey stone, wet, with a long dark stain down it where the rain had come in.

"Veska sent me," said Ness. "When the Reeve's boat came round the Holds hiring walkers. She came to me and said, *go, and find her, and tell her.*" She tore the tape with her teeth. "She said to tell you she was wrong. About what a song's worth. She said to tell you that."

"There's no second lung," I said.

"There's no second lung. There's no first one either. Arms up."

I lifted my arms, and she pulled the suit's body up over my shoulders, and her face came up close to mine for a moment as she reached round behind my neck for the collar seal, and I saw that her eyes were wet, and that her jaw was set so hard the scar stood out on it like a cord.

"I'm sorry," she said, against my hair, where Marrow could not see. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry."

Then she stood back and said, loudly, "Collar's good. She's sealed," and Marrow at the door nodded, and Ness bent and began to pack her tools, and did not look at me again.

* * *

There were thirty-one of us on the *Patience* when she lifted off Brine. The Reeve. Marrow. Nine of the Reeve's soldiers. Eighteen walkers. Brother Cinder, in his chains. And me. They packed the walkers in the hold, aft, in the dark, among the cargo nets and the water tanks and the boxes of tools and charges and the sledges they would drag across the Halo, and they put me forward, in the cockpit, in the chair along the side wall under the brass plate that said COMBINE, and strapped me in.

The Reeve sat in the front chair beside Marrow. He did not look at me. He had not spoken to me since the phase-ear. I sat in my chair in my too-big suit, with my helmet in my lap, and looked at the back of his big grey head, and thought about a grey box full of nothing on the dock floor of Hold Fourteen, and the children in the dome, and Hesta's girl, and Barrow with his three missing fingers, and the knots I had not been there to tie.

I had never hated anyone before. I did not know what it was. I thought it would be hot. It was not. It was cold and slow and heavy, and it sat in the middle of me like a stone in a pocket, and it did not move.

We were two days out from Brine when Kettle flared.

I did not know what was happening. There was a sound first, from the front of the ship—a shrill rising whoop from somewhere in the bank of screens—and Marrow swore and hit something and the whoop stopped, and the Reeve leaned forward and looked at a screen, and said, "How long?"

"Forty minutes, maybe. Big one."

"Get us behind the Halo."

The *Patience* turned. I felt it in my stomach. Through the long front window Kettle swung across, huge, filling everything, banded purple and oxblood and yellow, and I saw what the screen had seen: on its face, low down near the limb, a place where the bands had gone white. Not white like a lamp. White like the inside of a burn. It was spreading. Sheets of lightning were running away from it across the cloud tops in every direction, very fast, faster than I had ever seen Kettle's lightning go, so fast that it looked like the white place was throwing them.

"Kettle-weather," said Brother Cinder, beside me. He had been put in the chair next to mine, with his chain looped through a ring in the wall. His voice was very quiet. "Have you seen it from outside before?"

"No."

"It's beautiful," he said. "Isn't it. Terrible and beautiful." His eyes were on the white place, and his face was full of light.

The Halo came up across the window. A wall of black metal, laced with dead gold, sliding across the stars and across Kettle's face until there was nothing in the window but the Halo, close, so close I could see the seams in its plates, and the pits and scars of four hundred years, and here and there, frozen to its face, things: pieces of old machines, a tangle of cable, a cluster of white shapes that might have been tanks or might have been something else. Marrow brought us in closer, and closer, until the *Patience* was sliding along the Halo's face a hundred meters off, in its shadow, with the whole bulk of it between us and Kettle.

"Hold here," said the Reeve. "Everybody behind the steel."

In the Holds, when Kettle flares, you go behind the steel: into the deep levels, under the old reactor shielding, where the walls are thick, and you sit in the dark and play cards and wait. On the *Patience* the steel was the hold, aft, where the water tanks were; the tanks and the cargo and the engines between you and the outside. So they unstrapped us and we went aft, all of us, the Reeve and Marrow and the soldiers and Cinder in his chains and me, down the ladder and through the hatch into the hold, where the eighteen walkers were already sitting in the dark among the cargo nets with their helmets in their laps, waiting.

Ness was there. I saw her at once, in the dim red light, sitting against a water tank with her knees drawn up. I did not go to her. I sat down where Marrow put me, against the forward bulkhead, between two soldiers, with Cinder on the far side of one of them. The hatch closed. The red light dimmed. Somebody began to deal cards.

The flare came twenty minutes later. You could not see it, behind the Halo, behind the hull, behind the tanks. But you could feel it. The ship's metal began to tick, all round us, a fast faint ticking like rain on a roof, and the red light flickered, and every suit in the hold that had a dosimeter on it began to chirp, softly, one after another, until the whole dark hold was full of little chirping sounds like crickets. Nobody spoke. The cards stopped. We sat and listened to the ship tick.

And then one of the walkers stood up.

He was a big man, bald, with a black beard. I had seen him in the yard in Brine; he had been one of the quiet ones, patching his suit alone by the wall. He stood up in the red dark among the cargo nets and took something out of the front of his suit, out from under the seal at his chest, where he must have had it all along; and held it up.

It was a lamp. A little oil lamp, brass, like the ones on the steps of the Lamplighters' chapel in the rain. It was not lit. He held it up over his head in the red light so everyone could see it, and he said, in a deep calm voice that carried all through the hold over the ticking: "The Light belongs to the Wick."

Then four more of them were on their feet. Walkers. Two women and two men, from different corners of the hold, each with something in their hands that was not a lamp. One of them had a gun. I did not know where she had got it. One of the Reeve's soldiers started to rise and she shot him, very fast, in the throat, and the sound in the hold was so loud that I did not hear it at all; I only saw the soldier go back against the bulkhead and slide down it with his hands at his neck, and then my ears were ringing and the hold was full of people moving.

I do not remember all of it. I remember the red light, and the ticking, and the chirping, and the shouting. I remember the bald man with the lamp walking through the middle of it toward the forward bulkhead, toward me, very calm, with his eyes on me and the lamp held up, saying something I could not hear. I remember Marrow coming up off the floor with a knife in her hand and putting it into one of the zealot women under the arm, where the suit's seals were, and the woman folding over it. I remember the gun going again. I remember a soldier falling across my legs.

And I remember Brother Cinder.

He was on his feet. The soldier beside him was dead or down, and the bald man with the lamp had come to him, and was bending over him with a key in his hand—a key, to the cuffs, I never knew where it came from—and Cinder's chain fell off his wrists and rattled on the deck. And the bald man held out the lamp to him. Held it out, in both hands, the way you would hold out a child. And said, in that deep calm voice, loud enough now that I heard it over the ringing in my ears: "Brother. Take it. Take the Singer. Take her to the Spindle. The Light is coming home and it belongs to the Wick."

Cinder looked at the lamp.

He stood there in the red light with his thin starved wrists bare for the first time since I had known him, rubbed raw where the cuffs had been, and looked at the little brass lamp in the bald man's hands. His face was full of light. It was the face he had had in the cockpit, watching Kettle's white place spreading. Terrible and beautiful.

Then he looked past the lamp, at me.

I do not know what he saw. I was sitting against the bulkhead with a dead soldier across my legs and blood on my hands from trying to get him off, and I suppose my face was what you would expect. Cinder looked at it for a long time. Long enough that the bald man said, "Brother?" and then, sharper, "*Brother*."

"It doesn't belong to anybody," said Brother Cinder.

And he put out his hand and took the lamp, gently, from the bald man's hands, and turned, and threw it as hard as he could across the hold; and as the bald man turned to watch it go, Cinder put his whole thin weight into him, sideways, and they both went over into the cargo nets in a tangle, and the bald man came up first, roaring, with his hands at Cinder's throat; and then Ness hit him from behind with a suit helmet, twice, and he did not come up again.

It was over very quickly after that. It had all been quick. Afterward Marrow told me the whole thing, from the bald man standing up to the last zealot dying, had taken less than two minutes. There were five of them, and three soldiers dead, and one walker, and the Reeve had been shot through the meat of his left arm and was sitting against a water tank with Marrow binding it, saying nothing, with his face grey. The red light flickered. The ship ticked. The dosimeters chirped.

And then the ticking stopped, and a new sound began.

It was not inside the hold. It was the ship. A long groaning whine, rising, from somewhere aft, through the bulkheads, through the deck, and a shudder under it, and then a jolt, hard, that threw everybody who was standing off their feet; and then another; and the *Patience* began, very slowly, to turn.

"The bridge," said Marrow, and was on her feet and at the hatch.

It was locked. From the other side.

"There were six," said Ness, on the floor where she had fallen, staring at the hatch. "In the yard. There were six who prayed. I counted them. There were *six*—"

The ship turned. Through the deck you could feel the engines, now, a growl rising to a howl. And the floor tilted under us, and pressed up, and the water tanks shifted in their nets with a groan, and we were moving. Fast. Faster than I had felt the *Patience* move before. And there was only one place, I understood, that anyone who believed what the bald man had believed would be flying her.

"They're taking her in," said the Reeve, from the floor against the water tank, very quietly. "To the Spindle. Straight in." He looked at Marrow. "Into the dogs."

* * *

Cinder opened the hatch.

He did it with the key. The bald man's key, to his cuffs, that he had picked up off the deck when everyone else was looking at the hatch; and he looked at it, and looked at the lock, and said, in his soft hoarse voice, "It's a Keepers' key. The Wick has kept them. They'll open anything on a ship this old," and he put it in the lock, and turned it, and the hatch opened.

Marrow went through first, with her knife, and two soldiers behind her, and I heard the shouting forward and then a shot and then nothing, and then Marrow's voice yelling for someone who could fly; and then the Reeve was up off the floor, somehow, with his arm bound to his chest, and gone through the hatch after them. I went too. Nobody stopped me. I went up the ladder and along the corridor and through the hatch into the cockpit and stood in the hatchway with my hands on its frame.

There was a dead man in the front chair, slumped against the straps. Marrow had cut him out of one side of them and was hauling him out of the chair by his collar. And through the long front window—

We were not behind the Halo any more. We were out from it, a kilometer out, two, and running along it fast, so fast that its face slid past below us in a blur of dead gold and black; and ahead of us, getting bigger, standing up off the Halo's rim against the stars and Kettle's turning face, was the Spindle. The needle. I had seen it from Brine and from the dome of Hold Fourteen all my life, small and sharp. It was not small now. It was a tower, black, laced with gold, going up out of the window's top, with something wrapped round its waist, two-thirds of the way up, like a belt, or a band of glass. And all round its foot, on the Halo's face, scattered across the dead metal for kilometers, things were moving.

I could not see them clearly. Small dark shapes, low and many-legged, like beetles on a plate. Dozens of them. Moving. Turning toward us.

"Dogs," said the Reeve, behind me.

The first one fired.

It was a light. That was all you could see: a thin bright line, red, from one of the beetles on the Halo's face, very fast, reaching up toward us; and it missed, and went past the window on the left, so close that its glare lit the cockpit red; and then there were three more lines, and four, and the cockpit was full of red light from every side, flickering, and the *Patience* bucked and screamed under us like an animal, and something behind us went *bang*.

Marrow was in the front chair. I do not know how she had got the dead man out of it so fast. She had her hands on the controls and the *Patience* was turning, pulling up and away, hard, so hard that I lost the hatch frame and fell back into the corridor and lay there pressed against the deck with a weight on my chest like a hand. Something else went *bang*, aft, and the lights went out and came back red. The engines' howl went ragged. The red lines were all round us.

And then they stopped.

We were out of range. I felt it before I knew it: the weight on my chest easing, and the screaming of the hull dropping to a groan, and then to a long ragged shudder, and the engines coughing. Marrow was shouting at the screens. The Reeve was in the second chair, his face grey, his good hand on something, shouting back. The *Patience* was going down. Not down to anything. Down toward the Halo's face, which was close below the window now, closer, a field of dead gold lines rushing up.

"Clamps," Marrow was saying. "Clamps, clamps, clamps—"

We hit the Halo.

I was on the floor of the corridor and I went along it, all the way to the aft hatch, and hit it, and the world went black and red and came back. The ship was screaming. Not the engines; the hull. A long grinding shriek of metal on metal, going on and on, as the *Patience* went along the Halo's face on her belly, and I could feel it through the deck, in my teeth, every seam and pit of the dead ring going under us. And then a deep heavy *clunk*, and another, and another, and the shriek stopped, and the ship jerked once, hard, and was still.

Everything was very quiet.

Somewhere aft, somebody was crying. Somewhere forward, the screens were chirping. The red light flickered and steadied. And under me, through the deck, through the hull, through the clamps that had bitten into the Halo's skin and held us there, faint and far off and perfectly clear, I felt it.

*Knock-knock knock-knock. Knock. Knock-knock.*

* * *

They counted us in the hold an hour later, in the red light. Twenty-two. Three soldiers dead in the fight and one walker and six zealots, and two more soldiers dead in the crash when a water tank tore out of its net and crushed them against the aft bulkhead. The Reeve with his arm. Marrow with a cut across her scalp that bled into her eyes until somebody tied it. Brother Cinder with the marks of the bald man's hands on his throat, black, sitting by himself against a cargo net with his raw wrists in his lap and his chain in a heap beside him, not chained to anything. Nobody had put it back on him. Nobody had told him he was free. I do not think anybody knew what to do with him.

Ness was alive. She had a cut on her cheek, and she had come and sat beside me in the dark without asking anybody's leave, and nobody had told her not to.

The Reeve stood in the middle of the hold. He had to hold onto a cargo net with his good hand to stay up. He told us what we were. We were on the Halo, he said, clamped on, thirty-one kilometers round the rim from the Spindle, just outside the dogs' reach. The *Patience* was not going to fly again without a dock. The drive was cracked. The air was good for twenty days, the water for longer. The radio still worked; the relay on her back still reached Brine, and Tithe, and every Hold that had a box like Veska's. He had already called Brine. A boat would come, in time, if one could be found that the dogs had not already killed.

"Or," said the Reeve, "we walk."

Nobody said anything.

"That's what we came to do," he said. "That's what I hired you for. That's what you were always going to do: land outside the dogs and walk in. We've landed outside the dogs." He looked round at us. "So now we walk. We've got the suits. We've got the sledges. We've got the Tally." He did not look at me. "It's thirty-one kilometers instead of twenty. That's all. The dogs don't see small and slow, mostly. We go small and slow."

Still nobody said anything.

I stood up.

I had not known I was going to. I stood up in my too-big suit in the red light, with Ness's hand falling away from my arm, and everyone turned and looked at me, and the Reeve of Brine turned too, at last, and looked at me for the first time since the phase-ear.

"There was no second lung," I said.

The hold was very quiet.

"You said you didn't lie about lungs," I said. "You said you'd buried too many people to lie about lungs. You said two. And you rolled one down the ramp at Hold Fourteen, and it was empty. It was a box. You took its heart out and sent the box." My voice was not loud. I did not need it to be loud. "Number Three died four days after I left. Hesta's girl. Barrow. Three more, before Ness came away. And how many since, I don't know. Because I'm not there. Because I'm here. Because I came with you for a lung that was a box."

The Reeve of Brine looked at me for a long time. Marrow had gone very still beside him.

"Yes," he said.

He did not say anything else for a while. He stood in the red light holding the cargo net with his good hand and his bad arm bound to his chest, and his face did not move.

"There was one whole lung in Brine," he said at last. "One. The one we dug out of the Tenth, four months under the water, in the dark. One. And I had a city of eleven thousand people under a roof that's falling in, breathing the black. And children. And I had a Hold of eighty in Tithe with a Tally I needed." He breathed. "I couldn't give it to you. I'd have had to take it out of a children's ward on the Third tier and put it on a boat. I couldn't. So I sent you the box." He looked at me steadily. "And I told myself that when the Light came back there'd be lungs for everyone. In Brine and in Tithe and in every Hold. That the only lung that mattered was the Halo. That if I could get you to the Spindle, nobody would ever need to choose again."

"You told yourself."

"I told myself," the Reeve agreed. "I'm still telling myself. I think it's true." He let go of the cargo net, and stood on his own, swaying. "You can hate me, Tally. I'd hate me. But it doesn't change anything. They're still breathing the black in Fourteen. The only thing that changes it is up there." He lifted his chin toward the forward bulkhead, toward the Spindle, thirty-one kilometers round the rim. "And whether you sing."

I looked at him. The stone in the middle of me did not move.

Through my boots, through the deck, through the clamps and the Halo's skin, I could feel it. *Knock-knock knock-knock.* A run and a stop. It was louder here than it had ever been in the phase-ear under the ice. It was in the soles of my feet and the bones of my legs and the floor of my belly, like a second heart, like something counting. I did not know what it was. I knew that I knew. And I knew one more thing, standing there in the red light in the dark hold of the oldest ship in the Reach with the Reeve of Brine looking at me: that I was going to go to the Spindle. Not for him. Not for the lung he had not sent, or the Light he wanted to hold in his hand. For the knock. Because something on the other side of the door was counting, and had been counting for a hundred years, and getting louder, and I was the only one left in the world who might know what it was counting.

"I'll walk," I said.

The Reeve looked at me for a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, and turned away.

"We go at the next bell," he said to the hold. "Suit up."

Beside me, on the floor, Ness let out a long breath, and got up, and put her shoulder against my shoulder, hard, the way she had under the dome; and on the far side of the hold, sitting alone against the cargo net with his raw wrists in his lap and his chain on the floor beside him, Brother Cinder lifted his head and looked at me, and his eyes in the red light were wet and very bright, and he did not say anything at all.
