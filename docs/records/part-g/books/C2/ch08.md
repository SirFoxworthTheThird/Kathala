# Chapter 8: Substitution

*The Concord · 336 AL*

The Register desks of the Third Spoke lane were in the Spindle's lower gallery, a long low room cut into the tower's flank two hundred meters above the rim, where the Keepers on the Register rota sat in a row behind a sloping counter of brass and glass and watched the ships come and go. There were six desks. Each had its own bank of gauges, and its own great book, open on a lectern of black Brine wood, and its own lamp; and each faced a window, a tall narrow slot of armored glass through which the Keeper could see his lane, a lit corridor of beacons running down from the Spindle's shoulder into the Eye.

Idris had sat at the third desk two days in seven for nine years. He knew it the way he knew his own rooms. He knew the scratch on the brass by his left hand where some Keeper before him had dropped a stylus, and the slight sticky drag of the mass-balance dial's bezel, and the smell of the lamp oil, and the sound the book's pages made when he turned them, a dry whisper like a breath. He knew the lane's beacons by their colors, and the governor's voice in every weather, and he could feel a ship arriving in his teeth before it broke the surface.

He felt the *Clemency* coming a full minute before she surfaced, and the hair stood up on his arms.

It was the fourth bell of second watch, the dead hour, when the lanes were quiet. The novice at the second desk, a long-necked boy from the Sump named Tobiah who had been with the Keepers six months and was still afraid of the gauges, was copying out a phase table by lamplight and did not look up. The Eye was breathing slow and even, far below the window, its color at the center deepening toward that violet-black that had no name. And under the floor, under the brass, under Idris's feet, the Spindle's voice began to change.

A ship coming through the Eye was a weight arriving. You felt it in the governor the way you felt a man step onto the far end of a plank you were standing on: the faintest shift, a settling, as the Eye took the ship's mass from its sister on the far side and the governor breathed to hold the phase. An ore barge, forty thousand tonnes, was a step. A House courier was a cat landing. Idris had felt big ships, too, in nine years: the colony haulers that came through twice a year from the Inward with their holds full of settlers bound for Brine and Gall, a hundred and fifty thousand tonnes, that made the gallery's lamps tremble on their chains.

This was not a step. This was the whole plank going down.

The mass-balance dial began to move. He watched it. It went past the forty-thousand mark without slowing, past the hundred, past the hundred and fifty where the colony haulers stopped. The lamp on its chain above his desk began to swing, very slightly. At the second desk Tobiah looked up from his phase table with his mouth open.

"Keeper—"

"I know."

The dial went past two hundred thousand. Two hundred and fifty. The governor's voice was a deep hum now, in the floor and the walls and his teeth, a note he had never heard it make. The Eye, far below the window, swelled; its color at the center went darker, and the blue-white at its rim brightened, and the slow breathing of it caught, for a moment, the way a man's breath catches when he lifts something heavy.

Then the *Clemency* surfaced.

She came up out of the Eye's center slow and black and very long, and as she cleared the light he saw her shape, and she was a freighter. That was what she looked like. A big one, ungainly, with a blunt nose and a long hull of cargo frames and a cluster of engines at her stern, painted in a dull House grey with nothing on her flank but a registry number and her name in white. She looked like a hundred freighters he had watched come through his lane. Except that she rode deep, so deep in the light that she seemed to drag it up with her as she rose, a long bright wake of it peeling off her hull; and except for the dial, which had stopped, finally, trembling, at three hundred and eighty-eight thousand tonnes.

He sat and looked at it. The lamp swung above him on its chain and slowed and was still.

Three hundred and eighty-eight thousand tonnes was not a freighter. A freighter of that size, loaded to her frames with Gall ore, would come through at a hundred and twenty. Three hundred and eighty-eight was ten freighters. It was a hull of solid armor plate and engines and reactors and something else besides, something very heavy, packed into a frame built to look as though it held nothing at all.

"Keeper Vey," said Tobiah, in a small voice. "That's—"

"The dial stuck," said Idris.

He did not know he was going to say it until it was out. He heard his own voice say it, quite calmly, and turned on his stool and looked at the boy, and saw the boy's long earnest face, and his eyes going from Idris to the dial and back.

"It stuck," Idris said. "On the bezel. It does that. You've seen it. It drags." He reached out and put his finger on the dial's bezel, the bezel he knew so well, the one with the slight sticky drag, and turned it a little, and the needle jumped and swung and settled at zero. "It hangs up when a big one comes through. Then you have to knock it. Old brass." He made himself smile. "You'll learn. I'll show you how to knock it."

Tobiah looked at the dial. He looked at Idris. He was sixteen, and from the Sump, and he had been a Keeper six months; and Idris was a Keeper-Adjunct with nine years on the Register and a white hook on his breast and his father's line round his neck. Idris watched the boy decide to believe him. It took about a breath.

"Yes, Keeper," said Tobiah, and went back to his phase table.

Idris turned to the book.

It lay open on its lectern in the lamplight, the right-hand page half full, in his own small square hand. *Second watch, fourth bell. Lane Three. Inbound.* He dipped the stylus. He had written ten thousand entries in this book. He wrote the name. *Clemency.* He wrote the registry number, from the slate the lane-beacon had sent up. He wrote *Ore barge, returning, empty.* And then he wrote the mass.

*40,000 t.*

He looked at it for a while. It looked like every other entry on the page. Then he took the seal from its box on the desk, and the stick of grey wax, and held the wax to the lamp until it dripped, and let three drops fall on the page beside the entry, and pressed the seal into them, the hook, and lifted it, and initialed beneath. *I.V.*

His hand did not shake. He watched it not shake. Of everything he did that night, that was what he remembered afterward, for the rest of his life: that he had watched his own hand write a lie into the oldest book in the Reach, and seal it, and initial it, and that his hand had been perfectly steady.

* * *

The reconciliation came through two days later.

He was not there for it. Oake had said he need not be. A Guard came to the rooms on the forty-first level in the middle of the afternoon with a slate and a sealed paper, while Idris was at the Spindle and Lark was at home, and read the paper aloud in the doorway, and then took a short sharp knife from his belt and cut the red cord from her wrist, and went away. Lark told him this in the evening, flatly, when he came home and found her sitting at the table with the cut cord in front of her on the wood, a little red loop of silk, frayed at the ends.

"He said there'd been an error," she said. "In the Drum. Two disks stuck together. Mine and somebody else's. The wrong one was read out." She did not look up from the cord. "He said the Levy Office apologizes for the inconvenience."

"Lark—"

"Who was it?"

He stood in the doorway in his habit. He had thought about this moment for two days and he had not thought of what he would say.

"I don't know," he said.

It was true. He did not know. Oake had told him not to concern himself with the list, and he had not, and he had not asked, and he had told himself that not asking was the same as not knowing, and he had told himself that so often in two days that it had worn smooth.

Lark looked up at him.

"You don't know," she said.

"No."

"You went to the Levy Office. And you did something. And you don't know what." She picked up the cord and turned it in her fingers. "You know it's two thousand. Everybody knows it's two thousand. You know that if it's not me it's somebody else. And you didn't ask who."

"It was an error. In the Drum—"

"*Don't*." She stood up so fast her chair went over behind her. "Don't you dare say that to me. Don't you *dare* say it was the Drum." She held the cord up between them. "Who was it, Idris? Who did you give them?"

He did not answer. He could not.

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she put the red cord in her pocket and went past him, out of the door, and down the stairs, and did not come back.

He waited until the middle of the night. Then he went down after her.

He knew the way now. The service stair with the rusted gate, the door with NO ACCESS—SUMP and the older letters beneath, the curving floors and low ceilings and the paint flaking off the old grey walls in strata. He went quickly, with his head down and his hands in his sleeves and the hood of his habit up, which did not help. People still looked. He went to Mother Rook's, at the end of the short dead-end corridor, and knocked, and the old woman's gravel voice said "Come," and he went in, ducking under the pipes, and she was sitting in her chair in her shawls, exactly as she had been, as though she had not moved in thirteen days.

"She's not here," Rook said, before he could ask.

"Where is she?"

The black eyes looked at him from their folds for a long time.

"Fourteen," said Mother Rook. "Go out, left, down the curve, past the button stall, second stair down, first door on the right. The one with the writing over it." She paused. "Knock."

* * *

The door had writing over it.

Old letters, stenciled in the yellow gone brown, on the grey under the flaking paint: *C-14*. And under them, smaller, scratched into the wall by hand, so small that he had to lift his lamp to read them, two more words in the same old square letters. *ABANDON HOPE.*

He stood looking at them for a moment. Then he knocked.

The door opened a hand's width. A face looked out at him, a young man's, hard and narrow, with a shaved head and a scar through one eyebrow. It looked at the habit, and at Idris's face, and its expression did not change at all.

"She's here," Idris said. "Lark Vey. My sister."

The face looked at him a moment longer. Then it turned and said something over its shoulder into the room, low, and listened, and turned back, and the door opened.

It was a small room. Four long steps by six. It had once been something—you could see the brackets on the long walls, three high, where shelves had been, or beds—and now it was bare, and full of people. Fifteen, perhaps, crammed in, sitting on the floor and on crates and on each other, young, most of them, Lark's age or a little older, with lamps on the floor among them throwing their shadows huge up the walls; and they had all stopped talking when the door opened, and they were all looking at him. There was a round port at the far end, so scratched on the outside that it was milk-white. And on the wall by the port, at the height of a sitting man's head, there were marks.

Lark was sitting under them, on the floor, with her back against the wall and her knees drawn up and the red cord in her hand.

"Let him in, Teodor," she said. "He's my brother. He's harmless."

"He's a Keeper," said the young man with the scar.

"He's harmless," said Lark. "Aren't you, Idris? Completely harmless." She looked up at him across the room. Her eyes were red and dry. "Come in. Shut the door. Since you're here. Since you can't keep away."

He came in. The door shut behind him. Fifteen faces watched him pick his way between them to the far wall, and he did not know any of them, and they all knew him. He could see it. They knew who he was and what he was and, he thought, what he had done.

He stood in front of Lark.

"Benno Kask," she said.

He did not understand at first.

"That's the name." She did not get up. "Benno Kask. Ward Nine of the Sump. Sixteen. He sells krill cakes at the foot of the Nine stair. Fried, with salt. They're good. His mother sits with him and does the money because she can't stand at a stall any more, because she's only got one leg, because she lost the other one in a vat accident on the Fourth Spoke when he was four. His father's dead. His sister's dead. There's just him." She looked at the cord in her hand. "His name was on the reserve. Did you know there was a reserve? I didn't. Teodor did. Teodor knows everything." She looked up. "You know how you get on the reserve? You get on it if you lost your ward-number. If your family was Levy-dodgers, once, two generations back. If you're Sump. If you owe the Office money. If somebody up in the Spokes didn't like your face. Nobody knows they're on it. You just are. And then when somebody's brother goes to the Office and pays, they reach into the reserve and pull out a name, and a Guard goes down the Nine stair and ties a cord on a boy selling krill cakes." She held up the cut cord. "This was on my wrist two days ago. It's on his now."

Idris could not speak.

"What did it cost?" said Lark. "What did you pay? You haven't got any money."

"It doesn't matter."

"It matters to me."

"A number," Idris said. "In a book."

Lark looked at him. She did not understand, he could see. He did not want her to. Then something moved in her face, and he saw that she did, or part of it, enough.

"I'm going to put it back," she said. "I'm going to the Office in the morning, and I'm going to tell them there was another error, and put my name back, and take his out."

"They won't," Idris said. "It's sealed. A reconciliation's sealed. They can't undo it, even if they wanted to, which they don't. If you go in there and say there was a false reconciliation they'll arrest Oake and they'll arrest me and they'll put your name back in the Drum for the rest of your life, and his name will stay where it is. That's all that will happen. You can't put it back, Lark. Nobody can."

She looked at him for a long time.

Then she bent her head and put her face in her knees, and her shoulders began to shake; and he understood after a moment that she was crying, without any sound at all, the way she had cried at six, in the black smock, at the pyre, when she would not hold his hand.

He knelt down in front of her. He did not touch her. He did not think she would let him.

The room was silent around them. Fifteen people, and none of them moved. On the wall above Lark's bent head, in the light of the lamps on the floor, the marks went up in their rows, hundreds of them, thin lines scratched into the old grey with a point, very neat, in blocks; and some had a line through them, and most had, and down at the bottom, smaller, finer, a second set, in rows of ten.

"What are they?" he said. He did not know why he asked. Because he could not ask anything else.

It was the young man with the scar, Teodor, who answered, from behind him.

"Nobody knows," he said. "They've always been here. Sump kids touch them for luck. Before something hard. Before Levy Day." His voice was flat and cold. "The old ones say somebody was counting the days they owed. Somebody who lived in this room. Counting down. The ones with lines through are the days they paid." He paused. "You can see they didn't finish."

Idris looked at the bottom rows. Some of the small marks had lines through them. Some did not.

Lark lifted her head. Her face was wet. She looked at him, kneeling in front of her in his grey habit with the hook on his breast and his father's line round his neck, and then she reached up behind her, without looking, and put her fingertips on the marks on the wall, the way you would put your hand on someone's shoulder.

"Go home, Idris," she said.

* * *

He did not go home.

He went to the foot of the Nine stair in the Sump, in the early morning, when the stalls were opening and the lamps were being lit along the curving corridor and the smell of hot oil and frying was coming up off the hot plates. He did not know why he went. He had not decided to. He found his feet had taken him there, and he stood at the foot of the stair with his hood up and his hands in his sleeves, and looked.

There were four stalls at the foot of the Nine stair. One sold wire. One sold the grey stuff in bowls. One sold nothing he could identify. The fourth was a sheet of old hull plate on two crates, with a hot plate on it and a pan of oil on the hot plate, spitting; and behind it, on a stool, a woman with one leg and a crutch leaning against the wall beside her, counting coins into a tin; and in front of it, turning cakes in the oil with a long fork, a boy.

He was small for sixteen. Thin, quick, with a mop of black hair and big ears and a grin that took up most of his face, which he was turning on an old man in front of the stall, haggling over a cake. He had a red cord on his left wrist. Idris could see it from the stair, bright against his skin. He did not seem to be ashamed of it. He had pushed his sleeve up past the elbow so that it showed.

Idris went to the stall.

"Krill cake, Keeper?" said the boy. He had a quick light voice. "Two bits. Fresh. Salt's extra. Salt's worth it."

"With salt," said Idris.

The boy forked one out of the oil and shook it and dropped it on a square of old paper and sprinkled salt on it out of a twist of cloth and handed it over, all in one movement, grinning. Idris gave him three bits. The boy looked at the third bit, and then at Idris, and then shrugged and pocketed it.

"Don't see many Keepers down the Nine," he said.

"No."

"You lost?"

"A little."

"Where you headed? I'll tell you the way. No charge." The grin. "Well. One bit."

Idris did not answer. He stood with the hot cake in his hand on its square of paper, grease soaking through to his fingers, and looked at the red cord on the boy's wrist.

The boy saw him looking. He looked down at it himself, and the grin changed, a little, went sideways.

"Got called," he said. "Two days ago. Guard came right down the stair. Funny, that—they said there was a mix-up in the Drum, somebody else's name got read out by mistake, and it was mine all along." He shrugged. "So. Firstlight, I go."

"I'm sorry," said Idris.

"What for? Wasn't you that read it." The boy turned a cake. "Ma's took it hard. Haven't you, Ma?" The woman on the stool did not look up from her coins. "But it's not so bad. I've been thinking. They say if you go in the Levy and you're good with machines, they put you on the ships. Inward. On the big ships, the House ships." His eyes went bright. "I'm good with machines. I fixed this plate. I fixed half the stalls on the Nine. I could fly. I always wanted to fly. Nobody flies from the Sump. You've got to be Spoke-born to get on a ship, or have money, and I never had either. But in the Levy they don't care where you're from, they say. They just want hands."

"Who says?"

"Everybody." The boy's grin came back. "So maybe it's lucky. Maybe I'll fly a House ship back through the Eye one day and you'll be up there in your tower, Keeper, writing me down in your book." He laughed. "Benno Kask, pilot. You write that. Big letters."

Idris stood with the cake in his hand.

He could have told him. He thought about it. He stood at the foot of the Nine stair with the grease soaking through the paper into his fingers and the boy grinning at him over the pan, and he thought about saying: *It was my sister. It was my sister's name that was read out, and it was the right name, and I paid to have it changed, and they reached into a list and pulled out yours.* He thought about what the boy's face would do.

"I'll write it," he said. "Big letters."

"Good man." Benno turned another cake. "Eat that while it's hot, Keeper. It's no good cold."

Idris ate it. It was very good. He ate the whole thing standing there at the foot of the stair, and then he thanked the boy and nodded to the woman on the stool, who did not look up, and went away up the curving corridor toward the service stair, and was sick, quietly, against the old grey wall at the first bend, where nobody could see.

* * *

He did not sleep that night either.

At the twelfth bell he gave up trying and got up and dressed and went out. He did not go down. He went up, through the Third Spoke's empty galleries, past the dark chapels, to the Keepers' high walk: a narrow path along the top of the Spoke, under the outer hull, that the Keepers used to inspect the phase-taps where the city drew its power off the Ring. It was cold up there. It was always cold. You walked on a grating of iron with the dark metal of the hull a meter over your head, and every forty paces there was a window in the hull, a small round port of thick glass, and through it you could see out.

He walked it end to end, past the phase-taps humming in their housings, past window after window. Out, below and beyond, the city: the other Spokes, the towers, the lights. The Eye, breathing. He did not look at the Eye. He looked at the city. It was very beautiful at night. He had always thought so.

At the end of the walk, where the Third Spoke met the Hub, the last window looked down over the upper wards; and below the upper wards, at the edge of the Hub, on the Ring-side, the Factor's private berth.

It was a dock. A big one, cut deep into the Hub's flank, with its own lock and its own lights and its own wall round it, where House ships tied up when they came through, couriers and the Factor's own yacht; and it was not usually lit at this hour. It was lit now. Floodlights, white, very bright, shining down into the berth from its walls.

There was a ship in the berth. A long dull grey freighter with a blunt nose and a registry number on her flank and her name in white.

He stood at the window and watched.

The *Clemency*'s cargo doors were open. All of them, all along her flank, great square hatches swung back on their hinges like the lids of boxes. And out of them, down ramps onto the berth's floor, in the white light, figures were walking.

They walked in lines. That was the first thing he saw. Not in a crowd, as passengers walk off a hauler, or dockers off a shift. In lines, four abreast, evenly spaced, at the same pace, as though a single foot were coming down for all of them. They were in armor. Not the white-and-gold half-armor of the Wardens' Guard, which was for show; full armor, dark, faceless, helmeted, with long weapons slung across their backs. They came down the ramps out of the *Clemency*'s holds in lines of four and crossed the berth's floor and went into the Hub through a great door at the berth's far end that he had never seen open, and the door swallowed them, and more came.

He watched for a long time. He tried to count them. He lost count at six hundred. The lines did not stop.

Down at the berth's edge, under the floodlights, a small group stood watching too. He could just make them out. Three or four figures, not in armor. One of them in black, tall and narrow. One in white and gold, round and small, with two smaller figures behind her holding up the hem of something.

Idris put his hand on the cold glass of the window.

*Ore barge, returning,* he had written, *empty.*

The lines came on. Four abreast, out of the light, down into the city.
