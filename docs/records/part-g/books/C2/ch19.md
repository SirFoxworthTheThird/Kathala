# Chapter 19: Lock the Door

*The Combine · FY 2291*

Dov's letter was in the bottom of Teague's kit bag, under a spare liner and a pair of socks that had been darned so many times they were more darn than sock, in a Company envelope gone soft at the corners. Sol found it on the night of Day Two Hundred and Eighty-Six, sitting on Teague's bunk in Bay Fourteen with the bag open on her knees. Nobody else was in the bay. Pell was in the galley with the others. Juno was wherever Juno went now. Bracken's bunk was stripped. She had stripped it herself.

The envelope was addressed in Dov's hand, which was worse than hers, big and round and leaning backward, like a child's: *To the Engineering Office, Lindqvist Hardware & Fittings, Halvard Four.* There was a stamp on it from the Ostrava docks, and across it, in a Proctor's blue ink, *RETURN TO SENDER*.

She opened it with her scribe.

*Sirs,*

*My name is Dov Dace. I am a rigger on the Combine's transit works at TTH-9 by Kettle. I am writing about your harness clip, the Model Seven, the one with the swaged throat.*

*When the line comes off the clip at an angle, as it does when a man swings out on his line to ride a load, the throat flexes. Not much. You can't see it. But it does it every time, and it is not rated for it, and after enough times the steel inside the throat cracks where you can't see, and the eye pulls through. I have drawn it on the back of this so you can see what I mean. I am not an engineer but I have watched it happen twice and I am sure.*

*I have reported this to the Combine sixteen times. They say it is under review.*

*I am writing to you because you made it and you can make it better. I don't think it would cost much. A thicker throat, or a guard. You would know.*

*There are four hundred and some of us out here hanging off your clips, nine hundred meters up, over a brown dwarf. We don't mind the height. We mind the clips.*

*I have a sister on my gang. She counts the days till she can go home. She has eleven hundred and twenty-seven left, as of this morning. I would like her to get to the end of them.*

*Yours respectfully,*

*Dov Dace, 0418, Gang Four.*

On the back, in pencil, a drawing of a clip. Very careful. Much better than his handwriting. The throat, in cross-section, with little arrows showing where the line pulled and where the steel bent, and a crack drawn in, fine as a hair, where the crack would be.

She sat on Teague's bunk for a long time with the letter in her lap.

Then she folded it up along its creases and put it inside her liner, against her skin, where the sheet with the blue letterhead had been. And she got up, and went to her own bunk, and lay down with her face to the wall and her fingers on the marks, and found the bottom rows, and the line she had cut under Day Two Hundred and Eighty-Eight, deep, so that her fingertip fit inside it.

Two left, before the line. She crossed off one. She left the other.

* * *

The *Assessor* docked at the start of second shift on Day Two Hundred and Eighty-Seven, and the bonds went aboard her one at a time through the dock lock at the head of the spine, past the hatch that Bracken had lifted and that still hung jammed at half a meter with its hydraulics blown, past two Proctors with stinger guns who searched every one of them, and into her holds.

There were four hundred and six of them. Sol stood at the side of the dock lock and watched them go. It took four hours. They went in silence, mostly, carrying what they had been told they could carry, which was one bag each; and some of them looked at her as they passed and some did not, and some touched her arm, and Mother Szell stopped in front of her with her bag in one hand and her frying pan, which nobody had made her give up, in the other, and looked up into Sol's face for a long time, and said, in her voice like a rusty hinge, "They hanged nineteen on the cranes at Pier Six in '64. Teague told you."

"Yes."

"They're not hanging anybody this time," said Mother Szell. "Not even you, maybe. That's something." She looked at Sol a moment longer. "It's not enough. But it's something." And she went on, into the lock.

Oyelaran went. Ruzicka went, and Danek, with his bunkmate's hand on his shoulder, the bunkmate with a patch over one eye. The galley crew went. The air-plant crew. Gang Two and Gang Six and Gang Eleven, what was left of it. Faces she had seen every day for twelve years in the spine and the galley and on the Ring's face behind visors. Faces she did not know at all. The term of their natural life, in the service of the Combine, at the platform. They went into the *Assessor*'s holds to be carried out a thousand kilometers and kept safe and brought back, and they would come back to Bay Fourteen and the ballast hold and the galley and the Ring, and go out on it again tomorrow, or the day after, in Lindqvist clips, and not come home. Not ever. But alive.

Juno went near the end.

She came to the lock with her bag and stopped in front of Sol, and the Proctors waited, and Juno stood very straight and looked at her.

"I'd have flown her home," she said. "The *Patience*. After. If they'd let me."

"I know."

"She's at the dock, on the end berth. She's holding air. Just." Juno's voice was very precise. "Somebody should look after her. After. She's a good old girl."

"I'll tell them."

Juno nodded. She did not move. Then she said, in a smaller voice, "Sol—"

"Go on, Juno."

"Tibor's nine."

"I know."

"I'd do it again," said Juno. "That's the worst of it. I've thought about it every minute since Lusk came on the speakers, and if they came to me tomorrow and said Tibor, I'd do it again." She looked at Sol, and her eyes were dry, and terrible. "I'm sorry. I'm not sorry enough. I'm not going to be."

Sol looked at her for a long time.

"Go and see your boy, Juno," she said. "If they ever let you."

Juno went into the lock.

Pell was last.

He had waited. He had stood at the far side of the dock in his suit with his helmet under his good arm and his burned arm strapped across his chest, and let everyone go past him, all four hundred and five, and when the last of them was through and the Proctors were looking at their slate, he came across the dock and stood in front of her.

She had the flask in her hand. She had brought it from Bay Fourteen. She held it out.

He looked at it.

"Take it," she said. "Fill it with chicory. Every shift. It keeps it hot through the whole shift, even out on the face, even in the dark part. You'll want it hot, in the dark part."

"Chief—"

"Take it, Okafor."

He took it. His big cracked farm hand closed round it, and the dent in its shoulder fit under his thumb.

"I'll bring it back," he said. "After. When they bring you in. I'll—"

"Listen to me." She bent toward him, so that the Proctors could not hear. "Tonight. When I'm out there. I'm going to sing. On the open channel. All night, probably; I get bored. And some of it's going to be the Count, and some of it's going to be new. Verses you don't know." She looked into his face. "Learn them. All of them. Every word. Get everybody in that hold to learn them. Sing them back to me so I know you've got them. And then keep singing them. After. All of you. Teach them to anybody who'll listen." She held his eyes. "And never let it be in only one head. You understand me? Never one. If you're the only one left who knows it, you teach somebody before you do anything else. Before you eat."

"Why?"

"Because a head can stop," said Sol.

He looked at her. And she saw it come into his face, slowly, the look he got: the farm boy's look, at a thing that did not make sense yet but was going to.

"Okafor," said a Proctor, at the lock. "Now."

Pell did not move.

"Go on," Sol said. "Keep them singing, when it's bad. Teague said. It's the singing that gets you through."

He went. At the lock he turned round once, with the flask in his good hand, and she lifted her hand to him, and then the lock closed.

* * *

Haddad came to her in the dock lock's inner chamber, a little before, while the last of them were going through. She did not come close. She stood by the bulkhead in the grey rigger's suit too big for her in the shoulders, with her helmet under her arm, and said, without looking at Sol, in a voice so low that Sol had to step nearer to hear it:

"The third notch."

Sol waited.

"The deep one. In the Barn, on the third arm, with no dog in it. Your gang calls it the leftover." Haddad looked at the floor. "It isn't a leftover. It was the thirteenth notch. In my design. The quench position." She lifted her eyes. "The governor holds the throat steady by breathing. If you stop it breathing—a dog in an ordinary notch, like yours—and the field tries to couple, it shears. That's what you threatened him with. But if the throat's already open, and you seat a dog in the third notch, and turn it, the dog doesn't stop the governor. It *decouples* it. Throws it out of phase with the field. Cleanly. On purpose. And the throat closes." She paused. "Not a shear. Not a front. Nothing comes out. It folds in. Forty seconds, about. Like a hand closing."

"And then?"

"And then it's shut. And it stays shut, for as long as that dog's seated in that notch and turned. Nothing on the other side can open it. They can couple their ring as hard as they like; there's nothing on this side for it to couple to. They can knock on it till the end of time." Haddad looked at her. "I designed it in case the throat ever ran wild. A way to shut the door. From this side. Lind struck it out. He said a gate that could be shut by anybody with a wrench wasn't an asset, it was a liability. But the notch was already cut. And the bore through the Housing was already drilled. And nobody filled them in, because filling them in would have cost money."

Sol was quiet.

"A dog in the third bore," she said slowly. "Not seated. Just—sitting there. Waiting. Not in the notch. Would your instruments see it?"

"No." Haddad's voice was barely there. "Not until it was turned. Then everything would see it."

"And if somebody had the turns. Which way, how many."

"Twice counter to the governor's breath, then once with it, to seat the key in the step. Then pin it through the head so it can't back out. Then a quarter more." Haddad shut her eyes. "That's from the design. I drew it. I don't remember which way's which on the Housing's face; it depends where you stand. Left and right change." She opened them. "Chief. What you're thinking. It won't save you. It won't save anyone now. They're going to light it in the morning and come back and work this platform until everyone on it's dead. That's what you've bought them. A lock won't change that. Not now."

"Not now," Sol agreed.

They stood in the dock lock's inner chamber and looked at each other.

"Your instruments," said Sol. "Tonight. When I pull the dog out of number one. You'll tell them the governor's clear."

"It will be clear."

"And nothing else."

Haddad was quiet for a long time.

"Nothing else," she said.

Then she turned and went into the lock, and the Proctors searched her as they had searched everybody, and found nothing, because there was nothing to find; and Sol stood in the empty chamber and listened to the *Assessor*'s holds fill up through the bulkhead, four hundred and six people breathing.

* * *

They took her to the Spindle in the Proctors' boat, because the *Patience* was on the end berth holding air, just, with two of her arms gone and holes in her hull you could see through.

Lusk flew it himself. He did not speak to her. He sat in the pilot's seat with his square pale face behind his visor and his hands on the board, and she sat behind him in her rigger's suit with three extra air packs strapped to her harness and her own big driver, Gang Four's driver, a meter and a half of dark steel worn bright at the grip by twelve years of her hands, clipped across her back; and the Ring went by under them, and the Spindle came up, black against Kettle, laced with gold.

He put her off at the rigging hatch. He did not come in close; he held the boat twenty meters off and let her go across on her line, and when she was on the hatch frame and clipped to it, he said, on the open channel, flat and clipped: "Surety in place. Dace, M., zero four one seven. At the Spindle. Boat withdrawing."

And then, after a moment, on the private channel, so quietly she nearly did not hear it: "Twelve years, Dace. You'd nearly done it."

She did not answer him. The boat's thrusters puffed white and it turned and went, small and fast, back along the Ring's face toward the platform and the *Assessor*, and then it was gone, and she was alone.

The Barn was dark. The governor was still. Number One's dog stood out of its cuff a hand's width, where they had driven it home two days ago, where Bracken's hands had been.

She went to the crane.

She had never worked it alone. Oyelaran had, a hundred times, but Oyelaran was a crane man and she was a rigger and you did not touch another gang's crane, it was the oldest rule on the Ring after the one about not letting the young ones go first. She clipped in at its control seat. Its little lights came on amber. She looked at the board and thought about Oyelaran's hands on it, the way they had moved, and put her own hands where his had been, and the boom unfolded out into the dark with its long hiss.

It took her three hours to get the dog out of Number One. She had to back it out with the driver, alone, a quarter-turn and then the crane, a quarter-turn and then the crane, because there was no one else on the other three drivers to keep it even; and she called the Count to herself, out loud, *one for the hook*, and answered herself, *and two for the line*. Three rounds. When the last round was done the crane walked the dog out of the bore, four meters of steel, grinding, whispering through her gloves.

And the governor breathed.

She felt it, through the cuff under her hand. The hair one way. The hair back. Alive.

"*Assessor*," she said, on the open channel. "This is Dace. Number One's out."

It was Haddad who answered. "Confirmed. The governor's breathing. Cycle eleven point four. Nominal." Her voice was dry and level and accented and perfectly calm. "Governor clear."

There was a pause on the channel. Then Lind's voice, soft as folded paper: "Thank you, Doctor. Thank you, Chief. Please remain where you are."

"Where would I go?" said Sol, and switched them off.

Then she swung the crane round.

The dog hung in its claws, four meters of dark steel, turning very slightly on its grip as the boom swung it out across the Barn. Away from Number One, and its rack, and its empty bore. Toward the third arm. The big one, with the Inner Housing round it, the great boxy mass of steel plating four meters thick wrapping the cuff and a whole section of the governor ring behind it. Toward the empty bore through the Housing's face, that lined up on the deep notch, with its stepped profile, like a key-slot.

She brought the dog's point to the bore's mouth, and stopped the crane, and hung there on her line beside it in the dark with her lamp on the Housing's face, and breathed.

Then she switched the open channel back on, and began to sing.

* * *

She sang the Count first. The old one. All of it, the way Gang Four had always sung it, calling the numbers herself and answering herself, while she let the crane ease the dog into the bore, slowly, a meter, two meters, three, the long grinding whisper through her gloves, and stopped it with its point a hand's width short of the notch's mouth. Short. Not in. Waiting. Its head stood out of the Housing's face a hand's width more than a seated dog would, and no more.

*Eight for the dark and nine for the drop.*

She took her scribe out of her thigh pocket and cut three short lines in the steel of the Housing's face beside the dog's head. Side by side, a finger apart, each the length of a fingernail. Witness marks. So that whoever came would know where it had been, and where it was meant to go.

*Ten for the Company—ten, and stop.*

She stopped. And on the open channel, very faint, very far off, out of the *Assessor*'s holds a thousand kilometers away, she heard it.

*Count it up and count it slow,*
*count it back down and home we go.*

Four hundred voices. Ragged, and late, and out of tune, because the *Assessor*'s holds were not built for singing and the bonds were packed into them like cargo, and some of them did not know the words, and some of them were crying. But singing. She heard Pell's cracked tenor in it, a little sharp, leading. She heard Mother Szell. She heard, she thought, Juno.

She hung on her line beside the dog in the dark and let it go through her.

Then she sang the new verse.

She had made it in the dock lock's inner chamber, while Haddad talked and while the four hundred and six went past her. She had made it the way the riggers had always made verses, out of what was in front of them: the Housing's face, and which way the light came from, and the turns Haddad had given her, *twice counter, then once with it*, and the dog and the notch and the scribed marks. She had made it so that it would go to the tune of the Count, and so that a child could skip to it, and so that anyone who stood where she was standing now, in front of the Housing's face, with their hand on the dog, could do what it said.

She sang it slowly, the first time. As though she were speaking to someone just beside her.

*Sun on your left and the Kettle below,*
*dog to the notch where the old marks show,*
*twice to the dark and once to the light,*
*seat it and pin it and turn it tight.*

She had turned round, singing it, on her line, in front of the Housing's face; and the rigging hatch on the Barn's far side was on her left, the second hatch, the open one, and through it, small and dim and red as a banked coal, Hearth. And below her, through the Barn's open floor-hatch, beyond the Ring, filling everything, Kettle. And the dark was the way the governor did not breathe, and the light was the way it did, and that was how you would know which way to turn, wherever you stood, for as long as there was a Hearth and a Kettle.

*That is the way that you lock the door—*

She stopped. She breathed.

*—count it back down and it's open once more.*

The channel was silent.

She sang it again. And again. She sang the whole Count, the old part and the new, and stopped at the end of it, and waited. And the third time, out of the far-off hold, she heard Pell's voice come in under hers, on the second line, cracked and sharp and very careful, *dog to the notch where the old marks show*; and then others, a few, and then more, until by the fifth time through the whole hold was singing it back to her, ragged and late and out of tune, four hundred voices a thousand kilometers off, *twice to the dark and once to the light*, getting the words wrong and putting them right, over and over, the way you learn a song in a crowd, the way the riggers had always learned everything; and nobody on the *Assessor* who was not a bond had the least idea what they were singing about. It was a work song. A dying woman on the Spindle singing a work song to keep her spirits up, and her gang singing it back to her. That was all. Lind, she imagined, did not even look up from his book.

She sang it with them for an hour. Then she let them carry it, and went on working.

* * *

She opened the plate at the second hour of the night watch.

Ten bolts, in a star, round a plate the size of a cart wheel on the Housing's face, beside the dog's head. She took them out with her own driver in the order of the Count. *One for the hook.* Each one turned with a long dry squeal that she felt through her teeth. *Two for the line.* She stacked them on a magnet on her thigh. When the tenth was out she swung the plate back on its hinge, and her lamp went in through the opening and lit the chamber inside: a small steel room, four meters of steel on every side, with the governor's old instruments on its far wall, and, mounted in the middle of them, Haddad's clock. A grey box the size of a kit bag, with three cables running into it from three directions, and a small green display on its face, counting.

*3:41:09.*

*3:41:08.*

She did not look at it again.

She turned the plate on its hinge so that its inside face was toward her, in her lamp. Bare steel. Grey. Smooth. Five centimeters thick, Company plate, the best they had. Nobody would ever see this side of it unless they took out all ten bolts, in order, and swung it back; and nobody would ever take out all ten bolts in order unless they knew to.

She took out her scribe.

It was a rigger's scribe, a hand's length of hardened steel with a point like a needle, the same one she had used on her first night aboard to scratch six hundred and twenty-four marks into the wall by her bunk. She had kept it in the thigh pocket of her liner for twelve years. It had gone through the cloth and into her leg in the spine corridor on Day Two Hundred and Forty-Seven. It cut Company plate the way it cut Company polymer: slowly, with a squeal, a hair at a time.

She cut the letters large, and square, and deep, in the plain face of the stencils on the platform's walls, because that was the only lettering she knew how to make properly, and because she wanted them to last.

IF YOU ARE READING THIS YOU OPENED IT RIGHT.

WE BUILT THIS. 412 OF US. BONDED. THE COMBINE MEANT TO BURN US AT FIRST LIGHT FOR THE INSURANCE AND WE MADE THEM STOP. THEY PUT US ON FOR LIFE INSTEAD.

THIS IS A LOCK. IT WORKS FROM THIS SIDE ONLY. COUNT IT UP TO LOCK. COUNT IT BACK DOWN TO OPEN. THE SONG IS THE KEY. WE ALL HAVE IT.

DON'T LET THEM OWN THE DOOR. LOCK IT IF YOU HAVE TO.

FIRST LIGHT IS A WAVE. GET BEHIND THE STEEL.

It took her a long time. Her hand cramped in her glove and she stopped and worked it and went on. The squeal of the scribe was in her teeth and her jaw and the back of her skull. On the open channel, the hold on the *Assessor* was still singing, softer now, some of them asleep, some of them not, a few voices going round and round the new verse like water round a stone. Sometimes she sang with them. Sometimes she only listened.

Under the words she cut the verse. All of it. The old Count, the numbers, and then the Door. In the order she had made it. And beside the Door, two small curved arrows, one marked DARK and one marked LIGHT, the way a rigger marks a valve, so that nobody would ever have to guess. So that if the song drifted—and songs drifted; she had sung the Count on the Ring for twelve years and it had lost a verse and gained one in that time—whoever opened the plate would have it the way it was meant.

And then her name. She thought about it for a while before she cut it. Then she cut it the way it was written on the base of her flask, and on the sheet with the blue letterhead, and on the wall of Bay Fourteen above the door.

M. DACE. 0417. GANG FOUR. RIGGER.

And under it, after a moment, smaller, because it was true and there was no one else to say it:

AND DOV DACE 0418. AND KOSTYA LEM. AND ABRAM TEAGUE. AND BRACKEN. WHO HELD ON.

She looked at it for a long time in her lamp.

Then she took the locking pin out of her harness pouch—a short fat plug of hardened steel the length of her finger, the pin that would go through the dog's head when the dog was seated and keep it from ever backing out—and wired it to the inside of the plate, under her name, with a twist of tether wire through two holes she burned for it with the cutter's short blue tongue. Where the next pair of hands would find it, with the words.

Then she swung the plate shut, and put the ten bolts back in, in the order of the star, backward. Ten to one. *Count it back down.*

The display on the clock behind it had said *2:12:40* when she closed the plate. She did not need to see it any more. She could feel the time going, in her own body, like the governor's breath.

She unclipped her driver from her back, Gang Four's driver, a meter and a half of dark steel worn bright at the grip, and laid it on the steel lip at the foot of the Housing's face, under the dog's head, where the next pair of hands would find it. Then she clipped herself to the hardpoint by the plate, and hung there, and switched the private channel on, because the light on it had been blinking for some time.

"Chief Dace," said Castellane.

"Director."

"I wanted—" He stopped. She could hear him breathing. He was alone, she thought; there was no one behind him. "I wanted to say that as soon as the front's past I'll have the boat at the Spindle. Lusk's flying it. I've told him. Myself. The minute Dr. Haddad says it's safe."

"How long will that be?"

He did not answer.

"Six hours," said Sol. "Eight. To be sure. That's what Haddad'll say. Maybe a day."

"I'll send it the minute—"

"You won't," said Sol. "But you'll mean to."

The channel was quiet.

"I'd like to ask you for something," she said.

"Anything."

"There's a dog in the third bore. On the Housing. Sticking out of the face a hand's width." She looked at it in her lamp, its squared head, the three marks scribed beside it. "When your people come after, they'll see it. Your engineers. They'll want to pull it, because it's not in the plans. Don't let them."

"What is it?"

"Tell them it's structural," said Sol. "Tell them it holds up the Ring. Tell them anything. Tell them the Founders put it there." She almost laughed. "You'll be a Founder. All of you, after this. You'll have your names on things. Tell them it's yours, and nobody'll touch it."

He was quiet for a long time.

"Is it structural?" he said.

"It holds up something," said Sol.

She heard him breathe, a long breath, in and out.

"All right," said Imre Castellane. "I'll see to it. I promise you."

"And Annick," said Sol. "When you go home. When you see her."

"Yes?"

"Tell her who built it," Sol said. "That's all. Not the Founders. Tell her who built it."

"I will," he said; and then, after a while, in a different voice, very low: "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

"I know," said Sol, and switched him off.

* * *

She went into the Housing at the third hour of first shift, through the crawl hatch round the back, and dogged it shut behind her, and sat down in the dark.

It was very small. She had forgotten. She had been in it twice, and the second time with Dov, and they had sat back to back with their knees drawn up and their suit lamps off, and he had told her the plots of every serial on the platform screens, wrong. She sat with her back to the place where he had been. On the far wall, where she could not see it, Haddad's clock was counting. She could hear it, very faintly, through her helmet, through the steel: a tick, too small and high to be the governor, once a second.

She turned off her lamp to save the cells.

It was the darkest place she had ever been. Darker than the Barn. Darker than the shadow of Kettle on the Ring's face. Four meters of steel on every side, and nothing coming through it, and nothing in it but her own breath in her helmet and the tick of the clock and, under that, the governor, breathing. A hair one way. A hair back. She put her glove flat on the steel floor and felt it.

The open channel was very quiet now. The hold on the *Assessor* had gone to sleep, mostly. She could hear, faintly, one voice still going, low, cracked, a little sharp. *Sun on your left and the Kettle below.* Over and over. As if he was afraid that if he stopped he would forget.

She sang it with him for a while. Then she let him go on alone, and listened.

She thought about the soup. Pier Six, under the old cranes. Real fish from the tanks, with the bones still in. She thought about the cranes, where they had hanged nineteen men in '64 for the ships coming in to see, and about Teague at nineteen walking under them every day of his life. She thought about Kostya, sixty meters past the end of the chain, turning slowly in the work lights, saying *I never saw the whole thing*. About Dov, writing a letter to a factory on Halvard Four, with a careful drawing on the back. *I would like her to get to the end of them.* About the marks on the wall of Bay Fourteen above her bunk, six hundred and twenty-four of them, crossed through; and the small ones at the bottom, in their tens; and the one she had left uncrossed last night, before the line.

She had not got to the end of them. She thought Dov would mind that more than she did.

"Okafor," she said, on the open channel.

The singing stopped.

"Chief?" Very far off. Very small.

"Have you got it?"

"I've got it. We've all got it. Chief, they're saying it's an hour—"

"I know what they're saying. Fill the flask in the morning. Chicory. It's free."

"Chief—"

"Keep them singing," said Sol, and switched off the open channel, and the private, and every channel there was, and sat in the dark with her glove on the floor, feeling the governor breathe.

She began, under her breath, to count.

Not the days. She had stopped counting the days. The Count. *One for the hook.* Slowly, the way she had taught Pell. *Two for the line.* As though she were speaking to someone just beside her. *Three for the dog.*

The tick of the clock stopped.

And the governor took a breath it had never taken. She felt it begin, under her glove: not the hair one way and the hair back, but one way, only one, a long slow shift, as though something enormous on the other side of the steel had leaned its whole weight against the world. The floor of the Housing tilted under her. Not much. Enough. The whole Spindle around her, nine hundred meters of it, began to sing—a deep note, rising, in the steel and her bones and the floor of her belly and her teeth, a note she had never heard it make and nobody had ever heard it make, the note of a door opening that had never been opened.

*Four for the time*, she said.

Through four meters of steel she could not see anything. She did not need to. She could feel it. The throat forming, out there, in the Ring she had built; space tearing along a line drawn with ten thousand kilometers of gold, very precisely; here and Ostrava becoming two places and then one. Forty seconds, Haddad had said. She counted them. *Five for the air. Six for the seal.*

And then the front.

It did not feel like anything she had expected. It did not feel like heat, or light, or a blow. It felt like being remembered. That was the only word she ever had for it, in the few minutes she had to look for one: as though every part of her, every bone and cell, were suddenly and completely known, all at once, by something enormous that had been looking for it; and then let go. Her suit's dosimeter screamed in her helmet, a long high whoop, and then stopped, all at once, burned out. The note of the Spindle went up and up out of hearing. And through four meters of steel, through her closed eyes, through the back of her skull, there was a whiteness. Not light. Whiteness. Like snow on the hills above Ostrava that she had seen once as a girl, from a train.

*Seven*, she said, *the hand on the cold steel wheel.*

Then it was past.

The note came down. The floor of the Housing settled under her glove. And the governor—she felt it, she would swear to it afterward to anyone who would listen, except that there was no afterward and nobody to swear to—the governor breathed. A hair one way. A hair back. Steady. Slow. Alive.

She sat in the dark for a while with her hand on the floor.

Then she found the crawl hatch by feel, and undogged it, and went out.

It was not sensible. Haddad would have told her it was not sensible; the front had passed but the throat was open now and it would be throwing off a steady burn of its own for as long as it was open, and the Barn was not four meters of steel. She did not care. She went out through the crawl hatch and round the Housing on her line in the dark, past the dog's head standing out of its face, past the plate with its ten bolts, past Gang Four's driver lying on the steel lip where she had laid it; and across the Barn to the second rigging hatch, the one on the far side, the open one, that looked out on nothing.

And looked out.

The Ring was lit.

Not with work lights. With itself. The whole inner face of it, thirty-eight kilometers round, the gold lines that she and four hundred and eleven others had laid and seated and torqued by the Count for twelve years, were burning—not bright, not like fire, but alive, a soft clean gold, all the way round, so that from the hatch she could see the whole curve of it at once, going away on both sides and meeting on the far side, small, a bracelet of light. And inside it, filling it, where Kettle should have been—

She did not have a word for it. Nobody would ever quite have one. A disk of light from rim to rim, blue-white at the edges, deepening toward the center into a color that was not violet and not black. And it was moving. Not like water, not like fire. Like a held breath moves in a chest. A slow swell, and a settling. And through it, if you looked long enough, other stars.

She looked a long time.

"Kostya," she said, to nobody, on no channel. "I can see the whole thing."

She sat down on the hatch's lip with her boots locked to the frame and her line clipped to the hardpoint, the way you would sit on a pier, and looked at it. Her suit's air was at sixty percent. She had three packs on her harness. The boat would come when Haddad said it was safe; six hours, eight, a day. Castellane would send it the minute. He would mean to. She found that she was very tired, and that her hands, inside her gloves, were beginning to feel strange, as though they belonged to someone else, someone a long way off; and that she did not mind very much.

She sat on the hatch's lip and watched the Eye breathe, and counted. Not the days. Its breaths. A slow swell, and a settling. One. And two. And three.

Somewhere behind her in the Barn, in the dark, a dog stood out of the Housing's face a hand's width, by three short lines scribed in the steel. Waiting.
