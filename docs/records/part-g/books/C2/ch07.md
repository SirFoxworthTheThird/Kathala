# Chapter 7: Insured Losses

*The Combine · FY 2291*

The Director saw the gang chiefs on Day Two Hundred and Fifty-Eight, in the long room on the top deck of the admin stack where the Company held its inquiries.

There were thirty-one of them. Thirty-one gangs on the Ring, thirty-one chiefs, and every one of them had put in a request to see the Director in the eleven days since the speakers came on, and every one of them had been docked a day for it, and then on the morning of the twelfth day a notice had appeared on their terminals: *The Site Director will meet with gang chiefs at the second hour of first shift. Attendance is compulsory. Time will be charged as Company time.* As though, Juno said, he were doing them a favor by not docking them for coming to be lied to.

They stood. There were chairs, but nobody had told them to sit, and nobody did. The room had a carpet, real carpet, blue, worn grey in a path from the door to the long table at the far end, and the walls were paneled in something that looked like wood and might even have been, and there were windows, big ones, double-glazed and clean, looking out over the Ring. Sol had never been in it before. She had been in the inquiry room on the deck below twice, once for Dov and once for a cracked cassette that had not been her gang's fault and that she had been docked for anyway, and that room had had no carpet and no windows. She stood at the back with her hands behind her and looked out at the Ring curving away in the work lights, and at the Spindle standing up off the rim nine kilometers round, like a needle, and at Kettle filling everything, and she waited.

Imre Castellane came in by a side door with two aides and a Proctor, and the room went quiet.

He was not what she had expected, the first time she had seen him, at Dov's inquiry. She had expected someone harder. He was a tall soft-shouldered man of fifty-five or so with a big handsome tired face, grey at the temples, and he wore the Company's dark blue undress coat with the collar open, as though he had been working late and had not had time to button it, and perhaps he had. He had kind eyes. She had noticed that at Dov's inquiry, too, and had hated him for it, and had gone on hating him for it for three years; and she found now, seeing him come in and look round the room at all of them, really look, one face after another, that she could not quite remember how.

"Sit down," he said. "Please. All of you. I'm sorry nobody told you to sit."

They sat. He did not. He stood at the head of the long table and put his hands flat on it and looked down the room.

"I know why you're here," he said. "I know you've all asked to see me, and I know you were docked for asking, and I want to say first that I've reversed those docks, all of them, as of this morning. It shouldn't have happened. That's not how I want this platform run, and I'm sorry." There was a murmur. He waited for it to pass. "I also know what you want to ask me. You want to ask me about the Lighting. You want to know why it's been brought forward, and what it means for you. And you deserve answers. So I'm going to give you as many as I can."

He gave them a great many. He talked for most of an hour, without notes, in a low, clear, reasonable voice, and Sol, at the back, listened to every word.

There was a phase instability in the governor. It had been identified by Dr. Haddad's team in the course of routine modeling. It was not dangerous now, but it would become dangerous if the Ring were left unlit for too long in its present state, because the phase-lines were already seated and partly coupled, and Kettle's field was working on them every day, and an unlit Ring with coupled lines was like—he looked for the image—like a bow drawn and held. You could hold it for a while. Not forever. The longer you held it the worse it would be when it went. So the board, on Dr. Haddad's advice, had authorized an early Lighting. It was the safest course. It was, in fact, the only safe course.

The skirt work was suspended because the skirt was no longer needed. Dr. Haddad's revised models showed that the hab rings, at the platform's present distance from the Ring, would receive a dose from first light well within the Company's safety limits. The skirt had been designed under a conservative specification, at a time when first light was less well understood. Engineers, he said, and smiled a little, loved redundancy; they would wear a belt and braces to bed if you let them. But the platform would not be relying on the hab rings' rating alone. In the days before the Lighting, the mooring arm would be released, and the platform would be stood off, under tow, to a distance of four hundred kilometers, behind the shoulder of Gall, where it would be entirely shadowed. The *Assessor* would tow her. The tugs would assist.

As for the *Mercy*—he spread his hands. Yes, she was late. Haulers were late. The outer relay was old and had failed before. He had no reason to think anything was wrong with her, and every reason to think she would arrive on Day Three Hundred as scheduled, and that every bonded worker on the platform whose term was complete would be aboard her when she left. He would be on her himself. He was going home too.

"I have a daughter," he said. "On Ostrava. Annick. She's eleven. She was four when I came out here. I've seen her on a screen twice a year, when the courier comes. I want to go home and see her as much as any of you want to see your families. More, maybe." He looked down the table. "We've been out here a long time. Some of you longer than me. We've built something here that no one has ever built before. In thirty days, we're going to light it, and the Reach will be open, and everything that comes after—every ship, every colony, every child born out here for a thousand years—will come through a door that you built. Your hands. I want you to remember that. I want you to be proud of it, because I am. And then I want us all to go home."

There was a silence when he stopped. Not an angry one. Sol could feel the room. They wanted to believe him. She wanted to believe him. It came off the thirty other chiefs like heat off a hull.

Oyelaran put his hand up.

"The *Mercy*," he said. "If she's late. If she's not here on Day Three Hundred. What then?"

"Then she'll be here on Day Three Ten," said Castellane, "and we'll all have had ten more days to admire the view." There was a laugh, a small one, and Oyelaran sat back.

Sol did not put her hand up. She waited until it was over and the chiefs were filing out past the aides, talking low, some of them almost cheerful, and she went the other way, up the room along the grey path in the carpet, to where the Director stood by the head of the table talking quietly to one of the aides, and she said, "Director."

He turned. The Proctor by the side door turned too, and took a step, and Castellane lifted a hand without looking at him, and the Proctor stopped.

"Chief Dace," said Castellane. "Gang Four."

She had not expected him to know her name. Then she remembered Dov's inquiry, and thought he probably did not, that the aide had a list. Then she saw his face, and thought he probably did.

"I'd like to ask you something," she said. "In private. If that's allowed."

He looked at her for a moment, and something moved behind the kind eyes; and then he smiled and said, "Of course. Come through," and opened the side door himself, and held it.

* * *

His office was small and warm, and it smelled of coffee.

Real coffee. Sol had not smelled it in twelve years except on the clothes of Company staff passing in the spine, and it hit her in the doorway like a hand on the chest. There was a pot of it on a hot plate on a shelf behind his desk. The desk was wood, real, dark and old and scarred, and it was too big for the room, and on it there was a tablet and a stack of paper—paper—and a model of the Ring, a meter across, in brass and black enamel, with the Spindle standing up off its rim in silver and a tiny platform moored beside it, and Kettle, in the middle, a ball of dark glass with bands of color in it. And a picture, in a frame. A girl, in a blue school coat, laughing at something off to the side.

There was someone else in the room. Sol did not see him until she was inside, because he was sitting in the corner behind the door in an armchair, very still, reading. A thin man in grey, with thin grey hair combed straight back from a high forehead, and rimless spectacles, and a paper book open on his knee. He did not look up.

"This is Mr. Lind," said Castellane. "From the board. He's observing. Don't mind him." He went round the desk. "Coffee?"

"No," said Sol. "Thank you."

He poured himself one. His hands, she noticed, were not quite steady, either. He sat down and gestured at the chair in front of the desk, and she sat, on the edge of it.

"You wanted to ask me something," he said.

"The mooring arm," Sol said.

He waited.

"You said the platform would be stood off. Released, and towed to Gall." She kept her voice level. "The mooring arm was welded. Last year. Day One Hundred and Ninety, last fiscal. Kettle's tides were working the coupling loose, and Gangs Four and Seven welded the coupling solid, all the way round. Forty meters of weld. I ran the bead myself on the north face." She looked at him. "It's not a coupling any more. It's not designed to release. To stand the platform off you'd have to cut it. That's two gangs and nine days, if nothing goes wrong. And nobody's been tasked to cut it."

Castellane looked at her over his coffee.

"And the *Assessor*," Sol said, "is a command ship. She's not a tug. She's got maybe a tenth the pull she'd need to shift the platform's mass. With all our tugs together on it, the *Patience* and the other six, you'd still need a month to move her four hundred kilometers, and you'd need to start now. Today. And you haven't." She stopped. "That's what I wanted to ask. Sir."

The room was quiet. In the corner, the thin man turned a page.

"You're very well informed, Chief," said Castellane at last.

"I'm a rigger. I built it."

"Yes." He set the coffee down. He looked at the model of the Ring on his desk, at the tiny platform beside it, and touched it with one finger, the way a man touches a sore tooth with his tongue. "The engineers have a plan for the coupling. It may be that they intend to cut it closer to the date. It may be that the tow is staged. I'm not an engineer, Chief Dace. I'm an administrator. I take the plans the engineers give me and I make sure they're carried out." He looked up. "If there's a problem with the plan, I'll take it up with Dr. Haddad. I promise you that."

"And if the plan is that there isn't a plan?"

"Then I'll take that up with her, too."

She looked at him. He looked back at her. And she saw it again, the thing that moved behind his eyes; and this time she could name it. It was not cruelty. She had seen cruelty on Company faces, on Lusk's, on Fenn's, sometimes, when he was in the mood. It was not contempt. It was something quieter and much worse. It was the look of a man who had already made his peace with something, and who was very tired, and who wanted, more than anything, for her to stop asking him to make it again.

"What's your bond number, Chief?" said the thin man in the corner.

She turned. He had not looked up from his book.

"What?"

"Your bond number," said Mr. Lind. His voice was soft and dry and precise, like paper being folded. "For the record. Of this meeting."

"Zero four one seven."

"Thank you." He took a pencil out of his breast pocket, a real pencil, and wrote something in the margin of his book, and put the pencil back, and turned a page.

Castellane stood up. "Thank you for coming, Chief Dace," he said. "I mean that. I'll look into the coupling."

He came round the desk and opened the door for her, and as she went past him into the long room with the blue carpet, he said, very quietly, so quietly that afterward she was not sure he had said it at all: "Look after your gang."

* * *

The revised work schedules went up on the terminals that night. The Ring was to be finished in thirty days instead of fifty. Shifts went to eleven hours. Rest cycles were cut from three to two. Suit air was not increased. Nobody was docked for anything that week, which frightened people more than the docks had.

On Day Two Hundred and Sixty-One, in the ninth hour of a shift that should have ended in the eighth, Pell Okafor put a plasma cutter through the sleeve of his own liner.

It was not his fault. Sol said so to the medic, and to the shift engineer, and to Fenn, who came round with his tablet. It was the schedule's fault. Gang Four had been out for nine hours and on two rest cycles for three days running, and the boy had been working one-handed at the end of his line on a seam the cutter would not reach, and his other hand had gone numb with cold in a glove whose heater was failing, and he had not felt the cutter's nozzle drift. The flame did not go through the suit. It went through the outer layer of the sleeve and the insulation and stopped at the inner pressure layer, which held, as it was built to; and so instead of venting his arm to vacuum, it only cooked it, from the elbow to the wrist, through the liner, for the two seconds it took him to notice.

He did not scream. Sol had been waiting for him to. He said, "Chief," in an odd, interested voice, as Kostya had said *oh*. And then he said, "Chief, I think I've done something," and she looked over and saw the white smoke coming off his sleeve in the vacuum, boiling away in a thin bright plume.

They got him in. Juno flew the *Patience* back faster than Sol had ever seen her fly, and Bracken carried him from the dock to the infirmary in his arms like a child, in his suit, because they could not get the suit off him without taking the arm with it; and in the infirmary they cut the suit away while he lay on the table with his eyes open and his teeth clenched, and the smell came up, and Sol held his other hand and did not look.

The medic was a Company man named Amsel, thin and fair and exhausted, who had been on the platform two years and was owed, he said once, more sleep than anybody alive. He worked on Pell's arm for an hour. When he was done the arm was wrapped from elbow to fingers in a gel sleeve that glowed faintly blue, and Pell was asleep, because Amsel had put him to sleep, and Amsel sat down heavily on a stool by the instrument cabinet and put his face in his hands.

"He'll keep the arm," he said, through his fingers. "He won't have much of the use of it. Not for a long time. Not for work."

"How long?"

"A year. Two. With grafts. On Ostrava." He took his hands away from his face and looked at her. "Not here. I don't have the grafts here. He needs to be on the *Mercy*."

"The *Mercy*'s late."

"I know," said Amsel. "I've heard." He got up, slowly, like an old man. "I'm going to get some water. You can stay with him. Don't touch anything."

He went out. Sol sat on the stool by the table and held Pell's good hand, which was big and cracked and cold, and looked round the infirmary because there was nothing else to look at.

It was not a big room. Four beds, three of them empty. Cabinets. A terminal. A wall screen showing the platform's air and power figures, green, green, green. And on the counter by the terminal, under Amsel's elbow, where he had pushed it aside to make room for the burn kit, a stack of paper.

She would not have looked at it if it had been on a screen. Screens were Company; you did not look at a Company screen any more than you looked at a Proctor. But it was paper, and paper was rare enough on the platform that it drew the eye, and she looked, the way you look at anything strange, and saw the top sheet, and saw what was printed across the top of it in the Company's blue letterhead, and read it without meaning to, upside down.

*OSTRAVA MUTUAL ASSURANCE SOCIETY*
*CONSTRUCTION LOSS POLICY OM/2291/0044*
*SCHEDULE OF INSURED BONDED LABOUR — TRANSIT RING TTH-9*

She let go of Pell's hand. She stood up and went to the counter, and she did not touch anything, and she read.

It was a form. It had the look of forms, boxes and lines and small print, and most of it she did not understand. There was a Policy Period, which began on a day four years ago and ended on Day Two Hundred and Ninety of the current year. There was a Claimant, which was the Ostrava-Halvard Combine. There was a box labeled *Per Capita Indemnity (Remaining Bond Value + Completion Liability Avoided)*, with a figure in it so large that she read it three times. There was a box labeled *Medical Certification of Insured Status*, with a line for a signature, and under the line, *Site Medical Officer*, and on the line, in blue ink, still a little wet, *R. Amsel*. And there was a box labeled *Projected Loss Event*, and in it, typed, three letters and three numbers.

*D288.*

Under the first sheet there was a second. And a third. She turned the corner of the first one up with her fingernail, not touching the rest, and saw them: lists. Names and numbers, in columns, in small type, page after page. *Schedule A.* She ran her eye down the top one. *ABARA J. 0012. ADEYEMI T. 0388.* Down the next. She turned them, carefully, by the corners, one after another, until she came to the D's.

*DACE M. 0417. GANG 4. REMAINING BOND VALUE: 42 DAYS. COMPLETION LIABILITY: 1. INSURED STATUS: CERTIFIED.*

Under her name, *LEM K. 0233*, with a line through it, and in the margin in blue ink, *deceased D239, removed*. And *OKAFOR P. 0901*, typed in at the bottom of the page in a different face, new. *REMAINING BOND VALUE: 4,372 DAYS. INSURED STATUS:* and a blank, waiting for Amsel.

She heard his steps in the corridor. She did not decide to do it. She found the page with her name on it, and slid it out from the stack, gently, by its edges, the way you slide a cassette into a cradle, and folded it in four, and put it inside her liner against her skin, and was back on the stool holding Pell's hand when Amsel came in with two cups of water and gave her one.

He did not look at the counter. He sat down on the other stool and drank his water.

"Thank you," Sol said. "For his arm."

"It's my job," said Amsel. He looked at the boy on the table, at the blue sleeve glowing on his arm in the dim light. "God knows it's my job."

* * *

"What's indemnity?" she said.

Teague was sitting on his bunk with his back to the wall and the folded sheet open on his knees, holding it close to the work lamp, because his eyes were going. He did not answer her. He read it all the way through, slowly, his lips moving. Then he read it again. Bay Fourteen was dark except for the lamp. Bracken and Juno were asleep, or pretending to be. Pell's bunk was empty.

"Teague."

"It means insured," he said. He did not look up. "Indemnity's what they pay you if you're insured and you lose the thing you insured. A ship. A cargo. You insure the cargo, the ship goes down, the underwriters indemnify you. Pay you what it was worth."

"So we're insured."

"We're insured." He put one finger on a line of the sheet. "Not *us*. You see? *Insured bonded labour*. Not insured bonded laborers. The labor. Our bonds. What's left of them. What we're still worth to the Company, in days." He moved his finger. "And completion liability. That's the bonus. The completion bonus they owe every bond that finishes its term. And the passage home. Fifteen thousand a head, the passage on the *Mercy*. That's liability. If you don't finish your term, they don't owe it." He moved his finger again. "*Avoided.*"

"Insured against what?"

Teague looked up at her for the first time. In the light of the work lamp his face was the color of old paper, and his lips were blue.

"Against us dying, Sol," he said. "Construction loss. If the bonded labor is lost in an accident during construction, inside the policy period, the underwriters pay the Company everything we'd have been worth to them if we'd lived. Plus everything they'd have had to pay us if we'd lived." He laid the sheet down flat on his knees and smoothed it with both hands. "That's the policy. That's an ordinary policy. Every yard has one. The Combine's had them since the docks; it's how they could afford to build in the first place. That's not the thing."

"Then what's the thing."

He touched the box with his fingertip.

"There's a date on it," he said. "Policies don't have a loss date. You don't know when a loss is going to happen. That's why you insure. That's what insurance is. You pay so that if something happens, someday, you're covered." He tapped the box. "*Projected loss event.* That's a date they know. Two days before the policy runs out. And the policy runs out two days after the Lighting, and the Lighting was moved up to fit." He looked at her. "You see it? Day Two Ninety the policy ends, and after that we're just bonds, and they owe us. Day Two Eighty-Eight we're insured. The Lighting got moved to the last day we're worth more dead."

Sol stood in the middle of Bay Fourteen in the dark.

"Maybe it's a mistake," she said. "Maybe it's—that's the Lighting date, so they wrote it in, that's all. Because that's the riskiest day. Because something might go wrong on the day. Maybe it's routine."

"Maybe," Teague said. He folded the sheet up again along its creases and held it out to her. "You can ask."

"Ask who?"

"Whoever certified the phase instability," said Teague, "that the Lighting got moved up for."

* * *

There was one place on the platform where you were allowed to smoke, and it was the observation blister on top of the admin stack, a bubble of thick glass the size of a bunkroom stuck on the stack's roof like a blister on a thumb, with a scrubber all of its own and a bench around the inside of it. Bonded labor was not permitted. Sol had never been up there. She went up there at the fourth hour of third shift, when the admin stack was dark, by the maintenance ladder in the stack's core, which she knew about because Gang Four had replaced its rungs two years ago; and she came up through the hatch in the blister's floor, quietly, and the air in the blister smelled of smoke.

Haddad was alone on the bench, with her back to the hatch and her knees drawn up, looking out at the Ring.

She did not turn round. From the blister you could see all of it, the whole Ring at once, the way Kostya had seen it from out past the chain: the band of dark metal going round, and the gold lines on its face, and the gangs' work lights crawling on it, more of them than there had ever been, because the shifts were long now and the gangs never stopped; and the Spindle standing up off the rim, small and sharp; and Kettle filling it, turning, sending a long slow sheet of lightning across its face as Sol watched.

There was a thin white stick in Haddad's fingers, burning. She lifted it to her mouth and drew on it and the tip glowed.

"You're not allowed up here, Chief Dace," she said, without turning.

"No."

"I could call Lusk."

"You could."

Haddad drew on the stick again. Then she turned on the bench, slowly, and looked at Sol over her shoulder; and Sol saw her face without a visor for the first time, narrow and dark and lined, with the black eyebrows, and the eyes that had not looked at anybody on the Spindle. They were looking at her now. They were red-rimmed, as though from smoke, or from something else.

Sol took the folded sheet out of her liner and unfolded it and held it up so the doctor could see the blue letterhead.

Haddad looked at it for a long moment. She did not reach for it. She did not look surprised. She looked, Sol thought, like someone who has been waiting for a long time for a ship that is overdue, and has just seen its lights.

"Is it routine?" Sol said. "Teague says it might be routine. That they put the date on because it's the Lighting, and that's the dangerous day. Tell me it's routine, Doctor, and I'll go back down the ladder and you'll never see me again."

Haddad put the white stick out against the glass, carefully, until it was quite dead. Then she stood up. She was shorter than Sol had thought; the white suit had made her bigger.

"Not here," she said.
