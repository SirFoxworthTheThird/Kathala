# Chapter 11: The Instrument

*The Concord · 336 AL*

For eleven days Idris told no one, and went to the Register hall under the Spindle every evening after his shift, and read.

The Register hall was the Keepers' oldest room after the gallery. It ran the whole width of the Spindle's base, a long vaulted cellar lit by oil lamps on chains, and its walls were shelves, floor to vault, and on the shelves were the volumes of the Transit Register, three hundred and thirty-six years of them, bound in grey leather gone black, each with its year stamped on the spine in silver. The oldest were at the far end, in a locked case with a brass grille, in a hand nobody could read. The newest were at the near end, on a long table, open, where the six lanes' current books were brought down at the end of every watch and laid side by side so that the hall's archivist could copy their entries into the master. The archivist was an old Keeper named Brannoch who was nearly deaf and who liked Idris, and who let him sit at the end of the long table with a lamp after the hall had closed, because he thought Idris was doing research for a commentary on the transit tables, and Idris let him think so.

He read the last three months of every lane. He read them slowly, entry by entry, his finger moving down the columns. He did not know what he was looking for until he found it; and then he found it twice more.

Lane Five, forty-one days before Firstlight: *Constancy. Ore barge, returning, empty. 40,000 t.* Initialed *H.D.*, a Keeper of the Fifth Spoke he knew slightly, a careful man with a stammer.

Lane One, thirty-three days before Firstlight: *Benevolence. Ore barge, returning, empty. 40,000 t.* Initialed *A.S.M.*, a Keeper-Adjunct like himself, a woman he had trained with as a novice.

He sat for a long time at the end of the long table with the lamp on its chain swinging slightly above him, though nothing had come through, and looked at those three entries. His own, and two others, in two other hands. Forty thousand tonnes each, ore barges, empty. The same figure, exactly. As though someone had told all three of them what to write.

He thought about Keeper H.D., with his stammer, sitting at the Fifth desk at the dead hour with the lamp swinging over him, watching the dial climb past everything he had ever seen. He wondered what Keeper H.D.'s price had been. Whose name. He thought about Keeper A.S.M., who had once, when they were both fifteen, helped him copy out a phase table he had blotted so that their master would not see. He wondered if she had watched her own hand not shake.

Then he closed the books, and put out the lamp, and went up the long stair to the Archkeeper's study.

* * *

Sallow had sent for him anyway. There was a note on Idris's desk in the gallery that morning, in the Archkeeper's beautiful old Keeper's hand, asking him to call at the end of his watch; and he had read it, and folded it, and put it inside his habit, and gone to the Register hall first, because he had known what he would find and had wanted to be sure.

The old man was at his window with the rug over his knees. He did not turn round when Idris came in. The Eye filled the window, breathing.

"Sit down," Sallow said.

Idris did not sit.

"I've put you on the Firstlight rota," said Sallow. "You, and Marra from the First Spoke, and old Holloway, and Desmond Vane from the Fourth. The four best hands on the governor I've got." He still did not turn. "There's to be a great transit at Firstlight. A convoy of the House, inbound. Very heavy. Many ships at once. The governor will have to be tuned by hand all through it, by four Keepers at once, at the four quarters, as it was in the old days for the colony fleets. I want it done well." He turned his cup on the sill, a quarter turn. "You'll be at the north quarter. By the Inner Housing. Where the grille is."

"How many ships?"

Sallow was quiet.

"How many, Archkeeper? Forty? Fifty? How many abreast?"

The old man turned his head, then, slowly, and looked at him under his heavy lids. Idris saw him see whatever was on his face. Saw him understand it, and decide something.

"Forty-one," said Sallow. "Nine abreast, in the van. Then the rest. Over six hours."

"And what are they carrying?"

"They're House ships, Idris. They're carrying what House ships carry."

"Marines," said Idris. "In armor. In lines of four." He heard his voice shaking and could not stop it. "I've seen them. I saw six hundred of them come off the *Clemency* at the Factor's berth at the twelfth bell, and I stopped counting at six hundred, and they were still coming. And the Prelate was there. Watching. And the Factor." He took the folded note out of his habit and put it down on the sill beside the old man's cup. "And the *Clemency* came through my lane on my watch at three hundred and eighty-eight thousand tonnes, and I wrote her in the Register at forty. Ore barge, returning, empty. In my own hand. With my own seal."

The study was very quiet. Out in the window, the Eye breathed.

Sallow looked at him for a long time. His long sorrowful face did not move at all. Then he closed his eyes.

"Oh, my boy," he said. "Oh, Idris."

"So did Keeper Dunmore on Lane Five. The *Constancy*. And Keeper Sallis-Mead on Lane One. The *Benevolence*. Forty thousand, both of them. Exactly. It's in the books. Brannoch's copied all three into the master and never noticed, because why would he notice, nobody's ever written a false entry in that book in three hundred years." Idris's voice cracked. "Who told them what to write? Who told *me*? A clerk in the Levy Office who eats candied kelp. How did a clerk in the Levy Office know there was a warship coming through my lane on my watch?"

"Because someone told him," said Sallow, with his eyes closed, "who had it from someone in the Factor's House, who had it from the Factor."

"And you knew."

"I knew there would be a great transit at Firstlight. I've known for two months. I didn't know about the *Clemency*." He opened his eyes. "I didn't know they'd been coming through already. I didn't know they'd made my Keepers—" He stopped. He looked, for a moment, very old indeed. "I should have. I should have guessed. Ships abreast. The governor's recovery time. I answered all their questions, Idris, every one, in writing, under seal."

He reached down beside his chair, slowly, and lifted a flat case of black leather from the floor onto his knees, and opened it, and took out a document.

It was a single sheet. Heavy paper, cream, folded in three, with a great seal at its foot in black wax: a ring with a line through it, the House's seal, the same as the small gold disk at Factor Severs's throat. Sallow unfolded it and held it out. Idris took it. His hands were shaking so badly that the paper rattled.

At the top, in the long elegant letters of the Inward:

*AN INSTRUMENT OF CONSOLIDATION*
*between the Most Noble House of Ostrava, holder in trust of the Founding Debt of the Reach,*
*and the Concord of the Reach, debtor,*
*to take effect at Firstlight in the three hundred and thirty-seventh year of the Light.*

He read it. It was not long. It did not need to be.

*Whereas the Concord of the Reach has for three hundred and thirty-six years serviced the Founding Debt by the annual Levy, and whereas the said Levy has proved insufficient to the said Debt, which by the accrual of interest has increased in every year of its service and now stands at a sum greater than the assessed value of the entire Reach—*

*Whereas the Concord, by its Most Luminous Prelate, acknowledges itself unable to discharge the said Debt—*

*Now therefore the House of Ostrava, as holder in trust, calls the Debt, and the Concord, as debtor, consents—*

*That at Firstlight in the three hundred and thirty-seventh year the Concord of the Reach shall be dissolved, and its offices and properties and the Eye and all its works pass into the direct administration of the House, under a Steward of the House's appointment, who shall be the present Prelate—*

*That the annual Levy shall be abolished—*

*That in its stead every person born in the Reach, or resident in the Reach at Firstlight, of whatever age or condition, shall be assessed, and entered upon the rolls of the House as a bond of the House, to serve the said Debt in such place and at such labor as the House shall direct, together with their issue, until the said Debt shall be discharged—*

*That a Squadron of the House shall transit the Eye at Firstlight to secure the said administration and the said assessment, and that the Concord shall render it every assistance—*

*Signed and sealed, for the House: Lucan Aurel Severs, Factor.*
*Signed and sealed, for the Concord: Ottilie Marsh, Most Luminous.*

He read it twice. Then he stood holding it, with the Eye breathing in the window behind the old man's head, and he found that he could not feel his hands.

"Every person," he said.

"Every person."

"*Together with their issue.* Children. Children not born yet."

"Until the Debt is discharged," said Sallow quietly. "Which it never will be. You've read the clause. It's grown every year for three hundred years. It's larger than the Reach. It will always be larger than the Reach, because it has interest and the Reach has only people." He held out his hand for the paper. "Total Assessment, they call it in the Factor's House. I've heard the phrase."

Idris did not give it back.

"Nine million people," he said. "And the moons. Every one of them a bond. Forever. And the Prelate *signed* it."

"The Prelate had very little choice. The Concord's broke, Idris. It's been broke for fifty years. It's lived on the Levy and the tolls and the House's patience, and the House's patience has run out. If she hadn't signed, the Squadron would have come anyway, and she'd have been hanged in the Square, and somebody else would have been made Steward. This way she keeps her rooms in the Basilica." Sallow's hand was still out. "And we keep the Eye. The Keepers. That's in the Instrument too, if you read the back. The Keepers continue in their office, under the House. The Light holds."

"You agreed to this."

"I serve the Light." Sallow's voice did not rise. "So do you. The Light doesn't care who owns it. It doesn't care who comes through it, or what they're carrying, or what they've signed. It breathes. That's all. We keep it breathing. That's all we've ever done."

"Then why did they need me to lie about the *Clemency*?"

"Because the Concord's own Guard doesn't know. The Wardens' Guard. Because the Prelate doesn't want riots in the Square before Firstlight, and if the Guard knew they'd talk, and if the Sump knew there'd be fire in the Spokes for a month. So the House has been bringing its garrison in quietly, a ship at a time, to hold the Hub and the docks and the Basilica when the Squadron comes. So that there's no trouble." Sallow lowered his hand at last. "And for that they needed three Keepers to write three wrong numbers in a book. And they found three. And I didn't know." He looked down at his knees, at the rug, at the black stick lying across it. "I'll tell you something, Idris. I've been a Keeper for fifty-six years. I'd have said, before this morning, that there wasn't a Keeper in the order who could be bought. Not one. I'd have staked my line on it." He was quiet. "What did they give you?"

Idris could not answer.

"It was the girl," Sallow said. "Wasn't it. Your sister."

"Yes."

"Of course it was." The old man nodded slowly. "Of course."

They stayed like that for a long time, the old man in his chair with his hands on the rug, and Idris standing in the middle of the study with the Instrument in his hand. Out in the window the Eye breathed, and once, very far off and very small, a ship came up out of its center like a fish rising, and the governor took the weight of it under their feet, and let it go.

"What will you do?" Sallow said at last.

"I don't know."

"Don't do anything," said Sallow. "Please. I'm asking you. As your master. As the man who taught you the invocation. Don't do anything. There's nothing to be done. It's signed. It's sealed. The Squadron's waiting at Ostrava for Firstlight, and the garrison's in the Hub, and in twenty-four days it will all be over, and then it will just be how things are. The Light will hold. It always holds." He looked up. "You've already done one thing you can't take back. Don't do another."

Idris folded the Instrument along its creases, very carefully, and handed it back to him. Sallow took it, and held it on his knee, and did not put it away.

"Who else knows?" Idris said.

"The Prelate. The Factor. Some of the Factor's people. Me." Sallow paused. "And now you."

"Not the Square."

"No. Not the Square."

"Not the Sump."

Sallow looked at him for a long time.

"No," he said. "Not the Sump."

Idris bowed, from the waist, with his hand on his father's line, as a Keeper bows to the Archkeeper. Then he turned and went out of the study, and the old man did not call him back.

* * *

He knocked on the door under *ABANDON HOPE* at the second bell of third watch, and Teodor Gaunt opened it a hand's width and looked at him, and this time did not turn to ask anyone. He opened the door wide.

"She said you'd come back," he said.

The room was full again. More of them, this time. Twenty, twenty-five, sitting on the floor in the lamplight, young and not so young; there was an old man with a docker's hook for a left hand, and a woman with a baby asleep inside her coat, and two girls in the green smocks of the Fourth Spoke's water levels who could not have been more than fifteen. They all looked at him as he came in. They did not look away this time. He picked his way between them toward the far wall, toward the marks, and Lark was there, sitting under them where she had sat before, with her back against the wall; and she looked up at him, and her face was still and very tired, and she did not say *go home*.

"You found something," she said.

"Yes."

"Tell them."

He told them.

He stood in the middle of the old bunkroom with the lamps on the floor round his feet and the marks on the wall behind him and the milk-white port at the end, and he told them everything. The *Clemency* and her mass and what he had written. The *Constancy* and the *Benevolence*, and the two Keepers' initials. The lines of armored men coming down the ramps at the twelfth bell, four abreast, and the small figures in black and in white-and-gold watching from the berth's edge. The Firstlight rota. Forty-one ships, nine abreast in the van. And the Instrument: he had read it twice and he found he could say it nearly word for word, and he did, standing there, in Sallow's beautiful old study voice without meaning to, *whereas*, and *whereas*, and *now therefore*, down to *every person born in the Reach, or resident in the Reach at Firstlight, of whatever age or condition*, down to *together with their issue*.

When he stopped, nobody spoke.

The woman with the baby had put her hand over its head inside her coat.

"Together with their issue," said Teodor Gaunt, at last. He said it slowly, as though he were reading it off a wall. "That's a nice way to put it."

"It's the House's way," said Idris.

"I know whose way it is." Teodor looked at him. In the lamplight the scar through his eyebrow was white. "My grandmother's grandmother went Inward in the Levy, Keeper. In the year two hundred and something. So did her brother. They didn't come back. We've been dodging the Drum in my family for four generations. That's what we are, all of us in this room—you know that? You know what this is? This isn't a—" he waved a hand at the room, the lamps, the people. "This isn't a *club*. We're Levy-dodgers. The Unlevied. Some of us were drawn and ran. Some of us have families who ran so long ago they've got no ward-numbers, nothing, they're not in the Drum because the Concord doesn't know they're alive. We move people. We get them off the cord and down to the docks and onto ore barges and out to the mines on Gall, or the ice on Brine, where the Concord doesn't look. We've been doing it a hundred years." He looked round at the room. "We knew something was coming. Since the summer. Ships in the Factor's berth at night. Guard rotations changed, the Hub locked down in places nobody could say why. We knew. We didn't know what."

"Now you know," said Idris.

"Now we know." Teodor turned back to him. "And why do *you* want us to know, Keeper? You wrote the ship down wrong for your sister's name. You've got her name back. You're done. Why come down here and tell us?"

Idris did not have an answer that would fit in words. He stood in the lamplight with all of them looking at him.

"Because there isn't any getting her name back," he said finally. "Not now. Not from that. *Every person.* There isn't a reconciliation for every person. There isn't a reserve list long enough." He looked at Lark. "I paid to take her name out of the Drum. In twenty-four days they're going to throw the Drum away and write her name on a roll she'll never come off, and her children's, and theirs. Everybody's. Benno Kask's mother's. Yours." He looked back at Teodor. "I didn't come down here to help you. I came down here because there isn't anywhere else to go."

Teodor looked at him a long time.

Then he looked at Lark.

And Lark got up, slowly, off the floor under the marks, and came and stood beside her brother in the middle of the room, close enough that her shoulder touched his arm through his habit; and she looked at Teodor and at the rest of them, and said: "He's on the Firstlight rota. At the governor. North quarter. By the Housing." Her voice was steady. "He can get into the Spindle. Nobody else in this room can do that. Nobody else in the Sump. Nobody else in Tithe who'll talk to us." She paused. "So he's ours now. Aren't you, Idris?"

He looked down at her.

"Yes," he said.

* * *

They talked until the fifth bell. Plans, and half-plans, and plans that were nothing but anger with a shape on it. Idris listened to most of it without speaking. He did not know how to talk about such things. He knew the governor, and the gauges, and the Register, and the Rule; he did not know how many Guards stood at the Basilica's east gate or which ore barges' captains could be bought or what the Wardens' Guard would do if the Sump came up the service stairs with knives, and they did, and they argued about it in low voices, round and round, and he stood in the middle of it with his hands in his sleeves and watched their faces in the lamplight, and thought about Sallow's hands on the rug.

When it broke up, and they went out in twos and threes into the corridor, Lark stayed. So did he.

She sat down again under the marks, and after a moment he sat down beside her, on the cold floor, with his back against the old grey wall. He had not sat on a floor since he was a novice. His knees hurt.

"You lied in the book," she said, without looking at him.

"Yes."

"The Register. The holy one. Three hundred years, nobody's ever—you did that."

"Yes."

"For me."

"Yes."

She was quiet for a long time. The lamps on the floor had burned low and nobody had trimmed them, and the light had gone gold and small, and the marks on the wall above them were mostly in shadow now, except the lowest rows, the small ones, in their tens.

"Don't ever do anything for me again," Lark said.

"All right."

"I mean it. Not ever. I don't want to be a thing somebody does something for. I don't want to be the reason somebody's name goes on the cord. I'd rather go myself. I'd rather go a hundred times."

"I know."

"You don't know. You've never—" She stopped. She put her head back against the wall and shut her eyes. "Benno's going to the pens," she said. "Day after tomorrow. That's when they take the drawn. Ten days before Firstlight they go into the Levy pens at the Hub docks, by the berth where the *Remittance* is, and they don't come out again till they go aboard her. I went to see him. At the stall." She did not open her eyes. "I bought a cake. He gave me extra salt. He doesn't know who I am. He doesn't know anything. He thinks he's going to fly."

"He told me."

She opened her eyes and looked at him.

"You went," she said.

"Yes."

"And you didn't tell him."

"No."

"No," Lark said. "Neither did I."

They sat together on the floor of the old bunkroom in the Sump with their backs against the wall and the lamps going out round their feet. After a while, without looking, Lark reached up behind her and put her fingertips on the marks; and after another while, Idris did the same. The wall was cold. The little ridges of the scratches were rough under his fingers, very slightly, the way a scar is rough, and he found the bottom rows by feel, the small ones, in their tens, the ones some long-dead hand had cut to count the days it owed, and he ran his fingers along them in the dark, one row and the next and the next, and somewhere toward the end of them he found one with a deep hard line cut under it, deeper than any of the others, so deep his fingertip fit inside it.

"Feel that one," he said.

"I know," said Lark. "Everybody knows that one. The kids put their finger in it for luck. Teodor says it's where they stopped." She took her hand down. "Nobody knows what happened that day."

"No," said Idris, and left his fingertip in the cut for a long time, as though it might tell him.

"In the Square," he said. "When the cord went on. The Guard said *bit early for that*, and you said *not really, as it turns out*." He took his hand down from the wall. "You saw it before I did. All of it. Didn't you?"

"Not the Instrument," said Lark. "Not *together with their issue*." She looked at the dead lamps. "But the shape of it. Yes. Everybody down here's seen the shape of it all their lives. It's only you up there who didn't." She leaned her head, very slightly, against his shoulder. "Now you see it."

"Now I see it," said Idris.
