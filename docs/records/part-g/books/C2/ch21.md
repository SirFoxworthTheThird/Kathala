# Chapter 21: Count It Back Down

*The Dark · Year 419*

There is a stair inside the Spindle. I did not know that, before. Nobody in Tithe knew it. It goes up from the door in the foot, through the whole height of the tower, nine hundred meters, round and round inside a shaft of black metal so narrow that two people cannot pass on it; and every step of it is cut with a pattern of little squares so that a boot will grip, and every step of it is coated in frost, so that a boot will not. There is no air in it. There has been no air in it for four hundred and nineteen years. We climbed it in our suits, by the light of our lamps, the four of us: the Reeve first, with his gun, and then me, and then Ness, and then the young soldier, whose name was Aske, carrying the Reeve's relay on his back in its canvas, its long folded spike knocking against the shaft wall at every turn.

It took us eleven hours. I counted them. I counted the steps, too, for a while, to keep my mind off Ness behind me; and then I stopped counting the steps and counted her breathing instead, on the private channel. It was bad. The patch on her side was holding, she said, every time I asked; and every time I asked, her voice was a little thinner, and she stopped a little longer on the landings, and once, at the six-hundredth meter or so, she sat down on a step and did not get up until I went back down to her and took her hands and pulled.

"Small," she said. "It's a small one, Tally. I've had worse."

"You keep saying that."

"Because it keeps being true." She got up. She leaned on me. "Go on. I'm behind you. I'm always behind you."

And all the way up, through the frost and the dark and the black metal of the shaft, the knock. Louder than it had been on the Halo. Louder than anything. It was not in my boots now; it was in the walls, in the steps, in the air of my helmet, in the bones of my face, so loud that by the end I could not tell it from my own heart, and I stopped trying. *Knock-knock knock-knock. Knock. Knock-knock.* A run and a stop. A run and a stop. It had been knocking for a hundred years, the Reeve said, and getting louder. Here, inside the Spindle, it sounded as if whoever was knocking was standing just on the other side of the wall.

At the top the stair ended in a door, and the Reeve opened it, and we came out into the gallery.

* * *

I had seen it from outside. A band of glass round the Spindle's waist, from the Halo, from Brine. It is not a band. It is a room. A long room, curving, as long as the dock of Hold Fourteen, with a wall of glass all down one side, and a floor of glass under your feet, and on the other side a wall of brass, gone green and black, covered from end to end in dials. Hundreds of them. Behind little round windows of glass, most of them cracked, with needles frozen at the bottoms of their faces. Lamps hung from the ceiling on chains, dead, crusted with frost. Along the brass rail under the window there were long rotten strips of something that had been white once, cloth, hanging in rags, stiff with ice.

And through the window, and through the floor, the Halo.

We were above it. That was the thing. We had walked along it for seven days, and climbed for eleven hours, and now we were above it, three-quarters of the way up the Spindle, looking down; and from here you could see it the way you could not see it from anywhere else. All of it, at once. The great dark ring going round, its gold lines dull, curving away on both sides and meeting far off on the other side, small, a thread. And under us, through the floor of glass, Kettle, turning, enormous, with its storms. And inside the ring, filling it, where the Eye had been—

Nothing. That was what it was. A great round nothing, the size of the world, rimmed with dead gold, with Kettle on one side of it and the stars on the other. And in the nothing, very small, very far off, tumbling slowly, pieces. Long dark shapes, broken off clean at one end. Nine of them, I thought. Turning over and over in the middle of the empty ring, as they must have been turning for four hundred years, glittering a little at their broken ends where Kettle's light caught them.

The Reeve stood at the window and looked at them for a long time.

"Wrecks," he said. "The walkers' stories. I always thought they made them up."

There was someone else in the gallery.

I saw him when I turned from the window. At the far end of the long curving room, at the last bank of dials, sitting on the glass floor with his back against the brass. An old man. Very old, I think, when it happened; you could tell by the shape of him, even now, even so long after. He was all bone, in robes that had been grey and silver and were still, a little, under the frost. There was a stick lying across his knees, black. There were cords round his neck, two of them, old, braided, furred with age and ice, lying over the grey and silver like a stole. And over his heart, in the grey and silver, a hole.

"Keeper," said the Reeve, beside me, quietly.

I did not answer. I stood and looked at him. He was sitting very straight, with his head a little tipped back against the dials, as though he had been looking up at something. He had been sitting like that for four hundred and nineteen years. Nobody had come to sing him out.

I did not know who he was. I touched the string at my throat, the Tally's string, and I said under my breath the first two lines of the Count, the down way, *ten for the Company, ten and stop*, the way you say it for the dead when you cannot say it all; and I turned away from him, toward the other end of the gallery, where the Reeve was already walking, with his gun.

Toward the grille.

* * *

It was lying on the floor. A great square panel of brass lattice, taller than a man, green at its edges, bent almost double in the middle as though someone had wrenched it off the wall with a bar and thrown it down. Behind where it had been, set into a wall of black steel that went from the gallery's floor to its ceiling, there was a thing I knew, because the Reeve had told me its name in his cabin on the *Patience*, under the glass, from a page of a burned book.

The Knot.

It was not much to look at. That was what I thought, and I was ashamed of thinking it. A round head of old steel, as wide as a man's thigh, set flush into the black wall, with a square socket in the middle of it and a short fat pin through it crosswise, through the head and into the wall beside. Three little lines scratched into the wall next to it, side by side, very fine. And beside it, a round plate the size of a cart wheel, with a ring of holes round its edge, ten of them; and in four of the holes, bolts, at the four points of a cross; and in the other six, nothing.

And at the foot of the wall, on a ledge of steel, three things.

Six bolts, in a little heap, frozen together. A great old driver, a meter and a half of dark steel, worn bright at its grip. And a flask.

I knelt down and picked up the flask.

I do not know why I did that first. It was the smallest thing there. It was steel, old and heavy and dull grey, worn smooth at its shoulders where hands had held it, with a dent in one side that fit under my thumb as though it had been made for my thumb. It was so cold through my glove that my fingers ached. I turned it over, because there were marks on the base, and I can read the old letters, and I read them.

*TTH-9 · 0417.*

"Tally," said the Reeve.

I looked up. He was standing over me with the gun in his hand, not pointed at anything, his face grey behind his visor, his breath bad on the channel.

"I've brought you to the door," he said. "Sing."

"Not yet."

"Tally—"

"Not yet." I stood up, with the flask in my hand. "There's writing. Behind the plate. There has to be."

"How do you know?"

I did not know. I did not know how I knew. I knew the way I had known the knock, in the phase-ear under the ice, like a word on the tip of your tongue. I looked at the plate, at its ten holes, at its four bolts at the four points of a cross; and I thought of the inner door of the lock at the end of the Fourteen corridor, and the wheel, and a quarter-turn on every line, and the dogs of the door sliding into their seats all round the rim. And I thought of the numbers of the Count. Ten of them. *Two for the line and one for the hook.* They had never rhymed in the low numbers, and Quill had said that was how you knew they were old and true: that they were made for a purpose we did not remember.

Ten numbers. Ten holes.

"Give me the driver," I said to Aske.

He looked at the Reeve. The Reeve looked at me for a long moment, and then nodded. Aske bent and picked up the driver off the ledge, grunting at the weight of it, and gave it to me.

It fit the bolts. All four of them. I put it on the first one and leaned, and the bolt turned, stiff, with a squeal I felt through my gloves; and the second, and the third, and the fourth. They were only hand-tight. Whoever had put them in had been in a hurry. I took them out and put them on the ledge with the other six. And then I put my hands on the plate's edge and pulled, and it swung back on its hinge, slowly, heavy, grinding, and as it swung its inside face turned toward me, into the light of my lamp.

It was covered in writing.

* * *

There were two hands.

That was the first thing I saw. I am a Tally; I have been reading the knots of the dead since I was seven, and I know when two people have tied the same string. The top of the plate was one hand. Big square letters, the old kind, like the stencils on the walls of Hold Fourteen, cut deep, very deep, into the steel, by someone with a hard sharp point and a great deal of time. And under that, smaller, not so deep, scratchier, as though cut fast with a blade that was not meant for it, another.

I read the first.

IF YOU ARE READING THIS YOU OPENED IT RIGHT.

WE BUILT THIS. 412 OF US. BONDED. THE COMBINE MEANT TO BURN US AT FIRST LIGHT FOR THE INSURANCE AND WE MADE THEM STOP. THEY PUT US ON FOR LIFE INSTEAD.

THIS IS A LOCK. IT WORKS FROM THIS SIDE ONLY. COUNT IT UP TO LOCK. COUNT IT BACK DOWN TO OPEN. THE SONG IS THE KEY. WE ALL HAVE IT.

DON'T LET THEM OWN THE DOOR. LOCK IT IF YOU HAVE TO.

FIRST LIGHT IS A WAVE. GET BEHIND THE STEEL.

And under that—I put my glove on it, on the cold steel, as if it were the First Count, as if I were seven—the Count.

All of it. The numbers, and then the Door. The way I had never heard it. Going *up*. *One for the hook and two for the line, three for the dog and four for the time.* Rhyming, all the way. Every line. And then the Door, the way I had never heard it either: *Sun on your left and the Kettle below. Dog to the notch where the old marks show. Twice to the dark and once to the light. Seat it and pin it and turn it tight. That is the way that you lock the door. Count it back down and it's open once more.* And beside the Door, two little curved arrows cut into the steel, one marked DARK and one LIGHT.

And under the Count, a name.

M. DACE. 0417. GANG FOUR. RIGGER.

I looked down at the flask in my other hand. *TTH-9 · 0417.*

AND DOV DACE 0418. AND KOSTYA LEM. AND ABRAM TEAGUE. AND BRACKEN. WHO HELD ON.

I stood there in front of the open plate with the flask in my hand and I could not breathe.

*Four hundred and twelve of us.* Bonded. *They put us on for life.* Six to a room, in bunks, three high; the brackets on the walls of the Tallies' room in Hold Fourteen where Quill said the bunks had been, before the Light, when people came out here to build things. *C-14*, in yellow paint gone brown, over the door. *Abandon hope*, scratched under it so small you had to stand on the stool. Quill said the people who made the room were people who had a sense of humor, which he said was a kind of courage. And on the wall by the bottom bunk on the right, nearest the port, the marks. The First Count. Hundreds of them, in rows and blocks, very neat, some with lines through; and at the bottom a second set, smaller, finer, in rows of ten, and one of them with a deep line cut under it so that a child's fingertip fit inside. The hullwalkers said they were an old prisoner's sentence. The Lamplighters said they were the days until the Light came back. The Tallies said only that they were the First Count, and put their girls' fingers on them, one by one, at seven.

It was her. I knew it the way I had known the knock, before I knew it. M. DACE. 0417. It had always been her, on the wall by the bottom bunk. Counting the days she owed.

She had not got to the end of them.

"Tally," said the Reeve. "What does it say?"

I did not answer him. I read the second hand.

I READ YOU, M. DACE.

THE HOUSES CAME TO BOND US ALL, EVERY CHILD BORN. I LET 2,000 OF OURS GO THROUGH FIRST ON THE LEVY SHIP AND LOCKED IT ON THEIR WARSHIPS. NINE. THE CITY IS GOING DARK AS I WRITE.

I turned my head and looked out of the window at the nine dark shapes tumbling slowly in the middle of the empty ring, glittering at their broken ends. Then I looked back.

MY SISTER LARK WENT THROUGH. SHE HAS THE SONG. SHE WILL TEACH IT ON THE OTHER SIDE.

I AM GOING DOWN TO TEACH IT ON THIS ONE. THE WAY BACK.

FORGIVE THE DARK.

I. VEY. KEEPER. FIRSTLIGHT 337.

BENNO KASK. PILOT.

*Who came up out of the Dark when the Dark was new.* I said it in my head, the end of the lineage, the way I had said it every day that winter for Quill, lying on his bunk under the marks with his blind eyes open. *And would not give his name. And taught the children to count.*

He had given it. He had given it here. To the door. And then he had gone down into the dark city with nine million people in it and their lamps, and sat down somewhere, and taught the children the Count. Backward. *The way back.* Starting at ten. The way that does not rhyme in the low numbers, which is how you know it is the true way. And two rules. Never sing it on the Halo. Never let only one person know it.

*Count it back down*, the Tallies sing at every lock, every funeral, with their hand on the cold steel wheel, *we are home once more.*

I had sung it a hundred times. I had sung Pim home, and the baby, and the boy of three, and Quill, alone, with Ness holding my hand. And the Door at the end of it, the part we call the Door: *once to the dark and twice to the light*. Backward from this. Exactly backward. The way you would undo it, if you had done it. *Count it back down to open.*

We had been singing it open. At every funeral. For four hundred and nineteen years. We just never sang it here.

And Quill's lie on the dock floor at Fourteen, the Door he had sung false for the Reeve—*twice to the dark and once to the light, seat it and pin it and turn it tight*—had not been false at all. It had been this. The other way round. The locking way. I do not know whether he knew. I think now that he did not; I think that if you tell a lie by turning a true thing round, it will sometimes come out true. But I have never been sure.

* * *

I read it aloud.

I had not meant to. The Reeve asked me again what it said, and I turned round with the flask in my hand and my back to the open plate, and I read it to him, all of it, both hands, in the old letters, in the voice I use for the Count, because I did not have another one. The Reeve of Brine stood in front of me in the dead gallery with the gun in his hand, not pointed at anything, and listened. Aske listened. Ness, sitting on the glass floor against the brass wall a little way off with her hand pressed to her side, listened.

When I had finished, the Reeve did not say anything for a long time.

Then he began to laugh.

It was not a good laugh. It turned into coughing almost at once, and the coughing went on, bad, bending him over, and when it stopped he had to put his gloved hand against the black steel of the wall to stay up. But it had been a laugh.

"Four hundred and twelve bonds," he said. "I thought I'd be the first man to hold it." He shook his head slowly inside his helmet. "It was never the Founders'. Never the Keepers'. Never anybody's. It was theirs. A gang of riggers on a life sentence." He looked at the open plate. "*Don't let them own the door.*"

"You wanted to own it," I said.

"I wanted to hold it." He lifted his head and looked at me. "There's a difference."

"Not to them." I lifted my chin at the plate. "Not to her. Not to the man who let his sister go through and locked it behind her." I held his eyes. "Not to the children in Fourteen breathing the black because you sent them a box."

He did not look away. I will give him that. He never looked away from anything he had done. He stood in the dead gallery with his hand on the wall and looked at me, and his pale grey eyes behind his visor were tired and steady.

"Sing it, Tally," he said quietly. "And then whatever you think of me, it won't matter. There'll be lungs."

"I'll sing it," I said.

And I turned to Aske, and said: "Put up the relay."

The Reeve's eyes changed.

"You wanted every Hold in Tithe to hear," I said. "When it opened. So they'd know who. You said so. In the hold of the *Patience*." I did not look away from him either. "So put it up. Let them hear."

He understood. I saw him understand it. It came into his face the way the knock had come into my feet on the Halo: slowly, and then all at once. A key you can copy isn't a key. It's a rumor.

He lifted the gun.

"Tally," he said. "Don't."

"Then shoot me," I said. "And then shoot Ness. She knows it. I taught her, in the Tallies' room, after the old man died. She can sing it forward and backward and out of order. She puts the dog before the pin when she's tired." I heard Ness, on the floor, make a sound that was nearly a laugh. "And then shoot Aske, because he's heard the plate. And then you'll be up here alone with the Knot and a page of a burned book and a song you only know the half of. And you'll have to sing it yourself. Here. On the Halo. With nobody to hear." I held out the driver to him, the great old driver with its worn bright grip, across the space between us. "Or you can put the gun down. And I'll sing it. To everybody. And it'll never be in only one head again. Not mine. Not yours. Not anybody's."

He looked at the driver. He looked at me.

Through the floor, through the black steel of the wall, through the soles of my boots and the bones of my face, the knock went on. *Knock-knock knock-knock.* A run and a stop. I had been hearing it for so long that I had stopped listening to it; and I listened to it now, standing there with the driver held out, because it was so loud, here, by the Knot, that it was the only thing in the world.

*Knock-knock knock-knock. Knock. Knock-knock.*

*One for the hook and two for the line.*

I heard it. Under my breath, in my helmet, without meaning to, I had said it: the first line of the Count off the plate, the way it was cut there. Going *up*. The way no Tally had sung it in four hundred and nineteen years. *ONE for the HOOK and TWO for the LINE.* And the knock, under it, through the steel—

It fit.

Every beat. Every stop. *THREE for the DOG and FOUR for the TIME.* The run of knocks for the line, and the stop at the end of it where you breathe. *FIVE for the AIR and SIX for the SEAL.* I had never known it because we sing it down, ten to one, the way the First Tally taught the children; and down, it goes another way, and does not rhyme in the low numbers, and has another beat. But up. Up, the way M. Dace cut it in the steel, the way, I know now, the riggers sang it on the Ring with their hands on the drivers—up, it was the knock. It had always been the knock. Whoever was on the other side of the door had been knocking the Count. Counting it *up*. For a hundred years. Over and over. Louder and louder. Asking.

*She has the song. She will teach it on the other side.*

"They know it," I said.

The Reeve stared at me.

"On the other side. Whoever's knocking. They know the Count. They've been knocking it. Listen." I sang it, out loud, on the open channel, up, *one for the hook and two for the line*, and stopped, and in the stop, through the floor, the knock went on, *knock-knock knock-knock*, on the beat, exactly, like an answer. "His sister went through. With two thousand people. And the song. Four hundred and nineteen years ago." I could not stop my voice from shaking. "And they kept it. Like we did. They've been knocking it on the door for a hundred years, and we didn't know, because we sing it the other way." I looked at the Reeve. "It's not your Founders. It's not the Houses. It's not Cinder's dead with their lamps. It's them. It's her people. They want to come home."

The Reeve of Brine stood in front of me with the gun in his hand and listened to the knock.

Then, very slowly, he lowered it. He did not drop it. He lowered it to his side, and stood there, and listened. And after a long time he turned his head and looked at Aske.

"Put up the relay," he said.

* * *

Aske put it up by the window. He unfolded its long spike and set its black box on the glass floor and turned it on, and a little red light came up on its face, and then a green one; and the Reeve, without saying anything, unclipped a cable from his own suit and plugged it into the box's side, and held out his hand to me, and I unclipped mine and gave it to him, and he plugged mine in too. And then the light on the side of my helmet that meant *everybody* came on.

I did not know how many would hear. Every Hold in Tithe that still had one of the Reeve's little black boxes, that he had left with the Holds' bosses when he went round them hiring walkers. Every district in Brine. The *Patience*, on her belly on the Halo thirty-one kilometers round the rim, with whoever was left alive in her hold. Veska, in Hold Fourteen, with her box on Quill's empty bunk. Anybody.

I took the breath all the way down, into my belly, the way Quill taught me. I let it out.

"This is the Tally of Hold Fourteen," I said. "I'm at the Spindle. On the Halo. At the Knot." My voice sounded very strange to me. Very small. "I'm going to sing you the Count. All of it. Both ways. Listen to it and learn it. All of you. Whoever's listening. Learn it and teach it to somebody, now, today, before you do anything else. Before you eat. It's the key to the Halo. It's always been the key. It's not mine and it's not the Reeve's. It's yours." I stopped. I looked at the plate. "It was made by four hundred and twelve people who built the Halo and were never let go home. And it was kept by a man who closed the door so the Houses couldn't bond every child born in the Reach. And now there's someone on the other side, knocking. And they know it too."

I sang it.

I sang it up first, the way it was cut in the steel. The numbers, all of them, rhyming. The Door, the locking way. And then I sang it down, the way the Tallies sing it, the way the First Tally taught the children in the dark: ten to one, not rhyming at the bottom; and the Door backward, the opening way. And then I said, plainly, in my ordinary voice, what each line meant: which way to stand, where the dog went, which way the dark was and which the light, the pin, the quarter-turn. And then I sang it again. Both ways. And again.

And the third time, on the channel, faint, I heard someone come in under me.

Ness. On the floor against the brass wall, with her hand on her side. In her voice like a cracked pipe, flat and terrible, singing a quarter-beat late. *Dog to the notch where the old ones go.* She got the dog before the pin. I did not correct her.

And then someone else. I did not know who. A woman's voice, very far off, on the band, crackling. And another. And a child's. And somewhere, a long way off, a man's, deep and broken, that I thought, I will always think, was Barrow's boy. Voices coming in on the band from all over the dark, from the Holds, from Brine, from wherever there was a box with a red light on it, getting the words wrong and putting them right, over and over, the way you learn a song in a crowd. Singing it up. Singing it down. Singing it to each other.

I sang it with them until it was everybody's. Then I stopped.

"There's one more thing," I said. "It's written here. On the door. By the people who built it." I looked at the plate. "*First light is a wave. Get behind the steel.* When I open it, something comes out. A wave. It'll kill anybody it finds. So get behind the steel. All of you. The deep levels, the old reactor shielding, wherever your Hold goes when Kettle flares. Walkers, come in off the hull. *Patience*, get behind your water tanks." I breathed. "I'll count it back down at the next bell. That's one hour. Get behind the steel."

Then I reached up to the side of my helmet and turned off *everybody*.

* * *

There were things to do in the hour, and I did them.

I went to Ness first. She was sitting against the brass wall where she had been, with her hand on her side, and her face behind her visor was very white, and she was smiling.

"You sang it to everybody," she said.

"Yes."

"The old man's going to haunt you."

"The old man said never let it be in only one head." I sat down beside her on the glass floor and put my shoulder against her shoulder. "I don't think he meant two."

She laughed, and it hurt her, and she stopped.

"Can you walk?" I said. "There's a way into the steel. Behind the Knot. There has to be. The plate says *get behind the steel*, and that's the most steel I've ever seen."

"I can walk." She looked at me. "You're going to do it yourself. The turning."

"Yes."

"Not him."

"Not him."

She nodded. Then she took her hand off her side, slowly, and looked at the patch, and put it back. "Do you need two hands on that driver? It's big."

"Yes."

"Then I'll be the other one," said Ness.

Aske found the way in. A crawl hatch, round the back of the black steel wall, where the gallery curved away out of sight behind the Housing; round, small, with a wheel on it like the inner door of the lock at the end of the Fourteen corridor. He opened it and looked in with his lamp and came back and said it was a room, small, steel, with old instruments on the walls and a grey box in the middle with three cables going into it, and nothing else; and that the walls were thicker than his arm was long.

Then I did the last thing.

I had nothing to cut with. M. Dace had a hard sharp point; the Keeper had a blade. I had a walker's awl, from Ness's thigh pocket, a stub of hardened steel for punching holes in suit patches, and it was not meant for this either. I knelt in front of the open plate, under the second hand, under BENNO KASK, PILOT, and cut anyway. It squealed in the steel. I felt it in my teeth. My letters were worse than either of theirs. Smaller, and shallower, and not straight. Quill taught me to read the old letters, not to make them.

WE ARE COUNTING IT BACK DOWN. THEY ARE KNOCKING THE SONG FROM THE OTHER SIDE.

EVERYONE HAS THE KEY NOW. EVERYONE.

And then my name. I thought about it for a while before I cut it. A Tally gives up her name when she takes the string. Quill had made me say it once more, and then put it away. But the Keeper had given his here, to the door, and M. Dace had given hers, and her brother's, and the names of the ones who held on. And I thought that if the door was everyone's now, then the people who opened it ought to be named on it, so that whoever came next would know that it had been people, and not Founders.

MIRA, CALLED TALLY. HOLD FOURTEEN. TAUGHT BY QUILL. YEAR 419 OF THE DARK.

AND NESS, WHO WALKED. AND BROTHER CINDER, WHO SANG. AND MARROW. WHO STOOD UP.

I looked at it for a long time in my lamp.

When I stood up, the Reeve was at the window.

He had not moved, while I cut. He had stood at the long window with his back to us and his gun hanging in his hand, looking out at the great empty ring with the nine dark shapes tumbling in it, and Kettle under his feet through the glass, and the stars. He did not turn round when I came up beside him.

"You'll want to go behind the steel," he said.

"So will you."

He shook his head.

"I've got a year, Tally," he said. "Maybe less. You've heard me breathe. And I've lived in the dark my whole life. My father did, and his. Everybody I've ever known." He looked out at the empty ring. "I've read every scrap there is about the Light. I've listened to the phase-ear every day for five years. I walked here. I've buried four thousand two hundred and eleven people." He was quiet. "I'd like to see it."

"It'll kill you."

"Probably." He turned his head and looked at me. "You'll have to tell them I said sorry. In Fourteen. About the lung. I don't suppose it'll help."

"No," I said.

"No," the Reeve of Brine agreed. He looked back out of the window. "Go on, Tally. Go and count it."

I left him there.

* * *

The bell came. There are no bells in the Spindle, and there were none on the *Patience*, and there were none on the Halo; but I had been counting, the way a Tally counts, the way you count the First Count in the dark at seven, without missing, and I knew when it was the bell.

I stood in front of the Knot.

I turned round, where I stood. Until the long window was on my left. And through it, past the curve of the Halo, small and dim and red as a banked coal, Hearth. And under my feet, through the floor of glass, filling everything, Kettle.

*Sun on your left and the Kettle below.*

Ness was beside me. She had got up off the floor, somehow, and come, with her hand on her side. Aske was at the crawl hatch round the back, holding it open, waiting. I put the old driver's squared head into the Knot's socket. It went in to the shoulder, snug, as though it had come home. Ness put her hands on the crossbar beside mine.

Through the steel, through the driver, through our gloves, the knock. *Knock-knock knock-knock.* Counting up. Asking.

"Ten," I said, "for the Company. Ten, and stop."

I did not sing it to everybody. I did not need to. Everybody had it now. I sang it to Ness, and to the door, and to whoever was knocking, and to the old man at the far end of the gallery with the cords round his neck, and to Pim and Quill and the baby and the boy of three, and to M. Dace, 0417, who had not got to the end of them. As though I were speaking to someone just beside me.

The turning first. The last thing she did, undone first. *Turn it tight* was a quarter; so a quarter back. We leaned on it together and the Knot gave, a quarter, with a sound deep in the wall like a door's dogs coming out of their seats.

*Pin it.* I took the pin out. The short fat pin, through the Knot's head and into the wall beside it. It was frozen. I put my thumb on it and pushed, and it did not move, and Ness put her thumb beside mine and pushed, and it came, all at once, and fell out of the hole and tumbled away across the glass floor, and I felt it ring through my boots where it struck.

*Once to the dark.* I looked at the arrows on the plate. DARK. We turned it, the two of us, once, all the way round. The Knot moved in the wall. Something under the floor, in the steel, deep, shifted.

*And twice to the light.*

Once. The governor—I did not know the word then; I know it now; I have read it since in a dead Keeper's book—the governor, deep in the black wall, under the floor, in the Spindle's belly, took a breath. I felt it. A hair one way.

Twice.

And a hair back.

*Dog to the notch where the old ones go.* We pulled. The Knot came out of the wall, a hand's width, grinding, slowly, the way a tooth comes, until its head stood out of the black steel beside the three little scratched lines, where it had stood before the Keeper came, through the whole of the Light; where M. Dace had put it, waiting.

And stopped.

The knock stopped.

All at once. In the middle of a run. *Knock-knock knock*—and nothing. The silence came up through the floor like cold water. I had heard that knock in my boots and my teeth and my bones for seven days. I had heard it through the phase-ear under the ice, and in Ness's fingers tapping on her leg under the dome. It stopped, and the silence was so loud that I swayed.

And then, under the silence, the Spindle began to sing.

A deep note, rising. In the steel and the glass and the floor of my belly. A note nobody alive had ever heard it make.

"*Behind the steel*," said Ness.

We ran. Not small and slow. We ran, round the back of the Housing, Ness with her hand on her side and me with my arm round her and the old driver left standing in the Knot's socket where we had let go of it, and the flask—I had the flask. I did not remember picking it up off the ledge. I had it in my hand. Aske had the crawl hatch open and his hand out and he pulled Ness through and then me, and we fell into the dark inside, the three of us, on a floor of steel, and Aske spun the wheel, and the hatch shut, and the dogs of it slid home all round the rim.

It was very small. It was the darkest place I have ever been. Darker than the Tallies' room with the lamp out. Four meters of steel on every side, and nothing coming through it. The note of the Spindle went up and up around us, through the walls, through our suits, through our bones. Ness was beside me with her head against my shoulder. I had the flask in my lap. It was so cold.

*Count it back down*, I said, under my breath, in the dark, *and we are home once more.*

Then the wave.

I have tried since to say what it was like, and I cannot. The plate says *a wave*, and that is right, but it is not like water. It was not heat, or light, or a blow. It was like being remembered. That is the only word I have ever had for it. As though every part of me, every bone in my hands and every knot I have ever tied on a string, were suddenly and completely known, all at once, by something enormous that had been looking for it for a very long time; and then let go. My suit screamed in my helmet, a long high whoop, and then stopped. And through four meters of steel, through my closed eyes, through the back of my skull, there was a whiteness. Not light. Whiteness. Like nothing I had ever seen. Like something I had read about once, in one of the old paper books in the Tallies' room that I was only allowed to touch with my hands wrapped: snow.

Then it was past.

The note came down. The floor settled under us. And under my hand on the steel floor, deep in the wall, in the Spindle's belly, something breathed. A hair one way. A hair back. Steady. Slow. Alive.

* * *

We came out after a long time. I do not know how long. Ness was alive. I was alive. Aske was alive, though he was sick in his helmet twice and would not talk about it. We came round the back of the Housing on our hands and knees, and the old driver was still standing in the Knot's socket, and the plate was still open on its hinge with its three hands on it, and the gallery was full of light.

I stood up into it.

The Halo was lit.

Not with lamps. With itself. The gold lines on its face, all the way round, thirty-eight kilometers of them, curving away on both sides and meeting far off on the other side—burning. Not bright. Not like fire. A soft clean gold, alive, a bracelet of light round the whole of the empty ring.

And the ring was not empty.

I do not have a word for it. Nobody has, in Tithe; nobody has had one for four hundred and nineteen years. Someone will make one now. A disk of light, from rim to rim, filling the Halo where there had been nothing. Blue-white at the edges where it met the gold, and deepening, toward its heart, into a color that is not violet and not black. And it was moving. Not like water. Not like the flame of a lamp. Like a held breath moves in a chest. A slow swell. And a settling. And through it, if you looked long enough—I looked long enough—other stars.

The Reeve of Brine was sitting at the window.

He had sat down on the glass floor with his back against the brass rail and his legs out in front of him and his gun lying on the floor beside his hand, the way a man sits at the end of a long walk, facing the light. His visor was white. Frost, blooming across it from the inside. I did not go and look behind it. I did not need to. He was sitting very straight, with his head a little tipped back against the rail, as though he were looking up at something, like the old Keeper at the far end of the gallery. Two men in the Spindle who had come to look at the Light, and sat down to do it, and stayed.

I would sing them both out, later. Both of them. The Reeve of Brine, whose name I knew; and the old Keeper, when I had found his.

Ness came and stood beside me at the window, with her hand on her side and her shoulder against mine. She did not say anything. She is a walker. Walkers do not.

And on the band—the relay was still lit, by the window, its little green light burning; the wave had not killed it, or it had come back—on the band, there was a hiss. Like rain, in Brine. Like breath. And under the hiss, very faint, very far off, crackling, as though it were coming from a long way away and a very long time ago, a voice.

It was strange. The accent was strange, and old, and I did not know it; the vowels long and flat, the edges worn. A woman's voice, I thought, or a girl's. It was not loud. It was speaking as though to someone just beside it.

It was singing.

*One for the hook—*

And stopped. And waited. In the stop, where you breathe. The way, I know now, a gang chief called the number on the Ring in the old days, and waited, with the driver on the bolt, for the gang.

I had the flask in my hand. *TTH-9 · 0417.* I would take it home. Back to Hold Fourteen, to the Tallies' room, to the wall by the bottom bunk on the right, nearest the port, where the marks are. Where it came from. I thought she would like that. I thought she would like to be home.

I reached up and touched the side of my helmet, and the light that means *everybody* came on.

I took the breath all the way down. I let it out.

"—and two," I sang, "for the line."
