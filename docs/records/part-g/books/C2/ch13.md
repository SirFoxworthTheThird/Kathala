# Chapter 13: Command Deck

*The Combine · FY 2291*

They pulled the dogs on Day Two Hundred and Eighty-Three, as Haddad had said they would, in four hours.

Gang Four on the drivers and Gang Nine on the crane, in the Barn, in the dark, with their lamps swinging off the governor's gold and the great steel arms reaching in from the walls. Sol called the Count. Every dog took three rounds of it to back out even: a quarter-turn on each of the four big drivers at once, in the order of the star, so the dog came up out of its notch level and did not bind. *One for the hook.* Bracken and Pell and Juno and Sol on the four drivers, Pell one-handed with his bad arm strapped to his chest and a brace on the driver so he could lean his weight on it. *Two for the line.* Teague on the tether lines, keeping them all clear of the teeth. *Three for the dog.* And when the third round was done Oyelaran's crane came in out of the dark on its boom and gripped the dog's head in its claws, and walked it out of the bore, slowly, four meters of steel as thick as a man's thigh sliding out of the cuff with a long grinding whisper you could feel through your gloves, and swung it away to the racks on the Barn's wall where it would stay.

Twelve dogs. Four hours. Nobody spoke on the gang channel except to count.

When the twelfth came out, the governor moved.

Sol felt it before she saw it. She had her hand on the cuff of the twelfth arm, steadying herself while the crane swung the dog away, and the steel under her glove did something it had never done in three years: it shifted. A hair. Less than a hair. A tremor, as though something enormous on the other side of it had taken a breath. Then her lamp, which was on the governor's teeth, slid very slightly sideways along them without her moving it, and slid back.

"Oh," said Pell, on the channel.

"It breathes," said Teague. "See? Told you, son." And coughed.

The white scooter came up out of the dark from the Barn's lower hatch with its running lights blazing, and Haddad in her clean white suit rode it across the Barn to the third arm, the big one, the Inner Housing, and stopped there, and got off, and clipped on, and opened the plate.

Sol watched her do it. The plate was round, the size of a cart wheel, set into the Housing's flank, and it was held by ten bolts in a star, like a collar; and Haddad took them out with a small white driver from her belt, one by one, in the order of the star, and Sol caught herself saying the Count under her breath as the doctor did it. *Four for the time. Five for the air.* When the tenth was out Haddad swung the plate back on its hinge and pulled herself into the dark inside, and her lights went in after her, and for a moment the Housing glowed from within like a lantern.

Then she came out. She swung the plate shut. She put the bolts back in, in the order of the star, backward, ten to one. *Count it back down.*

"Chief Dace," she said, on the open channel, in her dry Halvard voice.

"Doctor."

"It's running."

"How long?"

"A hundred and one hours." Haddad's visor was turned toward the Housing, not toward Sol. "First light at the fourth hour of first shift on Day Two Eighty-Eight. Thank you, Gang Four. Thank you, Gang Nine. Good work." Her scooter puffed white and turned and went back down into the dark through the lower hatch, its lights getting smaller, and went out.

They rode home on the *Patience* with their helmets off and nobody spoke. Juno flew. Sol sat in the crew bubble and watched the Spindle get smaller behind them, black against Kettle, with its gold lines and its needle tip, and thought about the clock in the steel room in its belly, counting down; and she found that she did not feel anything at all about it, not fear, not anger; that she was perfectly calm, and very cold, and that her hands were steady.

"Tomorrow," said Bracken, beside her, in the bubble, very low.

"Tomorrow," she said.

* * *

Third shift change on Day Two Hundred and Eighty-Four was at the eighteenth hour, and at the seventeenth there were three hundred and sixty bonded workers in the hab ring transfers and the galley and the ballast hold and the long cold corridors at the bottom of the spine, waiting, in their liners and their work boots, with whatever they had been able to bring.

It was not much. Drivers. Pry-bars. Cutters, with the gas turned down so the flame was a short blue tongue; Sol had told them not to light them unless they had to, and not at anybody. Lengths of cable. Bracken had a wrench as long as his arm. Mother Szell had a frying pan from the galley. Teague had nothing. He had said he did not need anything, and stood by the ladder at the bottom of the spine with his cloth in his hand, coughing, and watching their faces come up out of the dark.

The plan was simple, because plans that were not simple did not work, Teague said, not with three hundred people who had never done anything like it before. At the eighteenth hour, when the shift horn went, the spine corridor would fill, as it always filled at shift change: crews going up to the dock and down to the rings, Company clerks going off duty, Proctors walking their rounds. And in the middle of it, the gangs would go up. Gangs Two and Six to the dock lock at the spine's head, to hold it shut. Gangs Four and Nine and Eleven to the command deck, to take it. The rest to the admin stack's three doors and the Proctors' barracks and the comms room. Nobody hurt if it could be helped. Nobody killed. Take, and hold, and then wait, and let the Company do the sum.

At the seventeenth hour and forty minutes Sol went up the ladder from the ballast hold and out into the bottom of the spine, alone, to look.

The spine corridor ran the whole length of the platform, from the ballast hold at the bottom to the dock at the top, six hundred meters of white-lit grey corridor four meters wide with hatches off it every forty paces to the hab ring transfers and the admin stack and the stores. At shift change it was always crowded. She had walked up it ten thousand times, at shift change, in a crowd.

It was empty.

She stood at the bottom of it and looked up its length. The white lights hummed. The grey walls went up and up toward the dock, narrowing in the distance, with nobody in them. No crews. No clerks. No Proctors walking their rounds. Somewhere a long way up, a door was banging, slowly, as the platform's spin moved it on its hinge, and the sound came down the empty corridor small and clear, *clang*, and a pause, and *clang*.

She went back down the ladder very fast.

"They know," she said.

The ballast hold went quiet.

"What do you mean they know?" said Oyelaran.

"The spine's empty. All the way up. Nobody. There's no shift change. They've cleared it." She looked round at them, at three hundred faces in the low light of the work lamps. "They know we're coming. They've known all day."

"How?"

Nobody answered. Sol did not look at anyone. She was afraid of who she would look at.

"We go now," said Teague, from the ladder.

They all turned to him.

"Now," he said. "Not at the horn. Now. If they've cleared the spine, they're waiting for us at the top of it, and every minute we stand down here they're getting readier. Or they're not waiting at all, and they're going, and every minute we stand here they're closer to gone." He coughed, and spat, and folded the cloth, and put it in his pocket. "Either way. Now."

"Teague—"

"I'm going first." He put his hand on the ladder. "Don't argue with me, Sol. You don't let the young ones go first. That's the rule. That's the oldest rule there is." He looked round at them, all of them, in the lamplight. "Come on, then," he said, and began to climb.

* * *

They were waiting at the top.

The command deck was at the head of the spine, below the dock, behind a pressure bulkhead with a hatch in it big enough for a cargo sled. The hatch was shut. In front of it, across the full width of the corridor, there was a line of Proctors: twelve of them, in grey armor, faceless, with their visors down and their long-barreled riot guns held across their chests. Behind them, at the hatch, Lusk. He was not wearing a helmet. He stood with his hands behind his back and his square pale face perfectly calm and watched them come up the spine toward him, three hundred of them, filling the corridor from wall to wall, with Teague in front, walking slowly, coughing.

When they were forty paces off Lusk said, without raising his voice, "That's far enough."

Teague stopped. They all stopped behind him.

"Go back down to your rings," said Lusk. "All of you. Now. And I'll forget I saw any of you."

"No, you won't," said Teague.

"No," Lusk agreed. "I won't. But you'll be alive to be disappointed about it." He looked over Teague's head at the crowd. "Dace. I know you're in there. Tell them to go home."

Sol did not answer. She was in the third rank, between Bracken and Oyelaran. She could feel Bracken breathing beside her, slow and deep, like a man about to lift something.

"We want to see the Director," said Teague.

"The Director's busy."

"Then we'll wait."

"No," said Lusk. "You won't." He brought his hands round from behind his back. There was something in one of them, small and round, like a can. "Last chance, old man."

"I had my last chance in '64," said Teague, and walked forward.

The Proctors fired.

It was not bullets. Sol understood that later. At the time she understood only noise, a flat hard coughing roar from twelve guns at once in the narrow corridor, and Teague jerking and stumbling and going down on one knee, and somebody behind her screaming. They were firing stingers: blunt heavy rounds of some soft dense stuff that did not go into you but hit you like a fist the size of a fist, and knocked you down, and broke things. She saw a man in the front rank take one in the face and go backward into the people behind him with his nose spread across his cheek. She saw Teague get up off his knee. And then Lusk threw the can.

It burst on the deck in front of the front rank with a soft pop, and a cloud came out of it, white, fast, filling the corridor from floor to ceiling in a breath, rolling toward them. She smelled it before it reached her: sharp, like hot metal and pepper. Then it reached her, and she could not see, and she could not breathe, and her eyes were full of fire, and her lungs had turned to stone.

She had heard about stinger gas. Every bonded worker had. The Company used it on the Halvard yards when there was trouble. It did not kill you, they said; it only made you wish it would. She was on her knees on the deck. She did not remember going down. Around her in the white there were people screaming and people retching and people trying to run and falling over the people on their knees. The stingers kept firing, the flat coughing roar, *whump whump whump*, into the white. Someone fell across her legs.

And then Bracken was on his feet.

She saw him through the fire in her eyes, a great dark shape rising up out of the white beside her, with the wrench as long as his arm in his fist. He did not make any sound at all. He walked forward through the gas, into the stingers, and she saw one hit him in the chest and saw him rock back on his heels and keep coming; and then he was at the Proctors' line and the wrench came round in a long slow arc, and a Proctor's gun went flying, and the Proctor went down; and Bracken kept going.

And then they were all going. The whole spine. She did not decide to get up and she was up. She did not decide to go forward and she was going forward, half blind, choking, with Oyelaran on one side of her and somebody else on the other, and the people behind pushing, three hundred people pushing up a corridor four meters wide into twelve guns; and the guns fired, and people went down, and the people behind them stepped over them and kept coming, because there was nowhere else to go, because the corridor was full behind them and the gas was in their lungs and the only way out of it was forward.

The Proctors broke. Not all at once. One, and then another, backing toward the hatch, and then all of them, and Lusk shouting something, and then the hatch was opening behind them and they were going through it, and Lusk was gone, and the last Proctor turned in the hatch's mouth and fired one more round into the white and then the hatch was closing; and Bracken got there.

He got his wrench into the gap. Sol saw it. The great steel jaw of the hatch coming down on the wrench's shaft and stopping, with a scream of metal, a hand's width from the deck. And Bracken got his fingers under the hatch's edge beside the wrench and lifted.

He could not have lifted it. Nobody could have lifted it. It was a pressure hatch, a meter thick, driven by hydraulics, rated to hold the command deck sealed against vacuum. Bracken lifted it. She saw his back bow and the cords stand out on his neck, and the hatch went up a hand's width, and another, and then somebody else's hands were under it, and somebody else's, and a pry-bar, and a driver, twenty people heaving at it with their eyes streaming and their lungs on fire; and the hatch went up, screaming, and stuck at half a meter, and the hydraulics behind it blew with a bang, and it stayed there.

They went under it on their hands and knees.

The command deck was empty.

She came through under the hatch into clean air, and fell on the deck, and lay there with her face on the cold metal breathing, and breathing, and could not stop; and when she could see again she lifted her head and looked round and there was nobody. Consoles. Chairs. Screens, still lit, green and amber. A coffee cup on a desk, still steaming. A coat over the back of a chair. A door at the far end, standing open, with a ladder beyond it going up. Up, to the dock.

"They're on the *Assessor*," said Oyelaran, on his knees beside her. His voice was a croak. "They're going. They're going now."

She got up. She did not know how. She went to the long window at the end of the command deck, the one that looked out along the mooring arm toward the dock, and put her hands on it.

The *Assessor* was moving.

She was a long grey ship, sleek and clean, with the Combine's blue on her flanks and lights along her length, and she was sliding away from the dock as Sol watched, slowly, very slowly, her docking clamps open, her lock sealed, her running lights blinking green and red. Her thrusters puffed white along her side. The gap between her and the dock was ten meters. Fifteen. Twenty. Sol could see her ports, lit, a row of them along her flank. In one of them there was a man standing. Tall, soft-shouldered, in a dark blue coat with the collar open. He was too far to see his face. He was standing at the port with his hand flat on the glass, looking back at the platform, the way you look back at a house you are leaving.

Then the *Assessor*'s main drive lit, a long blue-white flare along her stern, and she turned, and went, and the man at the port was gone.

* * *

Teague was in the corridor where he had fallen, twenty paces from the hatch, in the last of the white.

He was alive. He was lying on his side with his knees drawn up and his cloth pressed to his mouth, and the cloth was red, all of it, and his breath came in and out of him with a sound like a cutter's torch, a high rasping whistle, and his lips were blue. Two stingers had hit him: one in the shoulder, which was broken, and one in the chest. The gas had done the rest.

They carried him to the infirmary. It was empty, too. Amsel was gone; his stool by the instrument cabinet was pushed back against the wall, and the burn kit he had used on Pell's arm was still on the counter, open, and the stack of paper with the blue letterhead was not. Sol laid Teague on the table where Pell had lain and stood by him and did not know what to do. Nobody did. They knew how to splint a broken finger and seal a suit breach and drag a man out of a flare. They did not know how to do anything about glass lung and stinger gas, and the man who knew was on the *Assessor*.

Mother Szell came in, and pushed them aside, and put a mask on him from the cabinet, oxygen, and turned it up. It helped. A little. The whistle went lower. His eyes opened.

"Sol," he said, through the mask.

"I'm here."

"Did we get it."

"We got the deck," she said. "We got the deck and the dock and the stack. The whole platform. All of it. Nobody's left. They're gone. They left before. They knew we were coming and they left."

Teague's eyes moved behind the mask. She thought it might be laughter.

"Somebody told," he said.

"Yes."

"Always somebody." He breathed. "Doesn't matter. Don't—don't look for them. Not now. Later." His hand came up off the table, the one with the bloody cloth in it, and found her sleeve, and held on. "You've got the deck. You've got the platform. Now you've got to do the next thing."

"What's the next thing?"

"I don't know," said Teague. "That's why it's yours." His eyes closed and opened. "Sol."

"Yes."

"The letter. Dov's. In my kit. Open it. I think he'd want—" He coughed, under the mask, and the coughing went on, and when it stopped the whistle was louder again. "He'd want you to read it. Not me. He was writing to the factory. But he'd want you."

"All right."

"And the Count." His fingers tightened on her sleeve. "Keep the boy singing. You hear me? The farm boy. Keep him singing. When it's bad. It's—" he breathed— "it's the singing that gets you through. Not the—not the thing you're singing about. The singing." His mouth moved under the mask in what might have been a smile. "Ten for the Company."

"And damn the Company," said Sol.

"And damn the Company," said Teague. And then he was quiet for a while, breathing with that high whistle, his hand on her sleeve; and then he was quiet altogether.

* * *

They found out who it was an hour later, and they did not have to look, because Lusk told them.

It came over the platform speakers. Every speaker on every deck, the soft double chime first, high and low, the Company's chime; and then not the recorded woman's voice, but Lusk's own, flat and clipped and calm, from somewhere out in the dark on the *Assessor*.

"Attention all personnel on Platform TTH-9," it said. "This is Proctor-Captain Lusk. You'll be wondering how we knew. I'll save you the trouble. The Combine thanks Bonded Laborer Juno Szabo, zero three three one, Gang Four, for her information regarding tonight's planned disturbance, which she provided to my office on Day Two Hundred and Sixty-Four. Her service is noted." A pause. "Her request for passage on the *Assessor* was not approved. Lusk out."

The chime again. Then silence.

Sol was in the galley when it came, with a hundred others, sitting at the long tables with their eyes still red and streaming, drinking water, holding cloths to their faces. She heard it and did not move. She heard the room go still around her. And then she heard it begin to turn, the way a crowd turns, all those heads, looking; and she turned with it, slowly, and looked where they were looking, at the end of the far table by the wall where Juno Szabo was sitting alone with her hands flat on the table in front of her and her face the color of ash.

Juno did not try to run. She did not try to say anything. She sat with her hands flat on the table and looked straight ahead of her, at nothing, as they came.

There were a lot of them. Sol was on her feet and pushing through, and she was not fast enough; by the time she got to the end of the far table they had Juno up off the bench and against the wall, and somebody had her by the hair, and somebody had a cutter, unlit, against her throat, and somebody was shouting *the lock, the lock, put her out the lock*, and the shout was going round the galley and coming back louder. Ruzicka from Gang Nine had Juno's arms. Her face was wet and twisted. The man with the cutter was Danek from Gang Two, who had said *that's not true* in the ballast hold, whose bunkmate had taken a stinger in the eye in the spine and would not see out of it again.

"Stop," said Sol.

Nobody heard her.

"*Stop.*" She got her hands on Danek's arm and pulled the cutter down, and he swung round on her with his face wild, and she held on. "Stop it. Stop it. Teague said. You heard him. You don't kill anybody. Not one. Not a Proctor, not a clerk, not a Director." She looked at Juno, pinned against the wall with Ruzicka's hands on her arms and somebody's fist in her hair. "Not her."

"She sold us," said Danek. "She sold Teague. She sold *everybody*—"

"I know what she did."

"Then *why*—"

"Because Teague said." Sol's voice cracked. She did not care. "Because he said the minute you kill somebody they'll stop talking about what they did and start talking about what you did. Because he's lying on a table down the hall with his lungs full of blood and he told me that with the last thing he had. That's why." She looked round at all of them, the faces packed in round the end of the table, red-eyed, streaming, mad with it. "You want to put her out the lock? Then you put me out first. Go on. I'm her chief. Gang Four. My gang. You want her, you go through me."

Nobody moved.

Then Bracken was there. He came through the crowd the way he had come through the gas, not fast, not saying anything, and the crowd let him through the way the Proctors had; and he stood beside Sol, very big, with his arms at his sides. His eyes were swollen almost shut. There was blood all down the front of his liner from where the stinger had taken him in the chest. He did not look at anybody. He just stood there.

After a while, Ruzicka let go of Juno's arms.

After another while, whoever had her hair let go of it too.

Danek looked at Sol, and at Bracken, and at the cutter in his own hand. Then he threw it on the floor, hard, so it skidded away under the tables, and turned and pushed his way out through the crowd and was gone.

Juno slid down the wall until she was sitting on the floor. She did not cry. She sat with her back against the wall and her knees drawn up and her arms round them, and looked at the floor, and said, very quietly, very precisely, to nobody: "Tibor. They said they'd take Tibor off my bond."

Nobody answered her.

"He's nine," said Juno.

Sol looked down at her for a long moment. Then she turned away.

The speakers chimed.

Everybody in the galley flinched. The double chime, high and low; and then not Lusk, this time, but a different voice, a tired low reasonable voice, a little hoarse, as though the man it belonged to had been crying, or had been about to and had stopped.

"This is the Site Director," said Imre Castellane. "I'm speaking to everyone on the platform.

"I want you to know that the Spindle's clock is armed and running, and that it cannot be stopped from the platform. Not from the command deck. Not from anywhere. I want you to know that so you don't waste your time trying. First light will occur on Day Two Hundred and Eighty-Eight at the fourth hour of first shift, as scheduled.

"I want you to know also that any approach to the Ring by any vessel or person from the platform will be treated as an act of sabotage against Combine property and will be met with force. The *Assessor* is armed. I'm sorry. I'm telling you that, too, so that you don't waste your time.

"I'm—" He stopped. The speakers hissed. For a long moment there was nothing. Then: "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I want you to know that. I don't expect it to—I'm sorry. Castellane out."

The chime. Silence.

The galley was quiet. A hundred people, and nobody spoke. Somewhere out in the spine the banging door had stopped.

Sol stood in the middle of the galley with Juno on the floor at her feet and Bracken at her shoulder and Teague dead on a table down the hall and the clock in the Spindle counting, and she found that she was calm again. Perfectly calm, and very cold, and her hands were steady, the way they had been on the *Patience* coming home from the Barn. She thought about the clock in its steel room. She thought about the governor, breathing. About the twelve dogs on their racks on the Barn's wall where Oyelaran's crane had swung them, four meters of steel as thick as a man's thigh, each one.

*Because it holds on and doesn't let go*, Teague had said.

She turned. Pell was by the galley door, with his bad arm strapped across his chest and his face grey and his eyes on her. Behind him, in the door, small and dark and narrow in a plain Company coverall with no white suit and no running lights and no tablet, her hands hanging empty at her sides, stood Yusra Haddad. Sol had not known she had stayed. She had not known anyone had stayed. The doctor met her eyes across the galley and did not look away.

"Gang Four," said Sol. "Suit up."

"For what?" said someone.

"To go and put a dog back in," she said.
