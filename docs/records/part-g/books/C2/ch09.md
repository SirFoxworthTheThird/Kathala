# Chapter 9: Water

*The Dark · Year 419*

The second lung died on the eleventh day.

I heard it go. Everybody in Hold Fourteen heard it go, because you hear the lungs all your life without hearing them, the way you hear your own heart, and when one stops the silence comes up through the floor like cold water. I was in the Tallies' room with Ness, on the floor under the First Count, taking her through the Door for the hundredth time. She had it now. Not well. She sang like a cracked pipe and she still put the dog before the pin when she was tired. But she had it, all of it, the numbers and the Door, and I had made her sing it to me forward and backward and out of order, and she had sworn at me in every way she knew, and I had felt, for the first time since Quill went, that I could breathe. And then the floor went quiet under us.

Ness stopped in the middle of a line.

"That's Number Two," she said.

There had been four lungs in Hold Fourteen when I was born. One had died when I was nine. Number Two was the big one, the oldest, down in the deepest level of the Hold under the old reactor shielding, the one Veska had worked as a girl. It was the one that kept the children's levels clear. We heard it start to cough and grind, two floors down, a sound like a giant gargling grit, and then a long rising whine, and then a bang that went up through the walls, and then nothing.

Then the smell.

You cannot describe the smell of the black to anybody who has not smelled it. It is not a bad smell, exactly. It is sweet, a little. Like bread gone over, like the inside of an old boot. It came up the stairwells and along the corridors and in under the door of the Tallies' room, faint at first and then not faint, and Ness and I looked at each other, and then we were both up and running, out and down, toward the children's levels, with our sleeves over our mouths.

Veska was already there. She had come up out of the lung level with her face black to the eyes and her iron bar in her hand and blood on her knuckles where she had beaten on something, and she was standing in the corridor outside the children's dormitory shouting at people to get the children out, up, into the dome, where the third lung still pushed air; and people were running past her with children in their arms, wrapped in blankets, coughing. I took one. A little girl, four, Hesta's youngest, so light. She coughed on my shoulder all the way up eleven flights. When we got to the dome there were already thirty children in it, lying on the old grey garden beds under the glass with Kettle turning overhead, and their mothers kneeling beside them, and the sound of all of them coughing together under the dome was like rain.

Two of them died that night. A baby, and a boy of three. I sang them both out in the morning, in the cold corridor, with the Hold behind me singing under my voice; and the Hold was singing again by then. But it was not singing for them. It was singing at me.

* * *

Veska did not call a meeting. She did not need to. She came to the Tallies' room on the evening of the twelfth day, alone, and knocked, and came in when I said to, and sat down on Quill's bunk, which was empty, under the marks, as though it were hers to sit on.

"He's called," she said.

"Who has?"

"Kine." She held up something in her hand: a little black box, the size of a fist, with a red light on its face. "He left this with me. On the dock. Before he went. It talks to his ship. I didn't tell you." She turned it over. "He called it this morning. He knew about Number Two."

"How could he know?"

"I don't know. Maybe he's got somebody here. Maybe he just knows how old our lungs are, and how long they've got. He's been to every Hold in Tithe. He knows how old everybody's lungs are." She set the box down on the bunk beside her. "He said he's sorry for our loss. He said it like he meant it. He said he's changed the terms."

I waited.

"He doesn't want the song any more," said Veska. "Not just the song. He's learned the hard way that old Tallies lie, he said. He said no offense to the dead. He wants you." She looked at me. "On Brine. Till he's ready to go to the Halo. And then at the Halo, to sing it there, wherever it is he thinks it needs singing. And he says if you'll come, his ship will be here in two days, and it'll bring four tanks and the two lungs, and you'll walk up the ramp and the lungs'll be rolled down it, the same hour."

"And if I don't come."

"Then he doesn't come. Nor the lungs. Nor the water-boats." She did not look away. "We've got one lung, Tally. One. Number Three. And it's not well. If Number Three goes the whole Hold breathes the black. All of us. In a month there's nobody left to sing out."

I sat on the floor with my back against the wall.

"What do you want me to say?" I said.

"I don't want you to say anything," said Veska. "I'm not here to ask you. I'm the boss of this Hold, and this Hold is going to die if he doesn't send those lungs, and I'd put you on that ship in a sack if I had to. I'm telling you that so you know where I stand." She stood up. She was a short broad woman and the room was small and she filled it. "But I'd rather you walked."

She went to the door. At the door she stopped and turned round.

"Quill lied to him," she said. "On the dock. Didn't he? I'm not a Tally, I don't know the Count, I only know it at funerals the way everybody does. But I've heard it at enough funerals. That wasn't how it goes."

"No," I said.

"Why'd he do that?"

"Because of the rule. Never sing the Count on the Halo."

Veska looked at me for a long moment.

"There's a lot of rules in the Dark," she said, "that were made by people who didn't have to breathe the black." And she went out.

* * *

I told Ness at the top of the Hold, under the dome, at the evening bell, among the coughing children. She did not take it well.

She did not shout. Ness shouts at everything that doesn't matter and goes quiet at everything that does. She sat beside me on the edge of an old garden bed with her elbows on her knees and her hands hanging between them, and listened, and did not say anything until I had finished; and then she said, very quietly: "No."

"It's not up to you."

"It's not up to Veska either. It's not up to the Hold. It's yours. It's your Count. You don't have to go."

"Look round you."

She did not look round. She did not have to. The children were all around us on the grey soil, wrapped in blankets, and the mothers kneeling, and the sound of the coughing under the glass.

"He'll take you to Brine," said Ness. "And he'll keep you there. And then he'll take you to the Halo, to the Spindle, and you'll sing it, whatever it does, and then what? What happens to you after? What does a man like that do with the only person who knows a thing he wants, once he's got it?"

"I'm not the only person who knows it," I said.

She turned her head and looked at me.

"I'm not," I said. "Not now. That's what the old man made me promise. Never let one Tally be the only one who knows it." I took her hand where it hung between her knees. It was cold and hard and scarred across the knuckles. "You've got it."

"Badly."

"You've got it. You can sing it forward and backward. You can sing it out of order. You put the dog before the pin when you're tired, and I'll tell you this now, so you remember: don't. The pin's first going down. And you've got the turns right every time, and that's the hard part." I held on to her hand. "So if I don't come back, it's not gone. It's not in only one head. That's what matters. That's what the First Tally said matters."

Ness looked at me for a long time. Her face in the Kettle-light was purple and yellow and the scar was black along her jaw.

"I don't want to be a Tally," she said.

"I know."

"I don't want to have to sing out kids."

"I know."

"I want you to come back."

"I know," I said, and I put my face against her shoulder, and she put her arm round me, and we sat there among the coughing children under Kettle until the night bell.

* * *

The *Patience* came on the morning of the fourteenth day, and they rolled the tanks down the ramp, four, smoking, and set them in a row on the dock floor; and then they rolled down one lung, a great dull grey machine on a sled with its intake mouths sealed in cloth, and set it beside the tanks.

"One," said Veska. She stood at the foot of the ramp with her iron bar. "He said two."

"The other's on the next boat." It was a woman who said it, at the top of the ramp: tall and lean, with grey-blond hair cut short and a long white scar from the corner of her eye down into her collar, in the same armor as the men. She had a slate in her hand and the air of someone who did all the talking that mattered on the *Patience* so that the Reeve did not have to. "It's on Brine, in the old Ninth district. It's under the ice, half dug out. We'll have it out in a month. It'll come on the first boat after."

"He said two in the same hour."

"He said two," the woman agreed. "And here's one, Boss, and you can see it's whole, and you can see we brought it. The other one comes after. You can take that or not." She looked past Veska, at me, standing at the edge of the dock floor with my bag over my shoulder. "Is that her?"

"That's her," said Veska, without turning round.

"Then she can come up."

I did not look back at the gallery. I had said goodbye to the Hold already, in the corridor that morning, after I had sung out the boy of three; and I had said goodbye to Ness, in the Tallies' room, with the door shut, and she was not on the gallery, because I had asked her not to be. I did not look back at the lung on its sled, either, or at the tanks, or at Veska. I walked across the dock floor to the foot of the ramp and up it, and at the top the scarred woman looked at me with flat grey eyes and said, "I'm Marrow. Come on," and I went past her into the ship.

* * *

I had thought a ship would be like a Hold. Corridors and rooms. It was not. It was smaller, and much more crowded, and it smelled of oil and old sweat and something sharp and metal that I learned later was the engines, and everything in it was close: the ceilings low, the corridors so narrow two people could not pass without turning sideways, the walls covered in pipes and cables and boxes and handholds, and lights, small hard lights, everywhere, so that there was no dark anywhere at all. It was very loud. The engines were a growl under everything that never stopped. And it moved. That was the thing nobody had told me, and that I had never imagined: that when the *Patience* left the dock, the floor would tilt, and press up under my feet, and then go light, and that I would have to hold on to a handhold on the wall while my stomach went somewhere it had never been.

Marrow took me forward. Up a short ladder and along a corridor and through a hatch into a room at the very front of the ship, where there were chairs, and screens, and a long window of thick glass across the front, through which—I did not look through it at first. I did not want to.

There was a brass plate on the wall above the front chair.

It was old. Older than anything on the ship, I thought; older than the Dark. It was bolted to the bulkhead with four bolts whose heads had been polished flat, and it was dull and green at its edges and bright in the middle, the way old brass goes when hands touch it. There were letters on it, raised, in the old square writing Quill had been teaching me. I read them because I could, and because it was the first thing on the *Patience* that I had understood.

*PATIENCE*, it said. And under that, smaller, a number, and *OSTRAVA*, and some words I did not know—*TUG CLASS III*—and at the very bottom, so worn I had to put my face close: *COMBINE*.

"Old, isn't it," said Marrow.

"What's the Combine?"

Marrow shrugged. "Who knows. Something from before. Kine says she's the oldest hull in the Reach. Older than the Dark, older than the Light, maybe. He says she's been rebuilt so many times there's nothing original left but that plate and the bolts that hold it." She looked at the plate without much interest. "He won't let anyone take it down. Sit there."

I sat where she pointed, in a chair along the side wall, and she strapped me into it with a belt across my chest, and went out.

And then I looked out of the window, because I could not help it.

We were outside.

I had never been outside. I was a Tally's girl; I had lived my whole life in Hold Fourteen, and the only outside I had ever seen was through the dome's old glass, and the milk-white port in the Tallies' room, and the port in the inner lock door, at the dead. Outside was where the dead went. It was the Dark. And here I was in it, with a window in front of me.

Tithe was behind us. I could see it in a screen on the wall, if I turned my head: a great dark tangle of a thing, broken towers and dead spokes and districts black with frost, with only here and there a light, very small, where a Hold still burned. I had not known it was so big, or so dead. I had not known it was so beautiful, either, in the way a broken thing can be. I looked at it for a long time, trying to find Fourteen, and could not.

And ahead of us, filling the window, the Halo.

I had seen it all my life through the dome glass, a dark band crossing Kettle. That is not seeing it. From the *Patience*, from outside, I saw it whole. It was a ring. A great dark ring, going round, so big that its far side was a thin line, a thread, small as a hair across Kettle's face; and on its near side, close, it was a wall of black metal going up and up out of the window's top and down and down out of its bottom, laced all over with lines of gold. Dull gold. Dead gold. The gold of something that has been cold for a very long time. And standing up off its rim, a long way round, small and sharp against Kettle's bands, a needle. A tower.

"That's the Spindle," said a voice behind me.

It was not Marrow. It was a man's voice, thin and soft and a little hoarse, and when I turned my head as far as the belt would let me I saw him in the hatch: the gaunt man in grey with the iron cuffs on his wrists and the chain between them, and the flame-in-a-ring on his forehead, and the deep bright eyes.

"I've been told to look after you," he said. "I'm sorry. I'm sure you'd rather not be looked after by me. But there's no one else on this ship who's not a soldier, and the Reeve thought you might want someone to talk to who wasn't carrying a gun." He came in, slowly, with his chain held in his hands so it would not clink. "I'm Cinder. Brother Cinder. Of the Wick, on Brine."

"You're a Lamplighter."

"I'm a Lamplighter." He sat down in the chair beside mine, carefully, as though he were not sure it would hold him. He was so thin that I could see the bones of his wrists move under the cuffs. "And you're the Tally of Fourteen. And neither of us chose to be on this ship, and here we both are." He looked out of the window at the Halo, and his face changed. It went soft. It went, I thought, the way the faces of the mothers went in the dome, looking at their children. "Isn't it beautiful?"

"It's dead."

"It's sleeping," said Brother Cinder. "That's what the Wick teaches. The Light didn't die. The Light was taken, and it went away, and those who loved it went after it, to bring it home. And one day it will come home. And when it does, the Halo will wake, and the Eye will open, and they'll come back through it. All of them." He did not take his eyes off the Halo. "The Founders. And everyone who went with them. Everyone we've lost."

"Everyone we've lost," I said. "You mean the dead."

"I mean everyone."

I thought of Pim on the bench in his jersey with the red stripe. Of Quill, with his fingers on the marks. Of the baby and the boy of three, two mornings ago.

"The dead don't come back," I said. "I send them out. I know where they go."

"Do you?" Cinder turned and looked at me. His eyes were so bright. "*Count it back down*," he said softly. "*We are home once more*. Your own song, Tally. What do you think that means?"

I did not answer.

"I've read the Rule," said Cinder. "The piece the Reeve has. I read it for him. That's why he keeps me—I can read the Keepers' hand, the old script, my order's kept it since the Light. *The Knot is the Founders', and shall not be turned.* The Founders' Knot. In the Spindle." He leaned toward me, and the chain clinked despite him. "The Founders tied it, Tally. Before they went. They tied it to hold the Light asleep until they came home. And your song is how it's untied. I've always known it. The Wick has always known it. And now he's got you, and he's going to take you to the Spindle, and you're going to sing it, and the Light is going to come home." His voice shook. "I've waited my whole life."

"You're in chains," I said.

"Yes."

"Why?"

He looked down at his wrists as though he had forgotten.

"Because I told him," said Brother Cinder, "that when the Light comes home, it won't be his."

* * *

The Reeve sent for me at the evening bell, or what I thought of as the evening bell; there were no bells on the *Patience*, only the engines, and the hard lights that never went down.

His cabin was at the back of the ship, behind the engines, where the growl was loudest. It was small, like everything else, and full of paper. Not slates. Paper. Old charts, pinned to every wall, so many that you could not see the walls under them: charts of Tithe, of Brine, of the Halo, of Kettle and its moons, charts of things I did not recognize at all, drawn in old ink and new, with notes on them in a big rough hand. There was a narrow bunk, and a table, and on the table a lamp, and a book, burned at its edges, open, under a sheet of glass.

He was sitting at the table. He did not stand up when Marrow brought me in. He was coughing, and when he had finished coughing he wiped his mouth with a cloth that he then folded and put in his pocket so that I would not see what was on it; and I saw anyway.

"Sit down, Tally," he said.

I sat on the end of the bunk.

"Marrow says you're angry about the lung."

"You said two."

"I said two. You'll get two." He looked at me with his pale grey eyes. "I don't lie about lungs. I've buried too many people to lie about lungs."

"How many?"

He seemed surprised. Then he thought about it, seriously, as though it were a question that deserved an answer.

"In Brine," he said, "since I've been Reeve, which is nineteen years—four thousand and something. I've got the number written down. Four thousand two hundred and eleven, last I looked. Not all the rot. Some the ice. Some the cold. Some each other." He paused. "One of them was my daughter. She was nine. That was eleven years ago. The rot. It came up out of the lungs in the Fifth district where we lived, and she breathed it, and I didn't have a lung to give her, because there wasn't one in the whole of Brine that was spare." He was quiet for a while. "So I don't lie about lungs, Tally. You'll get two."

I did not say anything.

He turned the book on the table round, under its glass, so that I could see it. The pages were brown and burned at the edges, curling. The writing on them was not like any writing I knew; not the old square letters, not the new, but a fine slanting hand, very close, with little marks above the letters.

"The Keepers' hand," said the Reeve. "Cinder reads it. This is the only piece of the Rule that's left anywhere, as far as I know. I found it in the Ninth district of Brine, in a dead Keeper's house under the ice. It's burned. Most of it's gone. But the first page is here." He put his finger on the glass, over a line. "*The Knot is the Founders', and shall not be turned.* That's what Cinder says it says. I believe him. He's a fanatic, but he's an honest one." He took his finger away. "Your Tallies have a rule too. Never sing the Count on the Halo. The First Tally made it. You know what I think?"

"No."

"I think whoever made your rule and whoever made that one knew each other," said the Reeve. "Or knew the same thing. I think the Knot holds the Light down. And I think your song lets it up." He looked at me. "Am I wrong?"

"I don't know."

"No. You don't. Nobody does." He sat back. "That's the thing about the Dark. Nobody knows anything any more. Everything that knew things is dead. All we've got is scraps. A page of a book. A brass plate with a word on it nobody understands. A funeral song." He coughed, briefly. "I'm sick, Tally. I've got a year, maybe. Maybe two. Brine's got ten. I don't have time to wait for somebody to come along who knows. So I'm going to try it with the scraps."

"And if it doesn't work?"

"Then I'll have tried."

"And if it does?" I said. "What then? What happens when the Light comes back? Brother Cinder says it won't be yours."

The Reeve looked at me for a long time.

"Brother Cinder," he said, "thinks dead people are going to come out of the Eye. He thinks his Founders are going to come back with lamps in their hands and make everything well." He shook his head slowly. "I don't think anybody's coming back, Tally. I think the Light's a machine, like the lungs, like the heat-pumps under the ice. I think it ran everything once and it can run everything again. And I think when it does, somebody's got to be in charge of it. Somebody's got to hold the door." He spread his big scarred hands on the table. "Would you rather it was me? Or would you rather it was nobody, and every Hold in Tithe and every district in Brine fighting over it with knives? Because that's the choice. That's the only choice there is."

I did not have an answer to that. I sat on the end of his bunk with my hands in my lap.

"The second lung," I said at last. "Before I sing. Not after. Before. I want to see it go down the ramp at Fourteen with my own eyes."

The Reeve of Brine looked at me. Then he smiled, his tired slow smile, with the broken tooth at the side.

"You have my word," he said.

Behind him, through a small round port in the back wall of the cabin, I could see the Halo going by, dark and vast, its gold lines dull, and far off on its rim, the needle of the Spindle, standing up black against Kettle's turning face. I looked at it for a long time. And it seemed to me, though I knew it could not be true, through the hull and the glass and the growl of the engines, that the floor under my feet was very faintly, very far off, beating, in runs, and stops, and runs again; like something counting.
