# Chapter 10: First Light Is a Wave

*The Combine · FY 2291*

Haddad's quarters were on the fourth deck of the admin stack, at the end of a corridor of identical doors, and inside they were not identical to anything. Sol stood just inside the door while the doctor locked it behind her and looked round, because she could not help it, at the first room she had been in for twelve years that somebody had made for themselves.

There were books. Paper books, dozens of them, on shelves screwed to every wall and in stacks on the floor and on the narrow bunk, with strips of paper sticking out of them to mark places. There was a samovar, an old one, brass, on a stand by the bunk, with a little glass cup upside down on its top. There were pictures on the wall above the desk: a city on a hillside, white houses going up in terraces under a pale sky, and a woman in a scarf standing at a gate, and two small boys on a wall, squinting into the sun. There was a rug on the floor, red and blue, worn through in the middle. And there was a smell, under the smoke—something sweet and dry, like dust and flowers.

"Halvard," said Haddad, seeing her look at the city. "Kesra. Where I was born. That's my mother. Those are my brothers. They're old men now. One of them's dead." She went past Sol to the samovar and turned something on it, and it began to tick. "Sit down. Anywhere. Move the books."

Sol moved a stack off a chair and sat. Haddad did not. She stood by the samovar with her arms folded, looking at it, as though it might tell her where to begin.

"Is it routine?" Sol said again.

"No," said Haddad.

The samovar ticked.

"I'll tell you all of it," the doctor said. "Once. I won't tell it twice, I don't think I can. Then you can do what you want. Go to Lusk, if you like. Go to the Director. Go to the ballast hold, where I imagine you've been." She glanced at Sol. "Yes. We know about the ballast hold. Lusk has known for years. He lets you have it because it's cheaper than finding out what you'd do without it."

Sol said nothing.

"First light," said Haddad, "is a wave."

She took the little glass cup down from the samovar's top and turned it over in her fingers.

"When the Ring couples to Kettle's field, the throat forms. It takes about forty seconds. In those forty seconds, space inside the Ring—stops being one thing and starts being two. Here and Ostrava. It tears, if you like. A very precise tear, along a line we've drawn very carefully with ten thousand kilometers of phase-line. But it tears. And as it tears it throws off a front. A shockwave, in the geometry, moving outward at the speed of light from the throat's edge, across the plane of the Ring and up and down its axis." She set the cup on the samovar's spout. "It carries radiation. Hard. Harder than anything Kettle throws. And a gravitic shear, which is—it doesn't matter what it is. It goes through things. It goes through people. Within fifty kilometers of the throat, anything not behind four meters of steel, or the equivalent, takes a dose that kills it in between an hour and three days, depending on the dose and the person. Beyond fifty kilometers it falls off fast. Beyond two hundred it's nothing."

"The platform's six kilometers off the rim."

"Six point two."

"The hab rings."

"Have a centimeter and a half of hull plate and the water in the air plant." Haddad turned the tap on the samovar, and dark tea ran into the glass cup. "The skirt was the shielding. The skirt would have done it, just. Steel and water and a phase mesh tuned to bleed the front off sideways. We designed it four years ago. Gang Nine had sixty days of work left on it." She turned the tap off. "It isn't going to be finished."

"Castellane said the rings were rated. Your revised models."

"There are no revised models." Haddad held out the cup of tea to her. "Drink that. You're very white."

Sol took it. It was hot. She did not drink it.

"There's no instability, either," said Haddad. "In the governor. I made it up. Well. I didn't make it up, I was told to make it up, and then I made it up, which is worse. There's no such thing as a bow drawn and held. That was Imre's phrase. He likes a phrase. The Ring could sit unlit for a hundred years and Kettle wouldn't hurt it. But the board needed a reason to light it early, a reason that would go in the record and look like prudence, and the only person on this platform who could certify a phase instability that would satisfy the underwriters at Ostrava Mutual was me." She looked at the wall, at the white city on its hill. "So I certified it."

"Why early?"

"You know why. You've seen the sheet."

"I want you to say it."

Haddad looked at her.

"Because the construction loss policy expires on Day Two Hundred and Ninety," she said. "And after that the bonded labor on this platform isn't insured, and the Combine owes four hundred and twelve completion bonuses and four hundred and twelve passages home, and it can't pay them. It's overextended, Chief. It spent twice what it meant to on this Ring, and Halvard-Lyons is building one of its own at Corvo, two years behind us and catching up, and if Corvo lights first the Combine's paper isn't worth the ink. The board needs the Ring lit and it needs money. And there is a way to get both on the same day." She sat down, finally, on the edge of the bunk, among the books. "That's all. That's the whole of it. It's arithmetic."

"And the *Mercy*?"

"Reassigned. Day Two Thirty. To the Halvard yards, by board order, on Mr. Lind's authority. She was never coming. The relay signal didn't fail. There wasn't one to send."

"Standing the platform off. The tow."

"Never planned. You were right about the coupling." Haddad almost smiled. "Imre told me you'd been to see him. He was very shaken. He said, *she ran the bead on the north face herself.* As though that settled something."

"Does he know?"

"Imre knows." Haddad looked down at her hands. "Imre chose not to know the details, which is what Imre does. He is a good man in most of the ways that don't matter. He knows there's a policy and he knows there's a date and he knows the skirt was stopped, and he knows what those three things add up to, and he has decided that it is above his station to add them." She was quiet. "He'll be on the *Assessor* when she goes. He'll stand at the window and watch. And he'll be sick, afterward, I imagine. And then he'll go home and see his daughter."

"When does she go?"

"The *Assessor*? Day Two Eighty-Five. First shift. All Company staff, all Proctors, Mr. Lind, the Director. Three days out to Gall's shadow, which is two hundred and twenty thousand kilometers off, where the front is nothing. The story will be that the instability worsened unexpectedly on Day Two Eighty-Seven, and the governor coupled early, and the throat formed before the platform could be evacuated, and the staff were saved only because they had gone ahead to prepare the tow." She lifted her hands and let them drop. "There'll be an inquiry. I'll give evidence. The underwriters will pay."

"And the Lighting? If you're all gone. Who lights it?"

"The Ring lights itself." Haddad looked up. "That's the beauty of the design. My design. There's a clock in the Spindle. In the governor housing. Hardened, sealed, three redundant power feeds off the phase-lines. When the dogs come out of the governor, I arm it, and it counts down, and at the end of the count it couples the governor to the field, and the throat forms. Nobody has to be there. Nobody *can* be there; you couldn't be within fifty kilometers. The *Assessor* can stop it by signal, if something goes wrong. Nothing else can."

"When do the dogs come out?"

"Day Two Eighty-Three. Gang Four and Gang Nine. Four hours." Haddad met her eyes. "You remember I asked."

Sol sat with the cup of tea in her hands and did not drink it. She found that she was very cold, all through, the way she had been cold at the end of the chain with Kostya sixty meters past her fingers. The samovar ticked. On the wall the white city went up its hill in the pale sun.

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because you came up the ladder." Haddad's voice did not change. "That's all. Because I have been doing the arithmetic for thirty-one days, Chief Dace, four hundred and twelve people against a door that will carry ten million in the first century and God knows how many after, and I am very good at arithmetic, it's the only thing I've ever been good at, and every time I do it it comes out the same. Lind showed it to me on a single page. Four hundred and twelve. Against all of that. He said, *you're an engineer, doctor, you understand loads.* And I did. I do." She was looking at her hands again. "And then I go to bed and I can't stop doing it. I lie there and do the sum. Over and over. All night. And it always comes out the same, and it doesn't help." She looked up. "And then you came up the ladder with the sheet. And I thought, well. There's someone else who can do the sum now. At least I won't be doing it alone."

"That's it?"

"That's it. I'm not a brave woman, Chief. I'm not going to stand up in the galley and tell the platform. I'm not going to march on the command deck. I'm a coward with a samovar." She lifted her hand as though to drink from a cup of her own, and found she had not poured one, and looked at the empty hand, and put it down. "But you can. If you want. Now you know."

Sol put the tea down on the floor beside the chair, carefully, so it would not spill.

"If the *Assessor* can stop the clock by signal," she said, "then they can stop it if they're not on the *Assessor*."

Haddad looked at her.

"If they're still here," said Sol. "On the platform. On Day Two Eighty-Eight. Lind and the Director and Lusk and all of you. If they're still here when the clock runs out, then they're under the front with us." She stood up. "And then I think somebody will find a way to stop it."

* * *

There were two hundred people in the ballast hold on the night of Day Two Hundred and Sixty-Three. Sol had not known there were so many on the platform who would come. They came down the ladder all night, in twos and threes, through the cold, past the Proctors' dead cameras, and sat on the ore crates and stood along the walls and crowded into the aisles between the stacks until there was no more room, and the air went warm and close with their breath, and the two work lamps lit their faces from below.

She told them.

She had not meant to be the one. She had gone to Teague with it, and Teague had listened, sitting on his bunk with his hands on his knees, and then he had said, *you tell them.* She had said she couldn't. He had said, *they won't believe it from me. I've been telling them the Company's going to kill them for forty years, and the Company hasn't, mostly, and so they think I'm an old man with a cough. They'll believe it from you, because you never told them anything.*

So she stood on a crate in the middle of the ballast hold with two hundred faces turned up at her, and she unfolded the sheet with the blue letterhead, and she told them. All of it. The policy. The date. The skirt. The *Mercy*. The *Assessor*, on Day Two Eighty-Five. The clock in the Spindle. First light, which was a wave. She did not say where she had got it from. She said *I've had it confirmed by someone who'd know*, and people looked at each other, and nobody asked.

When she stopped there was no sound at all.

Then somebody began to cry. She did not see who. And somebody else said, quite loudly, "That's not true," and somebody else said, "Shut up, Danek, it's on the paper," and then everybody was talking at once.

It went on a long time. It went the way such things go: people shouting, and people crying, and people saying it could not be true and people saying of course it was true, what did you think, and somebody wanting to go to the Director, and somebody wanting to go to the galley and tell everybody, now, tonight, and somebody else saying they'd be shot in the spine corridor before they'd got halfway; and old Mother Szell, in her voice like a rusty hinge, saying over and over, *I told you, I told you, I said in '64 they'd do it one day, I told you,* until someone hushed her. Teague did not speak. Sol had thought he would, and he sat on his crate by the ladder with his cloth in his hand and coughed and watched her.

Oyelaran stood up.

"What do we do?" he said. He did not shout it. He said it in his ordinary voice, and the ballast hold went quiet to hear him. "Dace. You brought it. What do we do?"

They all looked at her again.

She had thought about it for two days. She had lain on her bunk with her face to the marks and her fingers on them and thought about nothing else. She had gone out on the Ring for two eleven-hour shifts and called the Count on the collar and thought about it with every bolt.

"They leave on Two Eighty-Five," she said. "First shift. On the *Assessor*. All of them." She looked round. "So they don't leave."

"How do we stop them leaving?"

"The *Assessor* docks at the head of the spine. On the mooring arm. There's one way onto her from the platform: the dock lock at the top of the spine, past the command deck. If we hold the command deck and the dock lock, nobody gets on her. And if nobody gets on her, they're still here on Two Eighty-Eight. Lind. Castellane. Lusk. Haddad. All forty of them. And the clock's still running." She paused. "And then they'll stop it. Because they'll have to. Because if they don't, they're under the front with us."

"And the Proctors?" said Ruzicka. "Twenty Proctors. With guns."

"Twenty Proctors. Four hundred of us." Sol looked at her. "And they don't want to die either. If it comes to it, they'll do the sum the same as anybody."

"You want to take the command deck," said the boy from Gang Eleven, slowly, as though he were tasting it. "You want to—that's mutiny. That's not a strike. That's mutiny."

"I don't want anything," said Sol. "I want a bowl of fish soup on Pier Six. I don't care what it's called. I care that on Day Two Eighty-Eight the people who decided this are standing in the same place as the people they decided it about. That's all. Then we'll see what they decide."

The hold was quiet.

Teague stood up then. It took him a while. He stood by the ladder, holding the rung, and when he spoke his voice was hoarse and thin, and the hold went so still to hear him that Sol could hear the ore ticking in the cold.

"We did it on the Ostrava docks in '64," he said. "Thirty thousand of us. We shut the docks for eleven days. And they brought in the Proctor school, the whole school, cadets and all, and they broke it, and they hanged nineteen men on the cranes at Pier Six for a week where the ships coming in could see them." He coughed. "I was one of the ones they didn't hang. I was nineteen. I've thought about those cranes every day of my life." He looked round at them. "Here's what I learned. You don't kill anybody. Not one. Not a Proctor, not a clerk, not a Director. You take, and you hold, and you don't kill, because the minute you kill somebody they'll stop talking about what they did and start talking about what you did. And you don't break. Once you've started you don't break, not for promises, not for threats, not for anything, because they'll promise you anything to make you break, and then they'll hang you." He sat down again, slowly. "That's all. Dace has the rest."

There was a long silence.

"Day Two Eighty-Four," said Sol. "Third shift. After the clock's armed and before they board. We go at shift change, when the spine's full and nobody's looking twice at anybody. Gang chiefs, come to me tomorrow, one at a time, in the galley. I'll tell you where." She looked round at all of them. "Anybody who doesn't want to be part of it, leave now. Go up the ladder. Nobody will say anything. Nobody will think anything."

Nobody went up the ladder.

At the edge of the lamplight, sitting on a crate against the ore stacks, with her knees drawn up and her arms round them, Juno Szabo was looking at Sol with an expression Sol could not read at all. When their eyes met Juno looked down.

* * *

Pell came back from the infirmary that night with his arm in its blue gel sleeve strapped across his chest, and sat on his bunk in the middle on the left, and looked at the rest of them, and said, "What's happened?"

They told him. Sol told him. She sat on the end of her bunk and told him all of it, while Bracken made stew on the hot plate and Juno lay on her bunk with her face to the wall and Teague coughed. When she had finished, Pell did not say anything for a long time.

"What does it feel like?" he said at last. "The front. First light. If it—if it comes. What's it like?"

"I don't know."

"Does it hurt?"

"I don't know, Okafor."

He looked down at his arm in its blue sleeve.

"My sisters think I'm going to come home," he said. "In twelve years. They've got a—there's a date. On the kitchen wall. My little sister drew it. A calendar. They cross off a day every night after supper." He laughed, a little. "Like you."

"Like me," said Sol.

"I don't want them to get to the end of it," he said, "and nobody comes."

"They won't," said Sol. She did not know whether it was true. She said it anyway. "Eat Bracken's stew and go to sleep."

He ate Bracken's stew. He did not go to sleep; none of them did. After a while the lights went down to the night setting, blue, and the platform made its night sounds, and Sol lay on her bunk with her face to the wall and her fingers on the marks.

"Sol," said Teague, in the dark.

"What."

"Can I tell you something about Dov."

She did not answer. He took it for yes; or he would have told her anyway.

"He reported the clips sixteen times," Teague said. "You know that. You know that because it came out at the inquiry, and Fenn read the sixteen dates into the record, and the Director said the reports had been received and logged and were under review. That's what you know." He coughed. "What you don't know is the seventeenth."

She lay very still.

"He wrote a seventeenth," said Teague. "The day before. Not to the Company. To the yards. To the factory on Halvard Four that made them. He found their address in the stores manifest and he wrote them a letter, on paper, by hand, and he gave it to the courier pilot on the shuttle with a bottle of shine and asked him to post it on Ostrava. He told me. He came down to the ballast hold that night—he'd been coming to the ballast hold for a year, Sol, did you know that? No. He didn't want you to know. He said you'd be angry. He said you were counting your days, and you'd think he was going to cost you some." Teague was quiet. "He came down and told me he'd sent it. He was pleased with himself. He said the Company could ignore a rigger but it couldn't ignore its own supplier telling it the clips weren't rated for side-loading. He said, *they'll have to change them now, Teague, they'll have to.*"

"And?"

"And the next day his clip went, at the Spindle, swinging out," said Teague. "And the courier pilot came back on the next run and found me in the galley and gave me the letter back. Still sealed. He said a Proctor met him on the dock at Ostrava and took his bag and went through it and found the letter and read the address and gave it back to him and told him to return it to sender." Teague coughed. "So he returned it to me. Because the sender was dead."

Sol said nothing.

"I've still got it," said Teague. "In my kit. I never opened it. I didn't think it was mine to open." He was quiet for a long time. "I thought you'd want to know. Now. Before."

She lay in the blue dark with her face to the wall. Her fingers were on the marks. She ran them along the bottom rows, the days, ten and ten and ten, feeling the ridges the scribe had raised; and she counted them, as she counted them every night, the ones without lines through, to the end.

Thirty-eight. Thirty-eight days, still, to Day Three Hundred. To the *Mercy*, which had been reassigned to the Halvard yards on Day Two Thirty and was never coming.

She took the scribe out of the thigh pocket of her liner. In the dark, by feel, she found the place in the rows where Day Two Eighty-Eight would be—twenty-five marks along from the last one she had crossed—and with the point of the scribe, carefully, she drew a line under it. A short hard line, across the bottom of that mark and the ones on either side, cut deep into the polymer, deeper than anything else on the wall, so deep that the scribe's point squealed and she felt it through her teeth.

Then she put the scribe away and lay with her fingers on the line she had cut and did not sleep at all.
