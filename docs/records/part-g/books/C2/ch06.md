# Chapter 6: Tribute

*The Dark · Year 419*

Veska went down the stair to the dock floor alone, with her lamp in one hand and her iron bar in the other, and the rest of us stayed at the rail and watched her go.

Aunt Veska had been the boss of Hold Fourteen since before I was born. She was not anybody's aunt that I knew of. She was a broad short woman with grey hair cropped close and forearms like a walker's, though she had never walked; she had worked the lungs, as a girl, down in the deep levels, and had come up out of them at thirty with a cough she never lost and a temper she never lost either, and the Hold had made her boss because nobody else wanted to be and because she was the only one who could make the lungs go when they stopped. She was not kind. She was fair, most of the time, which in the Dark is better. She walked across the dock floor toward the Reeve of Brine's ship with the iron bar resting on her shoulder as though she were going to mend a pipe, and stopped ten paces from the foot of the ramp, and set the end of the bar down on the deck with a clang that rang all round the dock.

"This is Hold Fourteen," she said. "I'm the boss of it. Who opened my lock?"

The big man in the long coat stopped coughing.

"I did," he said. "I've got the old words for it. The Keepers' words. I'm sorry for the fright." He did not sound sorry. He did not sound anything; his voice was deep and slow and a little hoarse, as though he had worn it out shouting a long time ago and had not bothered to get it mended. "I'm Kine. Absalom Kine. Reeve of Brine."

"I know who you are. Everybody knows who you are." Veska did not move. "You're not the water-boat. The water-boat's not due till the thaw month."

"No. I'm not the water-boat." He looked round the dock, slowly, at the junk piled in its corners, at the dead cranes, at the old letters on the walls. "But I brought water."

He lifted his hand, and two of his armored men went up the ramp into the ship and came down again a moment later rolling a tank between them, a big one, the kind the water-boats carry, frosted over and smoking in the air of the dock. They rolled it to the foot of the ramp and stood it up, and it rang like a bell when it settled, full. Then they went back up and brought another. And another. Six, in the end, in a row at the foot of the ramp, smoking.

At the rail beside me, somebody let out a breath.

Six tanks was a month's water for Hold Fourteen. More than a month. We had been on half-ration since the hot month, because the last water-boat had come in short, and the one before that. Six tanks was baths for the children. Six tanks was the algae vats topped up and the lungs' cooling loops refilled and nobody having to choose, for a while, whether to drink or to wash a wound.

"That's a gift," said the Reeve of Brine. "No price. That's to say I'm sorry for the fright."

"Nothing from Brine comes without a price," said Veska.

"No," the Reeve agreed. "But that does. Those six are a gift. What I've come to talk about is the rest."

He coughed again, then, a long rattling cough that bent him over and made the man he was leaning on take his weight, and the sound of it went round the dock and came back off the walls, and every one of us on the gallery knew what it was. You cannot live in Tithe and not know it. It was the rot. The Reeve of Brine had the rot in his chest, deep, the way Quill had it, the way Pim had had it at the end.

When it was done, he straightened, and wiped his mouth on the back of his glove, and looked up at the gallery again, along the rail, to where I was standing.

"I want to talk to your Tally," he said.

* * *

Quill would not be carried.

I had gone up for him, running, the whole way up the stair from the dock to the Tallies' room, with Ness behind me; and I had found him already sitting up on his bunk under the marks with his coat on and his stick in his hand, as though he had heard the big lock and known exactly what it meant. He was breathing very badly. When I tried to tell him what had happened he held up his hand, and I stopped.

"The Reeve of Brine," he said. "With his ship and his men and a present."

"Six tanks."

"Six." He nodded slowly. "He's not stupid, then. Good. I'd rather a clever one."

"He wants you."

"He wants the Tally," said Quill. "Help me up."

Ness and I got him up between us. He was so light. He stood swaying, with his hand gripping my arm through my sleeve, and his blind eyes turned toward the door, and he got his breath, and he said: "We walk. Down. All the way."

"Quill, it's eleven flights—"

"Then it'll take us a long time," he said, "and he'll have to wait for us. And everybody on that dock will see the Tally of Hold Fourteen come down the stair on his own feet, and not be carried down like a sack of old filters. And so will he." He squeezed my arm. "You think that doesn't matter. It matters. Walk."

We walked. It took nearly an hour. We had to stop on every landing, and on some of them twice, while he leaned on the wall and breathed with a sound like a lung's bellows full of grit, and I held him up and Ness held him up and neither of us said anything. The Hold had come out onto the stair. They stood in the doorways on every landing and watched us pass, and nobody said anything either, and some of them touched his coat as he went by, very lightly, the way you touch the funeral cloth.

When we came out onto the dock floor the Reeve was sitting on one of his own water tanks, with his long coat spread round him, waiting.

He had not gone back aboard. He had not sent anyone to hurry us. He had sat there on the cold tank for an hour, with the rot in his chest, and his men standing around him in their armor, and Veska standing across from him with her iron bar, and the Hold watching from the gallery, and he had waited. I did not know whether that was a good thing or a bad thing. When he saw us he stood up, slowly, and I saw that it cost him nearly as much as the stair had cost Quill.

He was big. Bigger than he had looked from the rail. Not fat; broad, made on a large frame, with a great head of grey hair going white and a grey beard cut close, and a face that had been handsome once and had been hit a great deal since. His coat was old. Older than him, much older. It was dark green, or had been, and it had brass at the cuffs, and the remains of some kind of insignia on the shoulders that had been picked off long ago and left darker shapes behind. Under it he wore armor like his men's, plates over padding. He had grey eyes, very pale. He looked at Quill, and then he looked at me, and then at the white string at my throat, and then back at Quill.

"Tally," he said. "Thank you for coming down."

"Reeve," said Quill. "Thank you for the water."

"You heard about that?"

"I'm blind," said Quill. "Not deaf. You could hear those tanks ring in Hold Ninety."

The Reeve's face moved, a little. It might have been a smile.

"Hold Ninety," he said. "Yes. I've been to Hold Ninety. I've been to all of them that still answer a lock. Ninety, and the Spokeholds, and the Ninth, and the Tens. I've been to their Tallies." He paused. "Ninety's Tally was a woman named Anselm. Did you know her?"

"I knew her."

"She died. In the hot month. On Brine." He said it flatly, like a man reporting the weather. "She was old, and she had the rot like you, and the cold of the crossing was too much for her, and she died. I'm sorry for it. She was a good woman. She sang me the Count before she went." He coughed, briefly. "But she didn't sing it all. She said she didn't know it all. She said Hold Ninety's line had broken in her grandmother's time, a Tally who died before she'd finished teaching her girl, and since then they'd had only the numbers and half the Door, and they'd been singing it that way for a hundred years. She said if I wanted the whole of it I should come here. To Fourteen. She said yours was the oldest line, and the only one that had never broken." He looked at Quill. "So I've come. And I'd like to hear it."

"The Count is for the dead," said Quill.

"Then sing it for mine. I've sent enough of them out the lock. More than you, Tally, I'd guess. A lot more." The pale grey eyes did not move. "Sing it for them."

Quill was quiet for a long time. His hand was on my arm. I could feel it shaking, and I did not know whether it was the stair or something else.

"Why?" he said at last.

"Because I'm going to wake the Halo," said the Reeve of Brine.

The dock was very quiet.

"Brine is dying," said the Reeve. He said it the same way he had said that the Tally of Ninety was dead: flatly, without weight, as if he had said it so many times that the words had worn smooth. "Not quickly. Not this year. But the ice is going. The roof. Brine's city is under the ice, you know that, it was cut into it in the Light, and the ice is the roof, and the ice is going rotten from underneath, because the old heat-pumps that kept it frozen are dying like your lungs are dying. Every year we lose a district. Last year it was two. In ten years it'll be the whole city, and then there won't be any more water-boats, Boss," he said to Veska, "because there won't be any Brine to send them. And the rot's in the city now. In me. You can hear it." He did not cough, as if to show that he would not. "Everything in the Reach that works was made in the Light. Everything. The lungs. The pumps. The ships. And everything that was made in the Light ran on the Light. The old records say so. They ran on it the way a lamp runs on oil, and when the Light went, they started to run down. And they've been running down for four hundred years, and now they're nearly stopped." He looked up, past the gallery, past the dock's dead lights, as though he could see through the hull to the Halo. "So I'm going to light it again."

Somebody on the gallery made a sound. Not a word. A kind of moan. I looked up and saw that it had come from old Geller, who kept the Lamplighters' little chapel on the ninth level, and that he had both hands pressed over his mouth, and that his eyes were full of tears.

"You can't light it," Veska said. "Nobody can. Nobody's even been to the Spindle in a hundred years. The ones who tried didn't come back."

"I know how many tried. I know how many didn't come back. I've got their names." The Reeve did not look away from Quill. "And I know that the Keepers left words behind them. A Rule. Most of it's lost. Some of it isn't. I've got a piece of it on Brine, an old book, burned, and the piece I've got says this." He spoke the words slowly, as if he had learned them by heart: "*The Knot is the Founders', and shall not be turned.*" He let the dock hear it. "Nobody knows what the Knot is. Nobody knows anything. But your Tallies have a rule too, don't they? Never sing the Count on the Halo. Ninety's Tally told me that one. She told me it was the First Tally's rule, the very first one, older than any of the knots on any of your strings." He leaned forward a little. "Why would you have a rule about where you could sing a funeral song, Tally? Unless it was about more than funerals?"

Quill said nothing.

"Sing it for me," said the Reeve. "That's all I'm asking, today. Sing it for my dead. All of it."

And Quill did.

He let go of my arm. He stood by himself on the dock floor with his stick in both hands, swaying a little, his blind face lifted toward the big man; and he took the breath all the way down, the way he had taught me, though I could hear it catch and rattle as it went; and he began. Not loud. As though he were speaking to someone just beside him.

His voice was a thread. But the dock was so quiet that the thread carried, all the way up to the gallery and round the walls.

"Ten for the Company," Quill sang. "Ten, and stop. Nine for the drop and eight for the dark."

I stood behind him and sang it with him in my head, the way I always did, keeping him true. Seven. Six and five. Four and three, two and one. All the numbers, all right. Then the Door.

"Twice to the dark," Quill sang, "and once to the light."

I stopped singing in my head.

"Seat it and pin it and turn it tight," Quill sang, "sun on your right and the Kettle above, dog to the place where the old ones go—"

It was wrong.

It was all wrong. Every line of the Door. Not much—a word here, a turn there—but wrong, every one, and in the wrong order, and in a voice that did not waver at all, so that anyone who did not know it would have thought he had sung it a thousand times exactly that way. I stood behind him with my mouth open. I had heard him sing the Count for the dead more times than I could count, and I had sung it with him, and I had been hit with his stick when I was seven for trying to make the low numbers rhyme, *a Tally keeps the Count, a Tally does not mend it*, and he was standing on the dock floor in front of the whole Hold and the Reeve of Brine and singing it false.

"That is the way that we close the door," Quill sang. "Count it back down, we are home once more."

He stopped. The thread of his voice went out across the dock and up the walls and was gone.

The Reeve of Brine looked at him for a long time.

"That's not what Ninety's Tally sang," he said at last.

"Ninety's line broke," said Quill. "You said so yourself. A hundred years of half a Door. Things drift."

"Do they." The Reeve stood very still. Then he smiled, a real smile this time, slow and tired, that showed a broken tooth at the side of his mouth. "You're a liar, old man. You're a good one. But I listened to that woman sing for nine days before she died, and I learned the Door the way she had it, the half of it, and I'd bet my ship your line goes the other way round from what you've just given me." He turned his head and looked at me. "And I'd bet my ship your girl knows it."

I did not say anything. I did not do anything. I kept my face as still as I could and looked at him. But I think he saw something in it anyway.

"Twenty days," the Reeve said. He turned back to Quill. "I'll come back in twenty days. Think about it, Tally. Talk to your boss. Talk to your Hold. And tell them this, so they understand." He looked up at the gallery, at all of us. "The water-boats from Brine are mine. When I come back, if I go away with the Count, the right one, the whole one, then Hold Fourteen gets water from Brine for as long as there's a Brine to send it. A full ration. And lungs. I've got scrubbers in Brine, three of them, whole, from the old districts that went under the ice. I'll send you two. That's the price. Two lungs and a full ration of water for a song." He looked back at Quill. "And if I go away without it, the water-boats don't stop here any more. Not ever. Not for anything."

He turned, slowly, leaning on his man, and went up the ramp into his ship.

And as he went, I saw for the first time that there was someone else at the top of the ramp, in the shadow of the hatch, who had been there the whole time: a thin man, very thin, gaunt, in a long grey robe, with his hands in front of him in iron cuffs joined by a chain. His head was shaved. On his forehead, in black ink, there was a mark I knew, because I had seen it on old Geller's forehead and on the doors of the Lamplighters' chapel on the ninth level: a little flame, inside a ring.

He was looking at me. He had been looking at me, I understood, all through the Count. His eyes were dark and deep-set and very bright in his starved face, and they did not leave me as the Reeve went past him into the ship, and they did not leave me as the ramp began to rise, and the last thing I saw before the hatch closed was the little black flame on his forehead, and his eyes, burning.

* * *

"Why did you lie?"

We were back in the Tallies' room. It was the middle of the night. Quill had not been able to walk back up the stair, and in the end, at the third landing, he had let Ness carry him, and she had carried him up the rest of the way in her arms as easily as a bundle of cable, and laid him on his bunk, and gone. Now he was lying under the marks with his fingers on them and his eyes open, and his breath came and went with that grinding bellows sound, and between breaths, sometimes, there was a long time.

"Sit down," he said.

I sat on the floor by the bunk, with my back against the wall, where I had sat since I was seven.

"Why did you lie?" I said again. "You told me a Tally doesn't mend the Count. You hit me. You said—"

"I said a Tally keeps the Count." He turned his head toward my voice. "Keeping is not singing it to everyone who brings you water. Keeping is keeping." He breathed. "Never sing the Count on the Halo. You know that rule."

"Yes."

"The Reeve of Brine wants to take the Count to the Halo. To the Spindle. And sing it there." He breathed. "That's all he wants. He's a clever man. He's put it together better than anybody has in four hundred years. A rule about where you can sing a song is a rule about what the song does. He's right about that. He's right."

"What does it do?"

"I don't know," said Quill. "I told you. Nobody knows is not the same as no reason. I don't know what the Count does on the Halo. Sorrel didn't know. Abel didn't know. None of us knew. The First Tally knew, and he didn't tell, and he made a rule instead. And for four hundred years we've kept it, and we've kept the song, and we haven't asked." He was quiet for a long time. "That's what keeping means. You keep a thing until somebody who knows what it's for comes to use it."

"And the Reeve?"

"The Reeve doesn't know what it's for. He only knows it's for something." Quill's fingers moved on the marks. "That's not the same."

I sat with my back to the wall and my knees drawn up.

"He'll stop the water," I said.

"Yes."

"If he stops the water—"

"I know what happens if he stops the water." Quill's voice was very quiet. "Veska will know too. And the Hold. They'll all know, by tomorrow. And they'll look at you. I'm sorry for that. I'm sorry I won't be here for it." He breathed. "Ask me again."

"What?"

"The other rule. You asked me, after Pim, and I said not today. Ask me again."

"What's the other rule?"

"Never let one Tally be the only one who knows it." He turned his face toward me. His blind eyes were wet. "That's the First Tally's other rule. The one we don't say in the lineage. The Count must always be kept by more than one. That's why every Tally takes a girl, or a boy, young, and teaches them, all of it, before the old one goes. So that it's never in only one head. Because a head can stop." He breathed. "Ninety's Tally broke it. Not on purpose. Her grandmother died too soon. And then there was half a Door in Hold Ninety for a hundred years, and now there's none, because she's dead on Brine. The Spokeholds' line broke when I was a boy. The Tens' Tally drowned in a vat. There were nine lines when Sorrel was young. There are three now that I know of, and two of them are broken, and the third is in this room."

I understood then what he was telling me, and I did not want to.

"You're the only one," I said.

"I was the only one," said Quill, "until I taught you. Now there are two." His hand found my shoulder in the dark. "And very soon there will be one again. And that one will be you, Tally's girl. And you will have broken the First Tally's rule, through no fault of your own, the moment I'm gone. Do you see?"

"Yes."

"So you'll have to teach someone. Quickly. Whatever else happens. Before he comes back." His hand tightened. "Promise me."

"I promise."

"Good." His hand loosened. He lay back. His breathing was very bad now, long grinding pulls and then a gap, and then another. "What's your name?"

"What?"

"Your name. Your mother's name for you. Before you were the Tally's girl. A Tally gives it up, when she takes the string. You know that. You'll give it up tomorrow, or whenever it is. What was it?"

I had not said it aloud since I was seven.

"Mira," I said.

"Mira." He said it as though he were tasting it. "That's a good name. It means something, in one of the old languages. Something about looking." He was quiet. "Say it once more. Then put it away."

"Mira," I said.

"Good," said Quill. "Now put it away."

I put it away.

He died a little before the morning bell. I was asleep against the wall by his bunk when he went, and I did not hear it, and when I woke his breathing had stopped and his fingers were still on the marks, on the wall above his head, resting there, on the last row, as if he had been counting them in the dark and had lost his place.

* * *

I sent him out alone.

That is not the custom. The custom is that the Hold comes, and stands down the corridor, and sings under the Tally's voice. But the Hold was not speaking to me that morning, or to Quill, or to anybody; the Hold had heard the Reeve, as Quill had said it would, and had gone quiet, and was in its rooms with the doors shut, thinking about water. Veska came. She stood a little way down the corridor with her arms folded, and did not sing. Ness came, and stood beside me, and held my hand while I turned the wheel, and did not sing either, because Ness cannot sing at all and has never pretended to, and I loved her for not pretending then.

I washed him and folded the good cloth back from him at the last, the blue cloth with the gold edge, and laid him on the bench in the little round room in his old coat, with his stick beside him. I put my hand on the wheel. I touched the string at my throat. It was still white. It had his knot on it now, the first knot, a big one with a twist in it, that I had tied myself that morning with my hands shaking, because there was no one else to tie it.

"Ten for the Company," I sang. "Ten, and stop."

I sang it the right way. All of it. The numbers and the Door, every word, in the order he had taught me, going down. My voice did not shake. I thought it would and it did not. I turned the wheel a quarter turn on every line, and through the round port he lay on the bench with his blind face turned up toward the ceiling, and I sang him home.

When it was done and the lock had cycled and the bench was empty, Veska unfolded her arms.

"Twenty days," she said.

"I know."

"He meant it. About the water."

"I know he meant it."

"Two lungs," said Veska. "And a full ration. For as long as there's a Brine." She looked at me, and her face was hard, and tired, and not unkind. "You think about that, Tally. That's all I'm going to say. You think about what a song's worth."

She went away down the corridor.

Ness and I stood by the lock. After a while Ness let go of my hand and wiped her face with her sleeve and said, roughly, "So you're it now."

"I'm it."

"Tally of Hold Fourteen."

"Yes."

"What happens if you die?"

"Then there isn't one." I looked at the empty bench through the port. "Then there's nobody. Then it's gone."

Ness was quiet for a while.

"Teach me, then," she said.

I looked at her. She was looking at the lock, not at me, with her jaw set so that the burn scar stood out on it black.

"You can't sing," I said.

"I can learn."

"Ness, you can't sing *at all*. You sang at the thaw feast once and Barrow's boy cried."

"Barrow's boy cries at everything." She turned and looked at me then, and she was not laughing. "I mean it. You heard him. The old man. It's not supposed to be in only one head. So put it in mine. I'm not a Tally, I don't care about the string, I don't need the lineage. Just put it in me. In case." She held my eyes. "In case something happens. On a walk. Or with the Reeve. Or anything."

I wanted to say no. I wanted to say that it was not hers to have, that it was the Tallies', that it had been kept in Hold Fourteen's line for four hundred years from the First Tally down and that Quill had hit me with his stick for mending a rhyme. I had been the Tally of Hold Fourteen for half a morning.

"All right," I said.

So we sat on the floor of the Tallies' room, under the marks, with Quill's bunk empty beside us, and I put my fingers on hers and put her fingers on the First Count, row by row, the way Quill had put mine when I was seven; and then I began to teach her the Count, from the top, from ten, a line at a time; and she was terrible at it. She could not hold a note to save her life. She got the words wrong and the numbers wrong and the turns of the Door wrong, and swore, and started again, and got them wrong again. We did it until the evening bell. By then she had the numbers, all of them, and the first two lines of the Door.

"That's enough for one day," I said.

"No it's not," said Ness. "Again."
