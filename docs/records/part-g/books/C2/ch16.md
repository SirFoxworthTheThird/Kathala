# Chapter 16: The Dog

*The Combine · FY 2291*

The tug locks were on the command deck, on a panel by the long window that nobody in Gang Four had ever seen, and Haddad unlocked them in eleven seconds.

"That's all?" said Sol.

"That's all." The doctor stood back from the panel. Its lights had gone from red to green, seven of them, one for each tug on the platform. "They're not complicated. They were never meant to stop anyone who could get to this room. Only people who couldn't." She looked at the green lights. "Like most things."

"And the *Assessor*'s guns?"

"Are on the *Assessor*." Haddad turned from the panel. She had found a suit somewhere, not her white one, a grey rigger's hardsuit too big for her in the shoulders, and she was holding its helmet under her arm like a thing she did not know how to carry. "Castellane won't fire on a tug. Not unless Lind makes him. And Lind won't make him until he knows what you're doing, because Lind doesn't do anything until he knows what everything costs." She looked at Sol. "What are you doing, Chief?"

"I told you. Putting a dog back in."

"Into a governor that's breathing. Into a notch that's moving."

"You can tell me when it's still."

Haddad was quiet. Then something in her narrow face moved, very slightly, at one corner of the mouth; and Sol realized, with surprise, that the doctor was trying not to smile.

"Yes," she said. "I can tell you when it's still."

* * *

There were six of them on the *Patience*, in the end. Sol. Bracken, with his chest strapped where the stinger had cracked his ribs, saying nothing. Pell, with his burned arm in its blue sleeve inside his suit, bound across his chest, who had said he was coming and had not argued when she said he wasn't; he had simply been at the dock lock when they got there, suited, with his helmet on, and had looked at her through his visor, and she had looked at the lock, and let him through. Oyelaran, for the crane. Haddad. And Juno.

Juno had been at the dock lock too. She had been standing a little way off from Pell, not with him, in her suit, without her helmet, with her hands at her sides. When Sol came down the spine she had not said anything. She had only stood there, very straight, the way she had stood against the galley wall.

"No," Sol had said.

"I'm Gang Four," Juno had said. "You said so. In the galley. *My gang.*" Her voice was very precise. "Nobody else on this platform can fly the *Patience* like I can. You know that. She pulls left under load and her number three arm sticks at forty degrees and her attitude jets are two seconds slow on the port side, and you need her to sit still off the Spindle's flank while Oyelaran works the crane, and nobody else can make her sit still. Nobody." She had looked at Sol then. Her eyes were dry. "Let me fly her. Then you can put me out the lock, after. I won't argue."

And Sol had looked at her for a long time, and thought about the eleven seconds it had taken Haddad to turn the lights green, and about Teague saying *don't look for them, not now, later*; and she had said, "Fly her, then," and gone past her into the lock.

Now Juno flew her. Nobody else in the bubble spoke to her, and she did not speak to anybody. She flew. The *Patience* went out from the dock and away from the platform and down along the Ring's face toward the Spindle the way she had gone ten thousand times, nine kilometers round the rim, low and steady, with her old Ostrava plate glinting over Juno's head in the light of the board; and the Spindle came up ahead of them, black against Kettle, laced with gold, standing up off the rim like a needle.

"*Assessor*'s turning," said Haddad, beside Sol, quietly, with her tablet in her lap.

Sol looked. On the board a small amber mark, a long way off, was changing its course.

"Coming back?"

"Turning. Watching." Haddad looked at the mark. "They'll have seen the tug locks go green on their own board. They'll see us now. They'll be wondering."

"Let them wonder."

The *Patience* came round the Spindle's flank and Juno brought her in, close, closer than Sol had ever seen anyone bring a tug in to the Spindle, until the rigging hatch into the Barn was right there off the bubble's port side, ten meters, a square black mouth in the tower's flank; and held her. She held her still. Sol had seen tugs held off the Spindle a thousand times and they always drifted, a little, a meter, half a meter, the pilot chasing it with the jets. The *Patience* did not drift. Juno sat in the pilot's couch with her hands on the board and her eyes on the hatch, not blinking, and the tug hung off the tower's flank as though she had been bolted there.

"Go," said Juno, without looking round.

They went.

* * *

The Barn was dark, and the governor was breathing.

Sol had felt it from outside, through the hatch frame under her glove: the faint tremor, the shift, as though something enormous inside were alive. Inside it was worse. Their lamps went out across the dark and touched the governor's gold, and the gold lit and went dark again as the light moved; and under the light, so slightly you could only see it if you held your lamp still on one tooth and watched, the whole ring was moving. A hair one way. A hair back. Slow, and endless, and alive.

"Racks," she said.

The dogs were where Oyelaran's crane had swung them, on the racks on the Barn's wall: twelve of them, side by side, four meters each, dark steel, their squared heads catching the lamps. Oyelaran went to the crane. It was bolted to the Barn's wall on its long boom, folded, its claws hanging open. He clipped in at its control seat and the crane woke, its little lights coming on amber, and the boom unfolded out into the dark with a long hiss of hydraulics you could feel through the wall.

"Which one?" he said.

"Any of them. They're all the same." She looked across the Barn at the governor, at the twelve arms reaching in from the walls with their empty cuffs, and the twelve empty bores. "Number One. It's closest. And it's square to the rack."

The crane took the first dog off the rack. It gripped it by the head in its claws and lifted it, and the dog came off its cradle with a slow heavy grace, four meters of steel turning very slightly on the crane's grip as the boom swung it out into the Barn; and Sol and Bracken and Pell went with it on their lines, alongside, hands on it, steadying it, the way you steady a cassette going in. It took a long time. Everything in the Barn took a long time. There was nothing to push against but your line.

They brought it to the first arm. To the cuff, the great steel collar through which the governor's teeth passed, with its bore standing empty where the dog had been the day before. The crane held the dog's point a meter off the bore's mouth. Sol hung on her line beside it and looked through the bore, past its mouth, into the notch in the governor's teeth that it lined up on; and watched the notch move. A hair one way. A hair back.

"Doctor," she said.

Haddad was on a line by the cuff, with her tablet strapped to her forearm, its screen glowing in the dark. She had a cable plugged from the tablet into a port in the cuff's flank. She did not look up from the screen.

"The cycle's eleven point four seconds," she said. "End to end. The notch is aligned with the bore for—call it a third of a second, at the mid-point. Each way."

"A third of a second."

"Twice every eleven point four." Haddad looked up. "You'll have to drive it in a third of a second, Chief. Four meters of steel. On a crane."

"No," said Sol. "I'll have to get the point in, in a third of a second. Just the point. Ten centimeters into the notch. Then the notch can't move off it, because it's got a dog in it. Then we can drive the rest at our leisure." She looked at the crane. "Oyelaran."

"I heard."

"When she says now, you put it in. Straight. Don't push. Just let it go in. We'll be on it." She looked at Bracken, and Pell. "Hands on the head. When it goes in, push. Everything you've got. Bracken, mind your ribs."

Bracken did not say anything. He put his hands on the dog's head.

"Count me down, Doctor," said Sol.

Haddad watched her screen.

"Ten," she said.

Sol put her hands on the dog's head beside Bracken's. Pell put his good hand there, and his shoulder.

"Seven," said Haddad. "Six. Five."

Through the bore, the notch was sliding. Slowly. Toward the mid-point.

"Three. Two."

*Three for the dog*, thought Sol, *and four for the time*.

"*Now*."

Oyelaran let the crane go.

The dog went forward. Not fast; it could not go fast. A slow heavy slide, the point going into the bore's mouth, a meter of steel and then two, the grinding whisper of it through her gloves, and the notch at the end of the bore sliding toward it, sliding, and the point reaching the end of the bore and going on into the notch—and Sol and Bracken and Pell all pushed at once, every gram they had, against nothing, against their own lines, and she felt the notch catch the point. Felt it, through four meters of steel, through her gloves, in her wrists. A small hard jolt.

And the governor stopped breathing.

She felt it stop. The whole Barn felt it. The tremor in the steel under her hands, that had been there since the twelfth dog came out yesterday, that she had felt in the hatch frame and the cuff and her own teeth—it caught, and held, and was gone. The governor was still. Her lamp lay on one of its teeth and did not move at all.

"It's in," said Haddad. Her voice was not quite steady. "It's in. Ten centimeters. It's holding."

"Drive it," said Sol.

They drove it. Three rounds of the Count on the four big drivers, the way they had backed it out the day before but the other way, *one for the hook*, driving it down into the notch, deeper, *two for the line*, until its head stood a hand's width out of the cuff's face and no more, the way it had stood for three years, *three for the dog*, and on the last round, at *ten*, Bracken said, very quietly, through his cracked ribs, "And damn the Company," and Sol said it with him.

Then they hung on their lines in the dark round the dog's head and breathed.

* * *

She called the *Assessor* from the Spindle, on the open channel, so that everyone on the platform would hear it too.

"*Assessor*, this is Dace. Gang chief, Four. I'm at the Spindle. Answer me."

It took a long time. Long enough that Pell, beside her, said *maybe they're not*—and then the channel clicked.

"Chief Dace." Castellane's voice. Tired, low. "I warned you about approaching the Ring."

"You did. Here's my answer. There's a dog in your governor."

The channel hissed.

"Governor dog number one," said Sol. "Seated. Driven home. Three rounds. Your clock fires in eighty-one hours, and when it fires it's going to try to couple a governor that can't move. Dr. Haddad's here. She can tell you what happens then."

"Dr. Haddad," said Castellane, after a moment. "Is that true?"

"It's true, Imre." Haddad's voice was quite steady now. "The governor can't breathe. When the clock couples it to the field, the field will try to move it anyway. It'll shear. The phase-lines are already partly coupled. The energy will run back up them, all of them, ten thousand kilometers of them, at once." She paused. "The Ring will tear itself apart. You'll lose it. All of it. Thirty years."

"And the platform?"

"Six kilometers off the rim. Some of the Ring will reach it. I don't know how much. Nobody knows. Nobody's ever done it."

"So you'll die too," said Castellane.

"Yes," said Sol. "That's the idea. We all die together. Or none of us does." She held on to her line and looked at the dog's head in her lamp, standing out of the cuff. "Come back and take us off. All of us. Every bond on the platform. Put us on the *Assessor* and take us out past two hundred kilometers, and wait for your first light with us, and then you can have your Ring. Or don't, and there's no Ring to light."

The channel hissed for a long time.

When it clicked again, it was not Castellane.

"Chief Dace," said the dry soft voice, like paper being folded. "This is Gessler Lind."

"I know who you are."

"Then you know I'll want to be sure of the figures." Lind did not sound angry. He did not sound anything. "You understand that the clock can be stopped from the *Assessor*. By signal. At any time. We needn't let it fire with your dog in it. We can stop it, and wait."

"Wait for what?"

"For you to run out of food. Or patience. Or air." A pause. "You have sixty days of stores on the platform. We have a ship."

"And you have a policy," said Sol, "that runs out on Day Two Ninety."

The channel was silent.

"Stop the clock," she said, "and wait. And on Day Two Ninety you've got four hundred bonded labor on a platform and no indemnity and a Ring with a dog in it that you can't pull without coming in here to get it. And then you owe us all completion bonuses and passages home and you can't pay them. You did the sum, Mr. Lind. I've seen it. On a single page." She breathed. "So did I."

The channel was silent for a long time. Long enough that she began to think he had gone. Then it clicked.

"Very good, Chief," said Lind. "Very good. I'll consider it."

The channel went dead.

"What does that mean?" said Pell.

Sol did not answer. She was looking at the board on her forearm, where the *Patience*'s relay was showing her what the tug could see. The small amber mark that was the *Assessor* had stopped turning. It was coming back. And off its flank, separating from it as she watched, a smaller mark. Much smaller. Fast.

"It means he's sending someone to pull it," she said.

* * *

It was Lusk. She had known it would be. There were twelve of them, Proctors in grey armored hardsuits, faceless, and they came in a Company boat, a fast ugly wedge of a thing with its own guns on its nose, and they did not try to dock. They came straight at the Spindle's flank, at the rigging hatch, at the *Patience* sitting still beside it; and Juno, without anybody telling her, took the *Patience* away from the hatch and out and round, fast, so fast the old tug groaned, and put her between the boat and the tower.

The boat's guns fired. Sol saw it on her board, and felt it, a moment later, through the Spindle's flank: a long stutter of impacts, small, hard, like gravel thrown at a window. Not at the tower. At the *Patience*.

"Juno," said Sol.

"I'm fine." Juno's voice was precise. "They hit her arms. Number two's gone. She doesn't need number two." The *Patience* swung again on the board, out and round, and the boat had to turn to keep its guns on her, and while it turned, it was not pointing at the hatch. "She's had worse. Haven't you, love? She's had worse than this."

The boat fired again. The *Patience* took it.

And then the Proctors were coming in. Not through the hatch. Over the tower's skin, on lines, fired from the boat on harpoons that bit into the Spindle's plating and held; Sol saw them coming on her board, twelve marks swinging across the gap and landing on the tower's flank and moving toward the hatch, fast, the way Proctors moved, the way they had been trained to move in the Proctor school on Ostrava.

"Lights out," she said.

They put their lamps out. All of them. The Barn went black. Utterly. A blackness you could not see your own hand in, could not see your own visor in, with only the faint amber glow of the crane's control lights, far off on its boom, and the tiny green of Haddad's tablet, and then Haddad put that out too.

"Riggers," said Sol, very low, on the gang channel. "This is our house. We built it. They didn't."

The Proctors came in through the hatch with their lights blazing.

It was not a fight. Not the way the spine had been a fight. Sol thought about it afterward and could not find a word for what it was. The Proctors came in through the hatch on their lines, twelve of them, with their helmet lamps and their gun lamps lit, sweeping, and their lights went out across the Barn and found nothing, and found the governor, gold, and lost it again; and they had to go forward, across the Barn, toward the governor, toward the first arm where the dog was; and the Barn was forty meters of nothing to cross, with nothing to push against but their lines.

The riggers were already in the nothing. On their own lines. In the dark. Where they had worked for three years.

Sol went across the Barn on her line hand over hand in the black, toward the nearest light, and the light was a Proctor, swinging, sweeping his gun lamp across the governor's teeth, and she came up under him in the dark, under his light, and found his line by feel where it ran back from his harness to the hatch frame, a thin taut cord in the black; and she had her cutter in her other hand, unlit, and she lit it.

The flame was a short blue tongue. It lit her own glove and his line, and nothing else. She put it to the line.

The line parted.

He did not feel it at first. He was swinging, and the line went slack behind him, and he kept swinging, out, across the Barn, toward the far wall, very slowly, the way Kostya had stepped back from the cassette, sweeping his light. And then his light swung round and found the cut end of his own line drifting past him, and stopped. And she heard him on the open channel.

He did not say *oh*. He screamed.

He screamed and grabbed for the line's end and missed it, and grabbed again, and missed, and his light went wild, spinning, across the Barn's walls and the governor and the other Proctors' visors, and he was still going out, very slowly, across the dark, toward the rigging hatch on the Barn's far side, the second hatch, the one that was also open, the one that looked out on nothing; and she watched him go through it. Still screaming. His light going round and round. Out into the black off the Spindle's flank, nine hundred meters over the Ring, with Kettle beneath him filling everything.

She switched the open channel off.

She did not switch it back on for the rest of it. She did not hear the rest of it. She saw it, in pieces, by the light of the Proctors' lamps: Oyelaran's crane coming round out of the dark on its long boom with its claws open, and a Proctor in the claws, and the claws closing; and a Proctor's gun firing, real rounds now, not stingers, little bright flashes in the black, and a rigger from somewhere jerking on a line; and Haddad, somewhere, on a line by the governor, with her tablet lit again, doing something, so that all at once every one of the Proctors' helmet lamps went out together, and they were in the dark too.

And Bracken.

He was at the dog. He had been at the dog the whole time. He was holding on to its head with both hands and his boots locked to the cuff's face, his great dark shape against the faint gold of the governor's teeth, because if they were going to pull the dog they were going to have to go through him first; and a Proctor came at him out of the dark, along the arm, with his gun lamp lit, the only light in that part of the Barn, and Bracken did not let go of the dog. He did not move at all. Sol saw it from across the Barn. She saw the Proctor stop on the arm a meter from him and put the gun's muzzle against Bracken's visor, and saw Bracken look at it, and not let go. And then the flash.

She did not hear it. The channel was off. She saw Bracken's head go back, and his visor go white, all at once, frost blooming across it from a small black hole, and saw his hands stay on the dog's head. They stayed there. His boots stayed locked. He hung there on the cuff's face with his hands on the dog and his head back and his visor white, and did not let go.

The Proctor reached past him for the dog's head.

And Pell hit him.

She did not know where Pell had come from. He came out of the dark along the arm on his line one-handed, with his burned arm bound across his chest and a driver in his good hand, one of the big drivers, a meter and a half of steel, and he swung it the way you swing a flail at a harvest, the way his father must have taught him in the pear orchards on Tamsin's Reach, a long flat arc from the shoulder with his whole weight behind it; and it hit the Proctor's helmet from the side and the Proctor went off the arm and away into the dark, spinning, his gun gone, his line paying out behind him.

Pell hung on the arm with the driver. He looked at Bracken. Sol saw his face, lit by the dead Proctor's gun lamp floating past, through his visor. He was nineteen. He looked about ten.

Then the Proctors were going.

She did not know why, at first. Then she switched the channel back on, and heard Lusk's voice on it, flat and clipped, saying *withdraw, withdraw, all units withdraw*, and saw their lights, the ones that were still lit, going back across the Barn toward the hatch, fast, hand over hand on their lines. Seven lights. There had been twelve. And outside, on her board, the boat was pulling away from the tower's flank; and the *Patience* was still there. Her number two arm was gone and her number four arm was hanging by its cables and there were holes in her hull you could see through, and she was still there, between the boat and the tower, still.

"Juno," said Sol.

"I'm here." Juno's voice was very precise. "She's holding air. Just. She's a good old girl."

"Juno, you did—"

"Don't," said Juno. "Don't say it. I'll fly her home. Don't say anything."

* * *

Castellane called them two hours later.

They were still in the Barn. They had not left. They had taken Bracken's hands off the dog, finally, one finger at a time, because they would not come off any other way; and Oyelaran and Pell had taken him on a line to the rigging hatch and held him there, at its edge, looking out, while Sol said the Count over him, all of it, from *one for the hook* to *home we go*; and then they had let him go. Out. The way Kostya had gone. They had not had to do anything; the Spindle's spin did it. He went out from the hatch slowly, turning, with his visor white, toward Kettle, and they watched him until they could not tell him from the dark. There had been three riggers dead in the Barn besides him, from the Proctors' guns. Two from Gang Nine. One from Eleven, the boy who had said *that's mutiny* in the ballast hold. Oyelaran had sent them out too.

And there were five Proctors who had not gone back to the boat. Sol had not counted them. She did not want to.

"Chief Dace," said Castellane, on the channel.

"I'm here."

"Mr. Lind has done the figures again." The Director's voice was very low. "He's asked me to read you his terms. I'd like to say first that they're his terms and not mine, and I know that doesn't matter to you, and I'd like to say it anyway."

"Read them."

He read them.

The *Assessor* would dock at the platform on Day Two Hundred and Eighty-Seven, at the start of second shift. Every bonded worker on the platform would board her. She would stand off to a thousand kilometers, beyond any reach of first light, and wait there until it had passed. Then she would return them to the platform. Nobody would be left behind.

The clock would not be stopped. First light would occur as scheduled, on Day Two Hundred and Eighty-Eight, at the fourth hour of first shift.

Every bonded worker party to the mutiny of Day Two Hundred and Eighty-Four—"which is," said Castellane, and stopped, and started again, "which Mr. Lind takes to be all of them"—would have their bond extended, for breach, to the term of their natural life. In the service of the Combine. At the platform. Which would need, after the Lighting, a permanent crew.

And the dog would be removed. But not before the *Assessor* had undocked with the workers aboard and was clear. And since the Combine could not verify the removal of a mechanical pin from a distance—since Dr. Haddad's instruments could tell them the governor was breathing, but not that it would go on breathing, not that someone had not put the dog back after the *Assessor* had gone—one person would remain at the Spindle. Until first light. To remove the dog, and to be seen to have removed it, and to ensure it was not replaced. Under observation. As surety.

"Mr. Lind has asked that it be you," said Castellane. "Chief Dace. Since you put it in."

The Barn was very quiet.

"And after?" said Sol.

"After first light, the Combine will send a boat to the Spindle to retrieve you. As soon as it's safe."

"I'll be at the Spindle at first light."

"The Inner Housing," said Castellane. "It's four meters of steel. It's the most shielded structure on the Ring. Dr. Haddad—" He stopped. "Dr. Haddad will tell you."

Sol turned her head. Haddad was on a line beside her, by the dog, with her tablet dark on her arm. Her visor was turned toward Sol. Behind it her narrow face was quite still.

"Forty percent," said Haddad. "Maybe. If the Housing's seals hold. If the front's what I think it is. If you're in the middle of it, in the dark, and you don't move." She did not look away. "Maybe forty."

Sol looked at the dog's head, standing out of the cuff, where Bracken's hands had been.

She thought about the ballast hold, two hundred faces in the lamplight, and Teague saying *you don't break, once you've started you don't break, they'll promise you anything to make you break and then they'll hang you.* She thought about the term of their natural life, which was not what Teague had meant by hanging and was not so very different from it either. In the service of the Combine. At the platform. She thought about Juno's son, Tibor, nine, on Juno's bond; and Pell's sisters, crossing off days on a kitchen wall on Tamsin's Reach. She thought about four hundred people alive on a platform for the rest of their lives, and four hundred people dead on it in eighty-one hours. She did the sum. It came out the same every time. Haddad had said that. It did not help.

"Is there a condition?" she said. "Can I have one?"

"You can ask," said Castellane.

"Dr. Haddad goes with the workers. On the *Assessor*. She reads the governor. Her instruments. Not your engineers. Her." She looked at Haddad. "If she says the dog's out, it's out. And nobody comes near the Spindle till she says so."

The channel hissed. She heard, very faintly, voices, behind Castellane, away from his pickup. A dry soft one.

"Agreed," said Castellane.

Sol let out her breath.

"Then tell Mr. Lind," she said, "yes."
