# Chapter 14: The Knot

*The Concord · 336 AL*

"We hold the Eye," said Teodor Gaunt.

He was standing in the middle of the old bunkroom with a sheet of salvaged plating propped against the wall behind him, and on the plating, in chalk, he had drawn the Spindle. Not well. Idris had given him the shape of it over three nights, the gallery and the Housing and the four quarters and the long stair of the Keepers' Spoke, and Teodor had drawn it as a tall thin box with lines across it, like a ladder, and marked the quarters with crosses, and the north quarter, where Idris would stand at Firstlight, with a circle. There were thirty of them in the room tonight. More than ever. They sat on the floor in the lamplight and looked at the chalk drawing as though it were an icon.

"Not the Hub," Teodor said. "Not the Basilica. We'll never hold the Basilica, not against what came off those ships. Not the docks. The Eye." He put his chalky finger on the circle. "Your brother's at the north quarter at Firstlight, by the Housing, with his hands on the governor. We get twenty people into the Spindle the night before, through the Keepers' Spoke, in habits. He can get us habits. And in the morning, when the Squadron's coming through, nine abreast, and the Prelate and the Factor are up in the gallery watching it like a feast-day parade—we take the north quarter. We take the governor. And we tell them: tear up the Instrument, or the Eye goes out with your Squadron in it."

There was a murmur round the room.

"How?" said someone. The old docker with the hook for a hand.

"Charges," said Teodor. "On the governor. We've got the stuff. We've been saving it for years—mining charges, from Gall, the kind they use in the deep shafts. We pack the governor with them. And if they don't sign, we blow it." He looked round. "They won't let it come to that. Not with forty-one ships in the Eye. Not with the Factor standing in the gallery."

They were all looking at Idris now. He had been sitting against the wall under the marks, beside Lark, with his knees drawn up the way she drew hers, and he had not said anything for a long time. He got up.

"You can't blow the governor," he said.

"Why not?"

"Because it isn't a lock on a door." He went to the chalk drawing and stood in front of it, and looked at the tall thin box with its ladder of lines, and tried to think how to say it. "The governor doesn't hold the Eye shut or open. It holds it *steady*. It breathes, all the time, a hair one way and a hair the other, and that's what keeps the phase from running. If you blow it—" He put his hand flat on the chalk, over the circle. "If you blow it while the Eye's open, the phase runs. All at once. Every phase-tap in the city's drawing off it, every one, the power for every Spoke and every ward and every lung, and the phase runs back up every one of them at once like lightning up a wire." He looked at Teodor. "The Squadron goes, yes. And the Spindle goes. And the phase-taps go, and the Spokes they're in. Maybe all of them. Maybe half. Nobody knows, because nobody's ever been stupid enough to try it. But you'd be killing millions of people to kill forty-one ships."

The room was silent.

"And they know it," Idris said, more quietly. "The Factor knows it. Sallow's answered all his questions in writing, under seal. If you stand at the north quarter with your hand on a charge, the Factor won't tear up anything. He'll stand in the gallery and wait. Because he knows you won't do it. And if you do, he's dead, and you've killed the city, and the next Squadron comes through a year later and finds it already in ruins and nobody left to argue." He took his hand off the plating. There was chalk on his palm. "It's not a threat. It's suicide with extra steps."

Teodor looked at him for a long time. His face in the lamplight did not change.

"Then what," he said. "Keeper. You tell me. You're the one who knows the Eye. What do we do?"

Idris did not answer. He did not have an answer.

"Benno," said Lark, from the wall.

They all looked at her.

"I don't know what we do about the Squadron," she said. "I don't know what we do about the Instrument. I don't know if there's anything. But I know there's a boy in the Levy pens at the Hub docks who's there because of my name, and I want him out." She got up. "Before Firstlight. Before the *Remittance* sails. I want him out and onto a barge to Gall where nobody'll look for him, and his mother with him. That's something we can do. That's something we've done before." She looked at Teodor. "Isn't it?"

Teodor looked at her, and then at Idris, and then back.

"Out of the pens is harder than off the cord," he said. "The pens are locked. Guarded. You'd need somebody inside."

"I can get inside," said Idris.

They looked at him.

"The Keepers bless the Levy," he said. "Every year. On Firstlight Eve. Four Keepers go down to the pens and say the invocation over the drawn, and bless them for the crossing. *One for the hook and two for the line.*" He heard how it sounded. He said it anyway. "It's on the rota. I'll put myself on it."

"And then what?" said Teodor. "You walk out with him under your habit?"

"I walk out with him in a habit," said Idris. "A novice's. Four Keepers go in. Five come out. Nobody counts Keepers." He looked at Lark. "But then he needs a ship."

"Then he needs a ship," Lark agreed, and looked at Teodor; and Teodor, slowly, began to smile.

"Then you'll want Dagny Shrike," he said.

* * *

Dagny Shrike drank on Gall Quay, in a place with no name at the end of a pier, where the ore barges tied up and the air tasted of rock dust and old oil. It was a long low room under a curved roof of bare hull plate, dim and loud, full of barge crews and dock hands and people who did not look like either, and when Idris came in at the fourth bell of first watch in his grey habit with his hood up, the room went quiet for about the time it takes to draw a breath, and then got louder.

She was at a table at the back. He knew her at once from Teodor's description, *you can't miss her, she looks like a crane with a beard*, though she did not have a beard. She was a big woman, broad and tall and heavy-shouldered, sixty perhaps, with a long grey braid down her back as thick as a hawser, and a face like something left out in the weather, and hands covered with tattoos so old they had gone blue and blurred and run together into a kind of second skin. She was smoking a pipe. When he sat down across from her she did not take it out of her mouth.

"Keeper," she said.

"Captain Shrike."

"Captain." She seemed to find that funny. "Teodor sent you."

"Yes."

"Teodor thinks I owe him." She drew on the pipe, and the bowl glowed. "I don't. He thinks so because I took six of his people to Gall last spring and didn't charge him full. That wasn't a debt. That was a mood." She looked at him through the smoke. "What do you want?"

He told her.

She listened. She did not say anything while he talked. When he had finished she took the pipe out of her mouth, at last, and knocked it out on the table's edge, and looked at the little heap of ash.

"Out of the pens," she said.

"Out of the pens. Firstlight Eve. During the blessing."

"And onto my boat. And out past the Hub docks, and the House pickets, and the Guard cutters, and out to Gall." She turned the empty pipe in her blue fingers. "On Firstlight Eve. With the city full of House soldiers."

"You know about the soldiers."

"Everybody on the Quay knows about the soldiers. You can't land three ships' worth of armor in a city without the people who move cargo noticing the cargo." She put the pipe away inside her coat. "And I'll tell you something else everybody on the Quay knows, Keeper, that you might not. Every ship in Tithe's been ordered to stay docked on Firstlight. Every barge, every tug, every cutter. Order came down from the Factor's House through the Harbor Office three days ago. No departures from the end of Firstlight Eve to the end of Firstlight Day. The lanes are closed to everything but House traffic and the *Remittance*." She looked at him. "So anything that leaves on Firstlight Eve's going to be the only thing moving in the sky. You understand what that means?"

"It means they'll see you."

"It means they'll see me." She did not seem troubled by it. "It means it'll cost."

"How much?"

She named a figure. It was less than Oake's, by half. It was more than the Unlevied had; Teodor had told him what they had.

"I can't—"

"I didn't say money." Dagny Shrike leaned back. Her chair creaked under her. "You're a Keeper. Teodor says you're on the governor. North quarter, Firstlight." She watched his face. "You know what I'd like, Keeper? I'd like to know what's coming through that Eye on Firstlight, and when, and in what order. Exactly. Lane by lane. Ship by ship. Nine abreast in the van, people are saying. I'd like to know which nine." She smiled, a little. "Not because I'll do anything with it. I'm an old woman with an old boat. Just because I like to know where the rocks are before I sail."

"That's not mine to give."

"No," Dagny agreed. "Neither's a boy out of a Levy pen."

He sat for a long time across the table from her in the loud dim room under the hull plate. He thought about Oake, eating candied kelp. *It's a number in a book.* He thought about Benno Kask at the foot of the Nine stair, grinning. *Benno Kask, pilot. You write that. Big letters.*

"I'll have it by Firstlight Eve," he said.

"Good." She stood up. She was taller than him, standing; she was taller than nearly anyone. "Come and see my boat. You should see what you're buying."

Her boat was at the very end of the Quay, past the last of the ore barges, in a berth so small and dark that he would have walked past it. She was a cutter. Small, old, ugly, with a blunt nose and stubby wings and a hull patched so many times that it looked like the funeral cloths the Sump wrapped its dead in, a quilt of mismatched plates in a dozen shades of grey; and she had a name on her flank, in old letters, faded and then painted over and then painted again by someone who had wanted it kept.

*PATIENCE*.

"Built before anybody's grandmother," said Dagny, with her hand on the hull the way a farmer puts a hand on a horse. "My mother had her, and her father before that, and back and back. There's a plate in her that says she's older than the Concord. Older than the Light, maybe. Older than the Founders." She looked at Idris sidelong. "You don't believe me."

"I didn't say anything."

"Come aboard, then, and see."

He went aboard. It was cramped and dark and smelled of oil and old pipe smoke, and she took him forward, up a short ladder and through a hatch into the cockpit, where there were two worn couches and a bank of gauges he half recognized and a long low window; and she pointed, without a word, at the bulkhead over the pilot's couch.

There was a plate there. Brass, green at the edges and bright in the middle, bolted to the bulkhead with four bolts polished flat.

*PATIENCE*, it said, in raised square letters. And under it, smaller: *OSTRAVA*. And a number. And words: *TUG CLASS III*. And at the bottom, almost worn away, a single word he had seen before. He had seen it every Levy Day of his life, at the end of the crier's call, and in the children's rhyme in the Sump, and in the Book of the Lighting, and under the Founders' feet in Basilica Square, cut into the plinth.

*COMBINE*.

"Told you," said Dagny Shrike.

* * *

He took the grille off at the second bell of third watch, alone, in the dark, with the Eye breathing below him through the gallery's long window and nobody on the gallery but himself.

He had thought about it for six days. He had thought about it on the stool in the Register hall at the end of the long table, and on the floor of the old bunkroom with Lark's head against his shoulder, and in the Archkeeper's study with the Instrument in his hands. He had thought about it standing in front of Teodor's chalk drawing with his hand flat on the circle. *You're the one who knows the Eye. What do we do?* He did not know. He knew the gauges and the governor and the Register and the Rule. And the Rule's first verse said there was one thing in the Spindle that a Keeper must never turn; and nobody knew what it did.

The grille was held by eight bolts round its frame, and each bolt was sealed with a disk of lead stamped with the hook. He cut the seals with a Keeper's knife, one by one, with his heart going so hard that he could hear it over the governor's hum. He had brought new lead, and the hook-stamp from his own desk, to reseal them after. He did not let himself think about after. He took the bolts out. He lifted the grille away from the Housing's face—it was heavier than he had thought, three centuries of brass—and set it down on the gallery floor, leaning against the wall, and stood back, and looked at the Knot without anything between them for the first time in his life.

It was smaller without the grille. It was only a lump of old steel. A cylinder as thick as a man's thigh, standing a hand's width out of the Housing's face, ending in a squared boss with a deep socket. He lifted his lamp and held it close.

There were marks on it. He had seen them through the grille all his life, fine regular scratches too worn to read. Up close, with the lamp, he could see that they were not letters. They were not decoration. They were scratches made by a tool. A wrench, maybe, or a driver, slipping in the socket, a long time ago, again and again, as someone turned it. And round the socket's mouth the steel was worn bright in a ring, the way a bolt-head goes when a driver has been on it many times. Someone had turned this. More than once. Someone had put a tool in this socket and turned it, and turned it, and then stopped.

And on the Housing's face beside it, cut into the steel, so small and fine that he had never seen them through the grille, there were three short lines. Scribed. Side by side, a finger's width apart, each one the length of a fingernail. Witness marks, a rigger would have called them, or a Keeper laying out a phase-line: marks you cut so that you would know, later, where a thing had been, or where it was meant to go.

He stood there with the lamp and looked at them.

Then he went to the round plate beside the Knot, the plate the size of a cart wheel, and held the lamp to it too. It had been painted over in the Keepers' grey so many times that its bolts were soft lumps. Ten of them. He counted them by touch, under the paint, going round. Ten bolts round a plate. Not in a ring, quite. He had always thought of them as a ring. He saw now, with the lamp close, that they were set so that the bolt opposite each bolt was not quite opposite, that if you joined them across the plate with lines—one to the one across from it, and that one to the one two places round, and that one across again—you would draw a star.

*One for the hook and two for the line.*

He stood very still in the dark gallery with the lamp in his hand.

*Three for the dog and four for the time.*

He had looked it up, two days before, in the Archkeeper's library, in one of the old maintenance manuals in their cracked leather that nobody had opened in a century: *dog*. It was there. In a glossary at the back, in small faded type. *Dog (rigg.): a locking pin, driven through a fixed collar to engage a notch in a moving member and secure it against motion during assembly. Cf. governor dogs.* And then nothing more, because the pages after that one were gone.

He looked at the Knot. A pin through a collar. A cylinder of steel as thick as a man's thigh, driven into the Housing's face and standing out of it a hand's width, with a socket for a driver and a ring of bright wear round the socket and three witness marks scribed beside it.

*Sun on your left and the kettle below.*

He turned. Without meaning to. He turned where he stood, in front of the Knot, until the long gallery window was on his left; and through it, far off, past the curve of the Ring, small and dim and red as a banked coal, Hearth.

And below him, through the gallery's floor of glass, beyond the Ring, filling everything, Kettle.

*Dog in the box where the old men go.*

"Idris."

He spun round. The lamp swung in his hand and threw his shadow huge up the gallery wall.

Archkeeper Sallow was standing at the end of the gallery by the lift, in his nightclothes, with a coat thrown over them and his black stick in his hand. He was very white. He did not come any closer. He stood at the end of the gallery and looked at the grille leaning against the wall, and at the cut lead seals scattered on the floor, and at the Knot standing out of the Housing's face bare in the lamplight; and he did not look angry. He looked frightened. Idris had never seen him frightened.

"I couldn't sleep," the old man said. "I came up to look at the Eye. I do, sometimes, when I can't sleep. I've done it for fifty years." He did not move. "What are you doing?"

"Looking."

"At the Knot."

"Yes."

"*The Knot is the Founders'*," said Sallow, very quietly, "*and shall not be turned.*"

"I haven't turned it."

"No." The old man let out a long breath. "No. Thank the Light." He came down the gallery then, slowly, leaning on his stick, until he was standing a few paces off, and looked at the bare Knot in the lamplight the way you would look at a sleeping animal. "Put it back, Idris. Please. The grille. Put it back and seal it and we'll say no more about it."

"What is it?"

"I don't know."

"You must know something. You're the Archkeeper. You've been a Keeper fifty-six years. You keep the Rule."

"I keep the Rule," said Sallow. "I don't know what it's for. Nobody does. That's the truth, my boy, I swear it on the Light. I asked my master, when I was a novice, as you asked yours. He told me what his master told him. That the first verse of the Rule is older than the rest of it. That it was cut over the grille before there was an order of Keepers. Before the Concord. While there were still people alive who had seen the Lighting. That the first Keepers found it there, cut in the lintel, and the grille already round the Knot, and didn't know who had put either there; and they were afraid of it, and so they made it the first verse of the Rule, and forbade anyone to touch it, and wrote no commentary on it, because they had nothing to say." He looked at the three witness marks on the Housing's face, which he could not possibly see from where he stood. "That's all. That's all anybody's known for three hundred years."

"It's a dog," said Idris.

The old man looked at him.

"A locking pin," said Idris. "It's in the old manuals. In the glossary. Riggers used them, when the Ring was built. You drive them through a collar into a notch in something that moves, to stop it moving. *Governor dogs.*" He turned and looked at the Knot. "It's a pin, Archkeeper. In the Housing. Where the governor is. Someone put it there and turned it, more than once—you can see the wear—and left it. And cut those marks beside it, so they'd know where. And then someone else cut a verse over it saying never to touch it." He looked back at Sallow. "And there's a song in the Sump about a dog and a notch and a door."

"A song."

"The children skip to it. Our invocation's the first two lines. The rest is about—" He stopped. He did not know how to say it without it sounding mad. "*Sun on your left and the kettle below. Dog to the notch. Twice to the dark and once to the light.* That's instructions. That's a procedure, Archkeeper. It's a procedure like the phase tables. Turns. Directions. You don't put directions in a skipping song unless somebody once needed them remembered and couldn't write them down."

Sallow was quiet for a long time.

"And what does the procedure do?" he said at last.

"I don't know."

"*That is the way that you lock the door*," said the old man.

Idris stared at him.

"I was a novice from the Sump," said Sallow. "Did you not know that? Most of us were, once. They've stopped taking us now. But I skipped to it too, seventy years ago, on the Eleventh stair." He smiled, very faintly, and the smile went at once. "I'd forgotten it till you said. I'd forgotten I knew."

"What door?" said Idris.

"I don't know." The old man looked past him at the Knot. "I've wondered all my life, you know. Standing here. Every morning. Saying the invocation with my hand on that grille. I've wondered, my whole life, whether it holds the Light up." He paused. "Or holds it down."

The governor hummed under their feet. Out beyond the long window the Eye breathed, slow and even, blue-white at the rim and darkening at the heart to the color that had no name.

"Put the grille back, Idris," said Archkeeper Sallow. "Please. I'm asking you. Seal it with your own lead. And then go home and go to sleep, and on Firstlight stand at the north quarter and tune the governor for the Squadron as well as you've ever tuned it, and let the Light hold. Whatever that is—" he lifted his stick a little, toward the Knot, and lowered it— "it's been there since before the Lighting. Somebody put it there and somebody told us never to touch it, and for three hundred and thirty-six years we haven't, and the Light has held. Every day." His voice was not steady. "If you open that plate, I don't know what's behind it. Nobody does. It might be nothing. It might be a dead man's joke. Or it might be the end of everything. Of the Eye. Of the city. Of all of us." He looked at Idris with his long sorrowful face, and his eyes were wet. "Put it back."

Idris stood in front of the Knot with the lamp in his hand for a long time.

Then he bent, and picked up the grille, and lifted it, three centuries of brass, and set it back in its frame over the Knot; and put the eight bolts in, one by one; and melted his own lead over each one, and pressed his own hook into it.

Sallow watched him do every one. Then he turned without a word and went back down the gallery to the lift, slowly, leaning on his stick, and the lift took him down.

Idris stood alone in the gallery. The new lead was still warm under his fingers. He put his palm flat on the grille, where the brass was brightest, where three centuries of Keepers' hands had touched it, and he said the invocation, because it was what you did.

"One for the hook," he said, "and two for the line."

And then, because he could not stop himself, under his breath, in the dark, with his hand on the brass and the Eye breathing below him and Hearth small and red on his left and Kettle filling everything underneath:

"Three for the dog. And four for the time."
