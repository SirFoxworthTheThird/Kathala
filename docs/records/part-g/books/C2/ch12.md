# Chapter 12: Brine

*The Dark · Year 419*

It rains in Brine.

That was the first thing I learned there, and the thing I remember first, still, when I think of it: the rain. It does not rain anywhere else in the world. Not in Tithe. In Tithe water is a thing that comes in tanks, and goes into pipes, and comes out of taps in a thin cold string that you catch in a cup and do not spill. In Brine it falls from the sky.

The sky in Brine is ice. The city was cut into the underside of the moon's crust in the Light, Marrow told me, when there were machines to cut it: great caverns hollowed out of the ice a kilometer under the surface, one above another, with the dark ocean underneath them all, and the ice above them for a roof. And in the Light there were heat-pumps in the roof, thousands of them, that kept the ice so cold that it was as hard as stone, and the city lived under it as dry as a Hold. But the pumps are dying, like everything, and the roof is going soft, and so it drips. Everywhere, all the time. A slow cold fall of drops from the blue-black ice overhead, far up, so far that you cannot see where they start; they come down out of the dark and hit your face and your hands and the oilskin coats everybody in Brine wears, and they patter on the roofs of the houses and run down the streets in channels, and the whole city is full of the sound of it, a soft endless whisper, like a crowd talking in another room.

"You get used to it," said Marrow.

"Do you?"

She thought about it. Water ran off the brim of her hood and down the white scar on her face.

"No," she said.

We had come down through a shaft in the ice, in a cage on a cable, the *Patience* left in a dock on the surface with her engines ticking in the cold. The cage had gone down for a long time through the blue dark, the walls of the shaft sliding past close enough to touch, glassy and lit from inside in places by old lamps buried in the ice, so that we went down through pools of blue light and then dark and then light; and then the shaft opened out, and the cage swung out into nothing, and below us was Brine.

I had thought Tithe was big. Tithe is a broken thing on the outside of a ring. Brine is a world inside a world. The cavern we came down into was wider than I could see across, and its roof was ice, black-blue, ribbed and fluted like the inside of a shell, hung with long spears of ice that dripped; and its floor, far below, was a city. Streets and houses and towers and bridges, stone and metal, built in tiers up the cavern's sides like the seats in a theater. Lamps everywhere. Not many—most of the city was dark, the way most of Tithe is dark—but more than I had ever seen in one place. Thousands. Little yellow lamps in windows, white lamps on corners, a long string of blue ones along a bridge. And under it all, below the lowest tier, where the city should have had a floor, black water.

"That's the Eighth district," Marrow said. She pointed down at the water, where you could see, under its surface, roofs. Towers. The tops of streets, going down into the dark. "Went under when I was a girl. The Ninth's below it. The Tenth below that. That's where we dug the lung out of. Took us four months, in suits, under the water, in the dark." She looked down at it. "We lose a district every year or two, now. The roof gets soft, a piece comes down, the ocean comes up. You move up a tier. And then another." She turned away. "The Reeve's Hall is on the Second. We'll walk."

We walked. Up through the city, through streets where the water ran down the gutters and people in oilskins turned to watch us pass, the Reeve's armored men in front and behind and Marrow beside me and Brother Cinder shuffling at the back in his chains. They were pale, the people of Brine. Paler than in Tithe, as though the rain had washed the color out of them. Many of them coughed. Children stood in doorways and watched me with big eyes, and one of them, a girl with her hair in a wet braid, pointed at the white string at my throat and said something to her mother, and her mother pulled her inside.

"They know what you are," said Cinder, behind me, softly.

"How?"

"The string. There are no Tallies in Brine. There never have been. But everybody knows the stories. The Tallies of Tithe, who keep the Count." He was breathing hard from the climb. "They've heard you've come. The whole city's heard. They're saying the Reeve's brought a Tally to wake the Light."

"Is that what they want?"

"It's what everybody wants," said Brother Cinder, and stumbled, and one of the men caught him by his chain and pulled him upright, not gently.

* * *

The Reeve's Hall had been something else in the Light. You could see it in the shape of it: a great square building of grey stone on the Second tier, with tall windows and a wide stair, and over its door, carved into the stone, a lamp. A lamp with a flame, like the ones the Lamplighters burn. Under it were letters, old ones, worn by four hundred years of rain. *CONCORD OF THE REACH — BRINE WATER AUTHORITY.* I read them aloud without meaning to, and Marrow looked at me.

"You can read that?"

"Quill taught me the old letters."

"Huh." She looked up at it. "I always wondered what it said." She did not ask what the Concord was. Neither did I.

They gave me a room on the Hall's top floor. It had a window, a real one, glass, looking out over the city and its lamps and its rain, and a bed with blankets, and a jug of water on a table that nobody would measure. I stood and looked at the jug for a long time. Then I drank all of it, and then I was ashamed, and then I lay down on the bed with my face to the wall, and thought of the children in the dome in Hold Fourteen, and could not stop.

The Reeve sent for me on the second day.

He took me down. Not up, to his rooms, as I had thought; down, under the Hall, through a door of old iron and down a long stair cut into the rock under the ice, where the rain could not reach, so that for the first time since I had come to Brine there was no sound of dripping; only our feet on the stone and his breathing, which was bad on the stairs, and the echo. Marrow came with us, and two men. Brother Cinder came too. I did not know why. He walked behind the Reeve with his chain held in his hands so it would not clink, and his eyes were very bright.

At the bottom of the stair there was another door, round, like a lock, and through it a room.

It was the strangest room I had ever been in. It was round, and domed, and its walls were metal, old, gone green and black, and they were covered—all over, every part of them, the walls and the dome and even the floor under our feet—in lines. Gold lines. Fine ones, close together, running in curves and loops and spirals all round the room, like frost on a window if frost were made of gold. Like the lines on the Halo. In the middle of the room there was a pillar of the same metal, waist high, with a flat top, and on the flat top a machine: a thing of brass and glass and old dials, with wires running from it to the walls, and other wires, newer ones, rough, patched with tape, running to a box on the floor that hummed. Two of the Reeve's people were sitting by the box on stools. Tinkers, by the look of them, with tools in their aprons. They stood up when the Reeve came in.

"This was a Keepers' house," said the Reeve. He went to the pillar and put his hand on it, as though it were a horse. "In the Light. The Keepers kept the Halo, and the Eye, and they had houses like this all over the Reach. Listening houses. Cinder found the word for it in a book."

"Phase-ear," said Brother Cinder softly.

"Phase-ear." The Reeve nodded. "It listens to the Halo. From here. From a hundred thousand kilometers off. All those lines—" he waved his hand at the walls— "are tuned to the lines on the Halo. When the Halo rings, they ring. In the Light, the Keepers sat in here and listened to the Eye breathe, so they'd know if it was sick." He looked at the machine on the pillar. "It took my tinkers six years to make it go. It's been going for five."

"What does it listen to now?" I said. "The Halo's dead."

The Reeve looked at me for a long moment.

"Play it," he said to the tinkers.

One of them bent to the humming box on the floor and turned something on it, and the hum changed, and then out of the machine on the pillar, out of a brass horn on its side that I had not noticed, there came a sound.

It was very quiet at first. A hiss. Like the rain upstairs, like breath. Then, under the hiss, a beat.

A beat. And another. A soft deep knock, far off, like someone striking a great hollow thing a long way away. And another. And then a run of them, quick, together, *knock-knock knock-knock*, and then a stop. And then another run. And a stop.

I stood very still.

It was what Ness had told me. Under the dome in Hold Fourteen, with her shoulder against mine, tapping her fingers on her leg. *A run of beats and then a stop. Over and over. Like somebody saying something.*

"The hullwalkers have felt it for a hundred years," said the Reeve. "In their boots, on the Halo. They call it the Halo singing. They say it means bad weather." He stood with his hand on the pillar, listening. "It doesn't mean weather. I've had my people listen to this every day for five years, Tally, and write down every beat. It's got a pattern. It repeats. It's always the same, over and over, and then sometimes it stops for days, and then it starts again, the same." He turned his head toward the brass horn. "And it's getting stronger. Five years ago my tinkers had to turn the box up as far as it would go to hear it. Now they turn it down."

*Knock-knock knock-knock. Knock. Knock-knock.* A stop.

"Where's it coming from?" I said.

"The Eye," said the Reeve.

I did not understand.

"Not the Halo. Not the Spindle. Not the ring. The middle." He held out his hand flat, palm down, in front of him, and moved it in a circle. "The phase-ear can tell. The lines on the walls can tell where on the Halo a sound starts, by which of them rings first. And this one doesn't start anywhere on the Halo. It starts in the middle. In the hole. Where the Eye was." He let his hand fall. "Where the door was."

*Knock-knock knock-knock.*

"Somebody's on the other side," said the Reeve of Brine.

The room was very quiet, except for the hiss and the knocking.

"In the Light," said the Reeve, "the Eye went somewhere. Everybody knows that. The Lamplighters know it and the walkers know it and your Tallies know it. Ships went into it and came out somewhere else. A long way off. That's what it was for." He looked at the brass horn. "And then it shut. And nobody knows why, and nobody knows where it went, and for four hundred years it's been a hole full of nothing. Except there's somebody on the other end of it, still. Somewhere. And they're knocking."

"The Founders," said Brother Cinder.

We all turned and looked at him. He was standing by the round door with his chained hands pressed together in front of his mouth, and his eyes were full of tears, and the tears were running down his starved face in two bright lines, and he was smiling.

"It's the Founders," he said. "Don't you see? They went after the Light, and they found it, and they're bringing it home. They're at the door. They're knocking to be let in." His voice broke. "Four hundred years. We kept the lamps burning. We kept them burning so they'd see." He turned to me. "Tally. Tally, they're *knocking*."

"Shut up, Cinder," said the Reeve, not unkindly.

"They're *knocking*—"

"I know they're knocking. That's why we're here." The Reeve turned back to me. "I don't know who it is. Cinder thinks it's his Founders with their lamps. Maybe it is. Maybe it's somebody else. Maybe it's the Houses, that the Lamplighters say stole the Light in the first place. Maybe it's somebody we've never heard of." He coughed, and wiped his mouth. "What I do know is, they're trying to open it. From their side. And they can't. And they've been trying for a hundred years, and getting louder, and one of these days maybe they'll find out how." He looked at me with his pale grey eyes. "And when that door opens, Tally, whoever opens it is going to be the one standing in it. Whoever owns the door owns what comes through it. That's how doors work. That's how they've always worked."

"So you want to open it first."

"I want to be the one holding it," said the Reeve of Brine. "When it opens. From this side. With the key in my hand." He turned to the pillar again, to the knocking. "So that whatever comes through comes through to me. Not to every Hold in Tithe with a knife. Not to them, whoever they are, walking through into the Reach like it was theirs. To me. And then we'll see what they've got to say."

*Knock-knock knock-knock. Knock. Knock-knock.*

I listened to it. I did not want to; I could not stop. It went on and on, out of the brass horn, under the hiss, a run and a stop, a run and a stop. And the strangest thing, the thing I did not say to the Reeve or to Marrow or to Cinder weeping by the door, was that it was not strange. It was not strange at all. It was like a word on the tip of your tongue. Like a tune you heard once, in another room, through a wall, a long time ago, and that you would know at once if you could only hear one more note of it.

"Do you know it?" said the Reeve, watching me.

"No," I said.

It was true. I did not know it. I stood in the gold-lined room under the ice with my hands at my sides and the knock going through me like a second heart, and I did not know it, and I knew that I did.

"Turn it off," said the Reeve, and the tinkers turned it off, and in the silence I could hear my own blood.

* * *

They let me walk in the city after that. Marrow walked with me, and two men, at a little distance; but they let me walk. I think the Reeve wanted me to see it. I think he wanted me to see what he was trying to save.

So I saw it. I walked down the tiers of Brine in the rain with the white string at my throat and people turning in doorways to watch me. I saw the Third tier, which was mostly dark, and the Fourth, where the ice of the roof had come down in the hot month and crushed a whole street, and they had not cleared it yet, and you could see a shoe under the ice, and an arm. I saw the Fifth tier's edge, where it went down into the water, and children fishing from it with lines, for the blind white fish that live in Brine's ocean, and an old woman with a basket of them on her head, shining. I saw a Lamplighters' chapel with a hundred little lamps burning on its steps in the rain, the flames hissing and spitting when the drops hit them and somehow staying lit, and old men and women on their knees in the wet in front of them, praying. They had heard about me. When they saw me they stopped praying and turned on their knees and stretched out their hands toward me, and Marrow put her hand on my arm and walked me on, fast.

I saw a door, too, on the Third tier, in an alley, that nobody prayed in front of. It was an old door, iron, set into the stone, rusted shut, and carved into the stone above it there was a sign I had never seen before: a hook. A plain hook, like you would hang a pot from, or a line. Under it there had been letters once, but the rain had taken them.

"What's that?" I said.

"Keepers' house," said Marrow. "There's a few in Brine. All shut. Nobody goes in. They say they're cursed." She looked at the hook. "Kine had that one opened, a few years back, looking for books. Two of his men went in. They came out with nothing. Said it was empty. Said it was full of gold lines, like the ear, all over the walls, and that when they touched them they hummed." She shrugged. "Then they wouldn't talk about it, and one of them drank himself to death."

I looked at the hook for a long time. I did not know why it held me. It was only a hook.

"The Reeve's hiring walkers," Marrow said, when we had gone on.

"For what?"

"For the Halo." She did not look at me. "He's going to the Spindle, Tally. In ten days. With you. You knew that."

"Yes."

"Did you know you can't fly there?"

I looked at her.

"The Keepers left dogs," said Marrow. "At the Spindle. Machines. Old ones, from the Light, still going. Nobody knows how. They sit on the Halo round the Spindle for kilometers and if a ship comes near they shoot it. Kine's lost two boats finding that out. Twenty people." She walked on. "So you can't fly in. You've got to land a long way out, and walk. On the Halo itself. On the outside. In suits. And the dogs don't see you, mostly, if you're small enough and slow enough and close enough to the metal." She paused. "Mostly."

"How far?"

"Thirty kilometers. Maybe more."

I had never walked thirty kilometers in my life. I had never walked one, outside. I had never been outside except in the *Patience*, behind glass.

"So he's hiring walkers," said Marrow. "Every walker in Brine and every walker in Tithe who'll come for water. He sent boats round the Holds last week. He's paying a tank a head." She stopped walking. We were at the edge of the Second tier, by the Reeve's Hall, where there was a yard behind a wall, and a gate, and through the bars of the gate I could see people in the yard, a lot of them, in the rain. "There," she said. "That's them. You'll be walking with them. You might want to look."

I looked.

There were forty or fifty of them, in the yard behind the Reeve's Hall, standing about in the rain, or sitting on crates under a tarpaulin, or checking suits laid out on the wet stone: hullwalkers' suits, patched and repatched like the funeral cloth, every one different, every one somebody's whole life. They were the hardest-looking people I had ever seen. Scarred, and burned, and missing fingers, and thin, with the walkers' look in their eyes that I had seen all my life in Hold Fourteen, in Barrow, in Barrow's boy, in Ness: a look that is always half somewhere else, out on the hull, listening for a seal to go.

And at the back of the yard, by the wall, sitting on a crate with a suit across her knees and a patch kit open beside her, with her head shaved and the burn scar black along her jaw, Ness.

She was looking at me. She had been looking at me, I think, since I came to the gate. She did not get up. She did not wave. She did not do anything that Marrow or the men could have seen. She only looked at me through the rain and the bars of the gate with her face perfectly still, and then, very slowly, she lifted her hand off the suit on her knees and tapped her fingertips on her leg, just above the knee.

*Tap tap. Tap tap.*

Then she bent her head and went back to her patching.

"Know any of them?" said Marrow.

"No," I said.
