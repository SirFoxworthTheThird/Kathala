# Chapter 17: Firstlight Eve

*The Concord · 336 AL*

On the afternoon of Firstlight Eve the whole city smelled of lamp oil.

It always did. Every household in Tithe that could afford it bought a lamp for Firstlight, a little brass storybook lamp like the one in the Founder's hand in Basilica Square, and filled it, and set it in a window to be lit at the twelfth bell, when the old year ended; and for days before, the lamp-sellers went through the Spokes with their carts, and the oil-sellers after them, and the smell of it got into everything, into the corridors and the lifts and your clothes and your hair, sweet and thick and a little rancid. Idris had loved it as a child. He had stood on a chair on the forty-first level with his father's hand on his shoulder, and Lark in his mother's arms, very small, and watched his father light their lamp at the twelfth bell, and all across the Spokes, all at once, ten thousand little flames had come up in ten thousand windows, and his father had said *there*, very quietly, *there's the Light*, as though he had made it himself.

He went down to the Sump through the smell of lamp oil with a folded paper inside his habit, against his chest. It was the transit order. The Squadron's, lane by lane and ship by ship, forty-one hulls, nine abreast in the van, the times of each, written out in his own small square hand from the copy that had been given to the four Keepers of the Firstlight rota that morning in the chapter-house, under seal. It was what he had promised Dagny Shrike. He felt it against his chest all the way down the service stair like a hot coal.

C-14 was full. It had been full every night that week, and tonight it was fuller than ever, people sitting shoulder to shoulder on the floor and standing against the walls and crowding the doorway, forty of them, fifty, so many that the air was thick and warm and the lamps on the floor guttered. They had their bags with them, some of them. Their children. The Unlevied were moving tonight, the ones who could; Teodor had arranged it, a dozen barges and Dagny's *Patience* and anything else that would fly, out to Gall and Brine before the lanes shut at the twelfth bell. Benno was to be on the *Patience*. Benno, and anybody else Dagny could carry.

Idris gave Teodor the paper. Teodor unfolded it and read it in the lamplight, his lips moving slightly, and then folded it again and put it inside his coat.

"She'll have it in an hour," he said. "Thank you, Keeper."

"Don't."

"All right." Teodor looked at him with something that was almost a smile. "I won't."

Lark was under the marks. She was always under the marks. She looked up at him as he came over, and moved her bag so he could sit, and he sat, with his back against the old grey wall. Mother Rook was there, too, which she had not been before. Someone had carried her down from her room in her chair, and she sat in it near the port, small as a child in her shawls, with her black eyes moving over everything.

"You're going to the pens tonight," Lark said. "For the blessing."

"At the tenth bell."

"And Benno comes out in a habit."

"If it works."

"It'll work." She said it as though she could make it true by saying it. Then she looked at him, sidelong, and her eyebrows met in the middle. "What else?"

"What?"

"There's something else. You've had a face on you for six days. Since you went up the gallery that night." She lowered her voice. "Teodor told me. He said you came down after and wouldn't say anything, and sat in the corner like you'd seen a ghost. What did you see?"

He looked at her. Then he looked across the room at Teodor, who was talking to the old docker with the hook, and at Mother Rook, who was looking straight back at him.

"I took the grille off the Knot," he said.

Lark went very still.

He told her. Quietly, with his back to the wall and his head close to hers, under the noise of the room. The marks on the steel; the wear round the socket. The three witness lines. The ten bolts in a star. The glossary in the old manual. *Dog: a locking pin.* The gallery window on his left and Hearth in it, and Kettle under his feet. Sallow at the end of the gallery in his nightclothes, saying *that is the way that you lock the door*.

When he finished she did not say anything for a long time.

"A lock," she said finally.

"I think so."

"On the Eye."

"I think so."

"A lock that does what? Shuts it?"

"I don't know." He looked down at his hands. "I think so. I think somebody built a way to shut the Eye. A long time ago. Before the Concord. And hid it in a song, and put a grille round it and a verse over it saying never to touch it, so nobody would." He turned his hands over. "I don't know why. I don't know who. I don't know what happens if you do it, or if it can be undone, or if it even still works after three hundred years. I don't know anything."

"If you shut it," said Lark slowly, "tomorrow. With the Squadron coming through."

"Then the Squadron doesn't come through."

"And the Instrument—"

"Is a piece of paper with nobody to enforce it." He made himself say the rest. "And the city's dark. Lark. Every phase-tap in Tithe draws off the Eye. The lungs. The lifts. The pumps. The lights. If the Eye's shut there's no power, nowhere, except what's in the cells, and the cells are for emergencies, they last weeks, not—" He stopped. "Nine million people. And the moons. In the dark. With no ships. No Inward. No medicine, no parts, nothing, ever again, unless somebody opens it. And nobody would know how."

"Forever?"

"I don't know."

Lark looked at the lamps on the floor.

"*Count it back down*," she said, very softly, "*and you're home once more*."

He stared at her.

"It's in the song," she said. "Isn't it. If it's instructions. The lock's the Door verse. And then *count it back down*." She looked at him. "That's how you open it again. You do it backward."

"Maybe."

"Maybe." She was quiet. Then she said, in a voice so low he barely heard it: "Do it."

"Lark—"

"I mean it. If it's real. If you can. Do it." Her eyes were very bright. "I don't care about the dark. The Sump's been in the dark for three hundred years. We know how to live in the dark. We know how to make lamps out of fish oil and grow chicory in a vat and fix a lung with wire. You know who doesn't? Them." She jerked her head upward, at the ceiling, at the Spokes and the Hub and the Basilica and the Factor's House, all of it, above. "Together with their issue. Do it, Idris."

Before he could answer, someone touched his sleeve.

It was Mother Rook. He had not seen her move; someone must have wheeled her over, or she had got out of her chair, he did not know how. She was beside him, small and folded in her shawls, and she had something in her lap, in her knuckled hands.

The flask.

"Here," she said, and held it out.

He did not take it. "Mother—"

"Take it." Her gravel voice did not rise. "I told you. It goes where it's wanted. When somebody's in trouble it goes to them for a while." She pushed it at him, against his chest, where the transit order had been. "You're in trouble, Keeper. I've never seen a man in so much trouble. Take it."

He took it. It was heavy, and cold, and smooth under his fingers, and the dent in its shoulder fit his thumb.

"And when you're done with it," said Mother Rook, and her black eyes held his, "take it back where it came from."

"Where did it come from?"

"How should I know?" She sat back. "Somewhere up. It's always wanted to go up. Every time I've held it. You'll know." She looked at him a moment longer, and then something in her ancient folded face moved, and it was a smile, the first he had seen on it. "You've got her eyebrows," she said, nodding at Lark. "Or she's got yours. Go on, now. Put it away."

He put it inside his habit, against his chest.

And the door blew in.

* * *

He did not hear the charge. He felt it: a hard flat push of air across the room that knocked the lamps over and threw the people nearest the door off their feet, and then the noise came after it, so loud it was not a noise but a blow, and then the dark, because the lamps were out, all but one, rolling on its side on the floor, its flame streaming. And in the doorway, in the smoke, lights. Hard white lights, the kind you strap to a gun. Many of them. Coming in.

People were screaming. He was on the floor. He did not remember falling. Lark was on top of him, or he was on top of her, he could not tell. The lights swept across the room, across faces, across the marks on the wall, and men came in behind them, in armor, dark, faceless, helmeted, with long weapons; and behind them, in white and gold, the Wardens' Guard, and a voice he knew, a woman's, hard and clear, shouting *down, down, everybody down, hands where we can see them*. Captain Varro of the Guard. He had seen her on Levy Day, on the Basilica steps.

Somebody did not get down.

It was Teodor. Idris saw him in the sweep of a gun light, on his feet in the middle of the room, with something in his hand—a knife, a docker's hook, Idris never knew—and his scarred face perfectly calm, and he went forward at the nearest armored figure as though it were a door he meant to walk through. The guns fired. Not stingers. Not anything soft. Flat hard cracks in the small room, three of them, four, and Teodor stopped going forward and stood still for a moment, quite upright, as though he had forgotten something; and then sat down on the floor, and lay down.

Someone fired again. At nothing. At the dark.

Mother Rook was in her chair by the port. She had not moved. She had not got down. She was sitting very straight with her hands in her lap, looking at the door, at the lights, at the armored men, with her black eyes perfectly steady; and a round came through the smoke from somewhere, from no one, aimed at nothing, and hit her in the chest. She rocked back in the chair. She did not make a sound. Her hands came up off her lap a little, and settled again.

Idris was crawling to her. He did not know he was doing it. Somebody stepped on his hand and he did not feel it. He reached her chair and got up on his knees beside it and took her hands in his, and they were very small and very cold, all knuckle.

She looked at him. Her black eyes were still bright. She opened her mouth and he leaned close to hear.

"Up," said Mother Rook. "Take it up."

And then the eyes were not bright any more.

He was still kneeling there holding her hands when they took him. Two of them, armored, faceless, one at each arm, lifting him up off his knees as though he weighed nothing. He did not fight them. He saw, as they turned him toward the door, Lark on the floor with a Guard's knee in her back and her face against the deck, looking at him, her mouth open, shouting something he could not hear. He saw Teodor lying still by the wall. He saw the marks on the wall in the gun lights, row on row, and the one at the bottom with the deep cut under it.

And he saw, in the doorway, behind the armored men and the white-and-gold of the Guard, out in the corridor in the smoke, a large soft figure in the brown coat of a senior clerk of the Concord, with a round pink face and very small pale eyes, standing well back, out of the way, with a handkerchief pressed to his nose against the smoke. Watching.

Ferrant Oake met his eyes. He lowered the handkerchief a little. He looked, Idris thought, almost apologetic.

Then they took Idris out past him into the corridor, and up.

* * *

The Factor's House was very quiet, and very beautiful, and smelled of lamp oil like everywhere else.

They took him up by a lift he had never seen, that went straight from the Hub's lowest level to its highest without stopping, with his arms held and a hood over his head, and when they took the hood off he was standing in a long room with a floor of black stone polished so bright that he could see himself in it, upside down, small, grey, with blood on his hands. Mother Rook's blood. And Teodor's, maybe. He did not know. There were windows down one side of the room, tall ones, looking out over the city, and the city was beginning to light its lamps.

Factor Severs was at the far end of the room, at a table, with a slate. Prelate Marsh was beside him, in a chair, in her white and gold, with her pleasant motherly face. And standing a little apart, by the windows, with his hands folded in front of his brown coat, Oake.

"Keeper-Adjunct Vey," said Severs. He did not get up. His voice was light and cool and very clear, with the long vowels of the Inward. "Please. Come closer. You can let go of him."

They let go of him. He did not come closer. He stood where he was on the black stone.

"I'll be brief," said Severs. "It's Firstlight Eve, and I'm sure we all have somewhere we'd rather be." He looked down at his slate. "You falsified an entry in the Transit Register on the thirty-seventh day before Firstlight. The *Clemency*. In exchange for a reconciliation in the Levy, removing your sister's name. Mr. Oake has very kindly brought this to my attention, along with the names of the two other Keepers who did the same, and the reconciliations they were paid in." He did not look at Oake. "Mr. Oake is to be Assessor of the Third Spoke, under the new administration. He has a gift for lists."

Oake inclined his head.

"You've since associated yourself with a body of persons calling themselves the Unlevied," Severs went on, "who have for some decades been removing Levy-drawn persons from the Concord's custody. You were present at their meeting this evening. You gave them this." He held up a folded paper between two fingers. Idris's own transit order, in his own small square hand. "Which is, I think you'll agree, a great deal more than a number in a book."

Idris said nothing.

"The persons arrested this evening," said Severs, "are to be added to the Levy. All of them. They'll sail tomorrow on the *Remittance*, at Firstlight, with the rest. Inward. As it happens, the Levy's the last one; after tomorrow there won't be a Levy. So I'm afraid they'll be the last people ever to go Inward on twenty-year bonds. Rather than the other kind." He glanced at the Prelate, who smiled. "Your sister is among them. So is a boy called Kask, whom I gather you planned to remove from the pens this evening in a novice's habit." He set the transit order down on the table. "That's not going to happen either."

"What do you want?" said Idris.

"I want the Light to hold." Severs looked at him for the first time directly, with his pale narrow face perfectly composed. "Tomorrow at Firstlight forty-one ships of the House will transit the Eye, and the Eye will need to be tuned by hand at four quarters by the four best Keepers in the order, as it was for the colony fleets. Your Archkeeper tells me you're one of the four. He tells me you're the best of them, in fact, after himself. He was very insistent. He came to see me an hour ago." Severs's mouth moved very slightly. "He's fond of you."

"And if I don't?"

"Then someone less good will tune the north quarter. And perhaps the Eye will hold, and perhaps it won't, and if it doesn't, some of my ships will be lost, and I shall be very angry. And your sister will not sail Inward on a Levy bond tomorrow. She'll hang in Basilica Square tomorrow, beside the man Gaunt's body and the other leaders of the Unlevied, where the city can see them on the first morning of the new administration." He said it in exactly the same voice. "You understand. I'm not threatening you, Keeper. I'm describing the figures."

The Prelate, in her chair, folded her hands in her lap.

"It's a very small thing we're asking, Keeper Vey," she said, in her motherly voice. "Only what you've always done. Keep the Light."

Idris looked at them. At the slate in Severs's hand, and the transit order on the table, and the Prelate's folded hands. At Oake by the window, with the city lighting its lamps behind him, ward by ward, ten thousand little flames.

He thought about the gallery at dawn, his palm on the brass. *One for the hook and two for the line.* He thought about Mother Rook's hands in his, and their coldness. About the flask, against his chest, under his habit; nobody had searched him for it, nobody had thought to. *Take it up.*

"I'll keep it," he said.

"Good," said Severs, and looked down at his slate again. "You'll be escorted to the gallery at the sixth bell. Two of my people will stand with you at the north quarter for the duration. They're not engineers, so you needn't worry about their getting in the way. They're simply there to see that you do what you're there to do." He touched the slate. "Oh, and you'll keep the Register for the transit, too. The Squadron's masses. We'll want them recorded accurately this time." He did not smile. "I'm told you have a steady hand."

* * *

They put him in a penitent's cell in the chapter-house, at the foot of the Spindle, a room he had not known the Keepers still used: three paces by two, stone, with a cot and a bucket and a window the width of his hand looking out at the Eye. Two of the House's armored men stood outside the door. He heard them shift, sometimes, and the small sounds their armor made.

At the twelfth bell the city lit its lamps.

He saw it through the slot of a window. Not the Spokes, which were behind him; but the reflection of them, in the Eye. The Eye was a mirror, at night, if you looked at it from the right angle; and at the twelfth bell, in its slow-breathing blue-white face, ten thousand little points of gold came up, all at once, reflected, scattered across the light like seeds. His father's voice, very quietly: *there's the Light.*

The door opened at the second bell after.

It was Sallow. The armored men let him in, and shut the door behind him, and he stood just inside it leaning on his black stick, in his vestments—the full vestments of the Archkeeper, the grey and silver, with the great hook worked on the breast and his old furred line round his neck—and looked at Idris sitting on the cot. He did not say anything for a long time.

"They killed the old woman," said Idris. "Rook. They killed Teodor Gaunt. They've got Lark. She's going Inward tomorrow on the *Remittance*. She and Benno and all of them."

"I know." Sallow's voice was very quiet. "I went to the Factor's House. I asked for her. I knelt, Idris. On his black floor. I haven't knelt to anyone but the Light in fifty-six years." He shook his head slowly. "He was very polite."

"He told me you said I was the best."

"You are the best." The old man came across the cell, slowly, leaning on the stick, and lowered himself onto the end of the cot beside him, with a long breath. "While I was waiting to be let in I saw his manifest, you know. On his table. For the Squadron. He didn't hide it; why would he. Forty-one hulls. And in the fourth rank, three ships of engineers. *For the reconstitution of the Keepers*, it said. Under the House." He looked down at his hands on the head of the stick. "We're to be assessed too. All of us. Every Keeper in the order. Bonds of the House, under House engineers, to keep the Light for the House's ships. Every novice. Tobiah. You. Me." He was quiet. "I asked him whether that was in the Instrument. He said *every person*, Archkeeper. *Of whatever age or condition.* He said he'd thought I'd read it."

Idris said nothing.

"I've served the Light all my life," said Sallow. "I told you it doesn't care who owns it. That it breathes, and that's all, and we keep it breathing. I believed that." He lifted his head and looked out of the narrow window at the Eye, with the city's lamps scattered across its face. "I think I believed it because it meant I didn't have to care either."

"What's the Light, Archkeeper?" said Idris.

The old man was quiet for a long time.

"A door," he said at last. "That's all. That's all it's ever been. A door. And for three hundred and thirty-six years I've told myself that a door doesn't care who comes through it." He turned the stick in his hands. "But doors are made by somebody. And somebody decides who holds the key." He looked at Idris. "Somebody put a lock on this one, my boy. Before the Concord. Before any of us. And then hid the key in a song, and gave the song to children, and cut a warning over it so that the people who thought they owned the door wouldn't go near it." He reached inside his vestments. "I've thought about what you said. Up there, in the gallery. All week. Holds it up or holds it down."

He drew something out from under the grey and silver. It was long, and heavy, and wrapped in a strip of altar cloth, and he laid it across Idris's knees.

Idris unwrapped it.

It was a driver. A great old driver, a meter and a half of dark steel, with a long shaft worn smooth and bright by hands, and a crossbar at one end for leverage, and at the other a squared head, heavy, blunt. He knew it. Every Keeper knew it. It had hung over the altar of the chapter-house chapel for three hundred years, on two brass hooks, under a lamp, and was carried in procession once a year on Firstlight morning; and it was called, in the Book of the Lighting, *the Founders' Wrench*, and it was said to be the tool with which the Founders had raised the Ring.

"Nobody's ever known what it's for," said Sallow. "It's been on those hooks since before the order. The first Keepers found it in the Spindle, they say, the same as the Knot. In the gallery. Lying on the floor under the grille." He looked at the squared head. "I measured it, after you went home that night. With calipers. Against the socket in the Knot. I took the grille off myself and put it back after, with my own lead." He almost smiled. "It fits, Idris. Exactly. To a hair. And the bolt-heads on the plate. Under the paint. I measured them too."

Idris sat with the Founders' Wrench across his knees.

"I'll carry it in procession in the morning," said Sallow. "As I always do. Up the Keepers' Spoke and into the gallery, before the transit, with the four of you behind me and the Prelate and the Factor watching from the high gallery and his men all round. And I'll lay it on the gallery floor, under the grille, at the north quarter, where they say it was found. Where you'll be standing." He put his dry light hand on Idris's wrist. "And then I'll go to the east quarter and tune the governor. And when you need them not to be looking at you—the two men he'll put beside you—you tell me. On the Keepers' channel. The old words. *The light holds.* And I'll make them look at me."

"How?"

"I'm an old man," said Archkeeper Sallow, "in the full vestments of the Light, with a stick. I'll think of something." He took his hand back. "I don't know what's behind that plate, Idris. I've told you that. It might be nothing. It might be the end of the world. But I know what's coming through the Eye in the morning. And I find I'd rather it was the end of the world than that." He began, slowly, to get up off the cot. "Wrap it up again. I'll take it with me. They'd find it on you."

Idris wrapped it in the altar cloth and gave it back to him. Sallow put it under his vestments. Then he stood a moment in the narrow cell, leaning on his stick, looking down at Idris.

"I taught you the invocation," he said. "Do you remember? You were fifteen. In a habit too long for you. I put your hand on the grille and said the words and you said them after me, and then you asked me what they meant." He smiled, and the smile was very sad. "And I looked at you so that you never asked anyone again. I'm sorry for that. I've been sorry for it for nineteen years." He turned to the door. "Get some sleep. You'll want a steady hand."

He knocked on the door with his stick, and the armored men opened it, and he went out.

Idris lay down on the cot. He did not sleep. He lay on his side with the flask inside his habit against his chest, cold, heavy, the dent in its shoulder under his palm, and looked through the slot of the window at the Eye, breathing, with the city's lamps scattered across its face; and toward morning, when the lamps had burned low and gone out one by one, he saw a ship move across it, slow and huge and lit along its whole length, from the Hub docks toward the Third Spoke lane.

The *Remittance*, taking up her station.

He watched her until the window's slot cut her off. Then he lay and listened to the governor hum through the stone under him, and counted its breaths, one, and two, and three, until the armored men opened the door and said it was the sixth bell, and time.
