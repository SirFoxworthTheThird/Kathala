# Chapter 18: The Halo

*The Dark · Year 419*

I had thought outside would be dark. It is not dark. It is the brightest place I have ever been.

There is no air outside to soften anything, Ness told me, so everything is as bright as it is and no brighter, and the shadows are as black as they are and no less: and so you walk on the Halo in a world cut in two with a knife. Where Kettle's light falls, the dead metal of the ring shines purple and oxblood and that slow sick yellow, every seam and pit and scar on it sharp as if it had been drawn with a pen, and the gold lines laced across it glint dully; and where Kettle's light does not fall, there is nothing. Not darkness. Nothing. You put your boot down into a shadow and your boot is gone. Your leg is gone to the knee. You have to trust that the metal is still there.

And above you—I did not look above me, the first day. Ness had told me not to. *Don't look up, not till you've got your feet. Look at my back. Look at your boots. Look at the metal.* So I looked at her back, and my boots, and the metal. But you cannot help seeing, at the edges. Kettle filling half of everything, turning, with its storms. The stars filling the other half, so many and so bright and so close that they did not look like lights; they looked like holes, as though the Dark were a cloth with the Light behind it, and someone had pricked it all over with a needle. And the Halo itself going away in front of us and behind us, curving, a road of black metal thirty kilometers long to the Spindle and on round beyond it, going up, and up, so that far ahead of us it rose like a wall across the stars, and went over the top of the sky, and came down again behind us, and we were walking along the inside of a wheel so big you could not see it was a wheel.

I was sick in my helmet on the first day. Twice. Ness talked me through cleaning it, on the private channel, very calm, the way she must have talked Barrow's boy through his first walk. Nobody else said anything. Walkers do not.

We walked in a line. Eighteen of us, at the start: the Reeve, and Marrow, and four soldiers, and ten walkers, and Brother Cinder, and me. Ness in front of me and a big silent walker from Brine named Hollis behind. Clipped to a line, all of us, a long thin cord that ran from the first walker to the last, so that if anyone's boots lost the metal the line would hold them. Three sledges, dragged on lines by the walkers, with air and water and food and the Reeve's tools, and on the last sledge, wrapped in canvas, a black box the size of a man's chest with a long folded spike on top of it, which Marrow said was the relay. *So that every Hold in Tithe hears it*, the Reeve had said, in the hold of the *Patience*, before we went out. *When I open it. So they know who.* We walked slowly. Small and slow. One boot, and the click of the magnets taking hold, and the other boot, and the click. A kilometer an hour, on the good stretches. Less on the bad.

And all the time, through my boots, the knock.

It never stopped, out there. It was louder every hour. Not in my ears—there was nothing to carry it to my ears, out in the Dark—but in my feet, and my legs, and the floor of my belly, and my teeth when I clenched them. *Knock-knock knock-knock. Knock. Knock-knock.* A run and a stop. I walked to it. I could not help it. After the first day everybody did. You could see it in the line, if you looked back: eighteen people in patched suits on the black metal, putting their boots down on the beat.

* * *

We slept, the first night, in the lee of a thing on the Halo's face that might have been a machine once. A great square housing of dead metal the size of a Hold's dock, frozen to the ring, with a door in its side rusted open. The Reeve's soldiers went in first with their lamps and came out and said it was empty, and we went in, all of us, and sat against its inside walls in the dark with our helmets on and our suits' little heaters ticking, and ate through the feeds, and did not talk.

I could not sleep. Nobody can sleep the first night out, Ness said. She was beside me with her shoulder against mine, the way she always was, and she slept anyway, almost at once, with her head tipped back against the wall and her breath slow in my private channel. I listened to it. And then, because I could not sleep, and because I was a Tally and a Tally keeps things, I began to go through the channels on my suit's wrist, one by one, the way Ness had shown me on the *Patience*, to learn them.

Walkers' channel: someone snoring. Private channel: Ness breathing. Soldiers' channel: nothing.

The fourth channel was the command channel. I should not have been able to get it; my suit was a walker's suit, an old one, and nobody had thought to lock it out. It was the Reeve.

"—told you before," he was saying, very low. "I'm not going to say it again."

"She's a child," said Marrow's voice.

"She's nineteen."

"She's a child." A pause. "And the walker's nothing. The walker's a hullwalker from a dying Hold who came for a tank of water."

"The walker," said the Reeve, "has been sitting next to the Tally for three days with her shoulder against her. Every time we stop. You've seen them. The Tally doesn't look at anybody else." I heard him breathe; the rot was bad in him, out here, in the cold. "The Tally's taught her. I'd bet the *Patience* on it. Tallies teach. That's what they do. That's the whole of what they are."

Marrow did not answer.

"I need the Count," said the Reeve of Brine. "I need it once. At the Spindle. In the Tally's mouth, with my hand on whatever it is she says to put my hand on. And then I need it never to be sung again by anybody who isn't me." His voice did not rise. "That's what a key is, Marrow. A key you can copy isn't a key. It's a rumor. If I open that door and come back across this ring to Brine with every hullwalker and Tally and Lamplighter in Tithe knowing how to close it again, or open it again, or open the next one—then I'm not holding anything. I'm just the first man through."

"So."

"So the Tally and the walker don't come back across the Halo."

The channel was silent for a long time.

"I won't do it," said Marrow.

"I didn't ask you to." The Reeve coughed, quietly, for a long time. "Go to sleep. It's a long walk."

The channel clicked. I sat in the dark with my back against the dead metal and Ness's breathing in my ear and my hands in my lap, and the stone in the middle of me, that had been there since Ness knelt in front of me in the Reeve's Hall with the tape in her teeth, did not get any heavier. It did not need to. It was as heavy as it could be already. I only understood, sitting there, that I had known. That I had known since the phase-ear, since *whoever owns the door owns what comes through it*, and had not let myself say it, the way Quill had said Veska would not let herself say that the lungs were dying.

I did not wake Ness. I did not tell her, then. I turned my suit back to the private channel and listened to her breathe, and toward morning I slept, and dreamed of Quill, sitting on his bunk under the marks with his blind eyes open, saying: *Nobody knows is not the same as there being no reason.*

* * *

We came into the dogs' country on the fourth day.

You could tell. Not because you saw them, at first; because you saw what they had done. The Halo's face changed. For three days it had been dead and pitted and scarred, but whole; now, as we came round the curve and the Spindle grew ahead of us out of a needle into a tower, black and gold, standing up off the rim against Kettle with the band of glass round its waist, the metal under our boots began to be cut. Long straight scars, bright-edged, burned into the dead gold. Hundreds of them, crossing and recrossing, like the lines a child draws on a slate. And between them, here and there, things that had been burned too. A piece of hull. A sledge, black. A boot, with the magnet still holding it to the ring. A suit.

There were people in some of the suits. You did not look. You looked at the back of the walker in front of you, and your boots, and the metal.

"Twenty kilometers," said the Reeve, on the walkers' channel. "From here in, they can see us. Smaller. Slower. If you see one, stop. Don't run. They see running. Stop, and get down flat, and wait."

We went smaller and slower. We went so slowly that the Spindle did not seem to grow at all. The knock came up through the metal, louder, louder, *knock-knock knock-knock*, until I could feel it in my eyes.

The first dog we saw was dead. It was squatting on the Halo's face a hundred meters to our left, in the full purple glare of Kettle, and it did not move. It was the size of a sledge. It had six legs, long and jointed, folded under it, and a body like a flat black stone, and on the front of the body a cluster of glass eyes, and above them, on a stalk, a short black tube. Its legs were rusted to the metal. Its eyes were cracked. It had been squatting there, Ness said, for a hundred years, or four hundred; there was no way to know.

The second dog we saw was not dead.

It was the second day in their country, the fifth of the walk. Hollis saw it first, behind me, and said "Stop," very quietly, on the walkers' channel, and we stopped. All eighteen of us—sixteen by then; one of the soldiers had lost his boots' grip at a seam the day before and gone off the side of the ring before the line could take his weight, and the line had parted at his clip, and we had watched him go, turning, out into the Dark—sixteen of us, stopped on the black metal in the open, in Kettle's glare, with nowhere to hide.

It was ahead and to the right. Two hundred meters. It was coming over a rise in the Halo's face, slowly, on its six long legs, the way an old man walks: lift, and reach, and set down, and the next. Its glass eyes were whole. They were turning. The short black tube on its stalk was turning with them.

"Down," said the Reeve.

We got down. Flat, on the metal, on our bellies, with our visors against the dead gold. I could feel the knock in my cheek through the helmet. I could see the dog sideways, out of the corner of my visor, coming on: lift, reach, set down. Its eyes turned across us. Across the line. Across the sledges. They did not stop. They went on turning, and the dog went on walking, slowly, across our path, a hundred meters ahead, and over another rise, and away.

We lay there a long time after.

"They don't see still," said Ness, on the private channel, very low. "Barrow told me. They don't see still and they don't see cold. They see moving and they see warm." I could hear her breathing. "We're warm, Tally. In the suits. Out here we're the warmest things for a hundred kilometers. They just haven't looked right at us yet."

The third dog looked right at us.

It came out of a shadow. That was why nobody saw it. A long black shadow cast by a ridge of the Halo's plating, where Kettle's light did not reach, where your boot disappeared to the knee; and the dog had been in it, squatting, still, cold, the way the dead one had been, for who knew how long. And we walked past the shadow's mouth, small and slow, one boot and the click, warm in our suits; and its eyes came open.

I saw them open. Red. Little points of red in the black, a cluster of them, very close, twenty meters. And the tube on its stalk turning.

Marrow was between me and it.

I do not know whether she meant to be. I have thought about it since, as I have thought about Cinder throwing the lamp. She was three walkers ahead of me on the line, with Ness between us, and she must have seen the red come up in the shadow's mouth when I did; and she did not get down. She stood up. All the way up, off the metal, tall, in the full glare of Kettle, with the white scar down her face lit purple through her visor, and she unclipped herself from the line, and she ran.

They see running.

The tube found her. A thin bright line of red, from the shadow to her chest, and she kept running for three steps, away from the line, away from us, across the open metal; and then she stopped running and stood still, and there was a small black hole in her suit where the line had been, and a thin white plume coming out of it, boiling away into the Dark. She stood quite still. Then she sat down on the Halo's face, slowly, the way you sit when you are tired, and folded over.

The dog came out of the shadow after her. It walked across the metal on its six long legs, past the end of our line, past Hollis lying flat and Ness lying flat and me lying flat with my visor against the dead gold, and its red eyes did not turn toward us at all. It walked to where Marrow had sat down, and stood over her, and its eyes looked at her, and the tube on its stalk looked at her, for a long time. She was getting cold. You could see it in the way the plume from her suit grew thin and stopped. And then, when she was cold enough, the dog turned away, and walked back into its shadow, and folded its legs, and was still.

Nobody moved for a long time.

Then the Reeve of Brine, at the front of the line, very quietly, on the walkers' channel, said: "On. Small and slow."

And we went on.

* * *

Kettle flared on the sixth day, and Brother Cinder died on the seventh.

The flare saved us. I know that now. The Reeve said it would kill us, when the dosimeters started to chirp, and it nearly did; we lay in a phase-trench in the Halo's face for nine hours, a channel cut in the metal for some purpose nobody knew, a meter deep and a meter wide and a hundred meters long, all of us in a row in the bottom of it like the dead in a grave, while Kettle's white place spread across its face over our heads and the whole Halo ticked and sang around us and our suits chirped and chirped. Two of the walkers died in the trench. Not of the flare. Their suits' heaters failed, in the cold at the bottom of the channel, and they got cold, and they went to sleep, and did not wake. One of them was Hollis.

But when the flare passed, and we came up out of the trench, the dogs were gone.

Not gone. Still. All of them. We could see them, now that we were close: dozens, scattered across the Halo's face around the Spindle's foot, the nearest a kilometer off, the farthest small as beetles. And none of them was moving. They were squatting where the flare had found them, with their legs folded and their eyes dark.

"It blinds them," said Brother Cinder.

He was sitting up in the trench, at the end of the row. He had not spoken in two days. He had walked behind me the whole way with his raw wrists in his suit's gloves and his chain left in a heap on the floor of the *Patience*'s hold, and he had kept up, somehow, thin as he was, and he had not said anything; and now he sat at the end of the trench with his visor turned toward the Spindle, and his voice on the walkers' channel was soft and hoarse and quite calm.

"The Wick has a book," he said. "Of the Keepers' machines. What's left of it. The dogs are old. Older than the Dark. They were made to guard the Spindle and they have no one to tell them to stop, so they don't. But they were made in the Light, when Kettle-weather was something you sheltered from behind the Mantle, and nobody thought to make them stand it. A big flare blinds them. For a day, the book says. Sometimes two. Until their eyes heal." He turned his visor toward me. "We should go now."

We went. Faster, now. Not small and slow. Eleven of us. Across the open metal toward the Spindle's foot, past the squatting dogs with their dark eyes, close enough to one of them, once, that I could have reached out and touched its folded leg; and it did not move. The Spindle went up in front of us. Up and up. It filled the whole of the sky ahead, a wall of black metal laced with dead gold, going up out of the top of my visor to the band of glass round its waist, nine hundred meters, and beyond.

At the foot of it, there was a door.

And around the door, a ring of dogs.

Six of them. Squatting on the Halo's face in a half-circle round the door's mouth, a hundred meters out from it, facing outward. Their eyes were dark, like the others. But as we came across the last open stretch toward them, one of them moved.

Only a little. One leg, unfolding. Lifting, and reaching, and setting down. Its eyes did not open. But the tube on its stalk turned, slowly, toward us.

"They're waking," said Brother Cinder.

We stopped.

"These ones are closer to the Spindle," he said. "Under its shadow. They took less of the flare." Another one moved. A leg. Another. "They'll have their eyes back in an hour. Less."

"Then we go through now," said the Reeve. "Before they—"

"They see moving," said Cinder. "They're not blind enough to miss eleven people walking through the middle of them. Not this close." He was quiet for a moment. "One, maybe."

Then he unclipped himself from the line.

I knew what he was going to do the moment he did it. I think we all did. Ness said "*No*—" on the walkers' channel, and the Reeve said "Cinder," sharp, and I did not say anything at all; I only turned and looked at him, through my visor and his, in Kettle's purple light, standing on the Halo's face a little apart from us with the line's end drifting from his hand.

"I said it doesn't belong to anybody," said Brother Cinder. "In the hold. Do you remember, Tally? When he held out the lamp."

"I remember."

"I've thought about it. All the way here. All the way across." His voice was very soft. "I've believed all my life that the Founders would come home through the Eye with the Light in their hands. I believed it so much I couldn't see anything else. And then I saw the Reeve's face in the phase-ear room, listening. And the bald man's in the hold, holding out the lamp. And yours." He was quiet. "And I thought: whoever's on the other side of that door, they're not going to come home to anybody if all that's waiting on this side is people who want to own it." He reached into the front of his suit, the way the bald man had, and took something out, and held it out to me in his glove. "Here."

It was the key. The Keepers' key, that had opened the hatch on the *Patience*. A plain thing, brass, long, with a bit at one end shaped like nothing I knew.

"It'll open anything the Keepers made," he said. "The Wick has kept them since the Light. I think it'll open that." He nodded at the door in the Spindle's foot. "Take it. Don't give it to him."

I took it.

"What are you going to do?" I said, though I knew.

"Something terrible and beautiful," said Brother Cinder, and I could hear that he was smiling. "Go when they turn."

He turned away from us. He walked out across the Halo's face. Not small, and not slow. He walked the way you would walk across a Hold's dock, upright, at an ordinary pace, with his arms swinging; away from us, away from the door, out into the open metal to the left of the ring of dogs, in the full glare of Kettle. And as he walked he did something to his suit, at the wrist, and its lamps came on. All of them. Every light on it, at once, white, blazing, so that he was a star walking across the black. And then he turned up his suit's heater, I think, too, as high as it would go; Ness said afterward that you could see it, the frost on the metal around his boots boiling away into steam as he walked.

And he began to sing.

It was not the Count. It was a Lamplighter hymn, one I had heard old Geller sing on the ninth level of Hold Fourteen, on the chapel steps, when the lamps were lit: something about the Founders coming home, and the Light in their hands, and every window burning. He sang it on the open channel, on every channel, so loud that it filled my helmet; and his voice was not good, it was thin and hoarse and cracked on the high notes, and it was the most beautiful thing I have ever heard.

The dogs turned.

All six of them. You could see it ripple round the half-circle: legs unfolding, lifting, reaching, setting down; the tubes on their stalks swinging, all of them, away from the door, away from us, toward the blazing singing star walking out across the Halo on the left. Their eyes were opening. Red, in clusters. One, and then another.

"Go," said the Reeve.

We went. Across the last hundred meters, between the dogs, behind their backs, fast, not running—Ness had my arm, and was pulling, and saying *don't run, don't run, walk fast, walk*—with the singing in my helmet and the red lines starting to reach out across the metal to our left, one, and then three, and then all of them, toward the singing, toward the star; and the singing did not stop. It did not stop when the first line touched him. It did not stop when the second did. It went on, thin and cracked and beautiful, *every window burning*, and I did not look, I looked at Ness's back and my boots and the metal, and the door was in front of us; and then the singing stopped.

There was a long silence on the channel. Only the knock, through my boots, in the Spindle's shadow, louder than it had ever been. *Knock-knock knock-knock.*

"Tally," said Ness.

I turned round.

She was standing behind me at the door's mouth with her hand on her side, just under her ribs, on the left. There was a little white plume coming out between her fingers. Very thin. Boiling away. One of the lines had found her, at the end, as she pulled me across—not the whole line, only the edge of it, a graze. Her face behind her visor was very white, and very calm, and she was looking at me.

"It's small," she said. "It's a small one. I've got a patch." She was already getting it out of her thigh pocket with her other hand, a grey square of the stuff walkers carry, and slapping it over the place between her fingers, and pressing, and the plume stopped. "See? Small." She swayed. "I'm fine, Tally. Don't look at me like that. I've had worse than this."

"Open it."

We both turned.

The Reeve of Brine was standing at the door. There were four of us left at the door's mouth, out of the eleven who had crossed: the Reeve, and Ness, and me, and a soldier, a young one, whose name I never learned. The rest were behind us on the open metal, or had stopped to go back to the sledges, or had not made the last hundred meters. I did not know which. I did not look.

The Reeve had a gun in his good hand. He was holding it quite steadily, for a man with the rot in his chest and a hole in his other arm and a seven-day walk behind him. It was pointed at Ness.

"I heard you on the command channel," I said. "The first night. You and Marrow."

"I thought you might have." He did not lower the gun. His voice was tired. "It doesn't change anything, Tally. It's the same as the lung. I'm sorry for it. But it's the same." He nodded at the door, without looking away from Ness. "Cinder gave you something. Before he went. I saw. Open it."

I looked at the door.

It was round, like the lock at the end of the Fourteen corridor. Steel, old, with a wheel on it. Above it, on the Spindle's black flank, there was a sign cut into the metal, a sign I had seen once before, over an iron door in an alley on Brine's Third tier: a hook. Plain, like you would hang a pot from. Or a line. And beside the wheel, a little brass plate with a slot in it.

I took out Brother Cinder's key and put it in the slot, and turned it.

Something inside the door, very deep, moved. I felt it through my gloves, through the key. A long slow grinding, like the inner door of the Fourteen lock sliding home, but backward; the dogs coming out of their seats, all round the rim. And then the wheel was free under my hand. I turned it. Through my boots, through the Halo, through the Spindle's foot, the knock was so loud now it was like a hand beating on my chest.

The door swung inward, into the dark.

"Up," said the Reeve of Brine. "All the way up."
