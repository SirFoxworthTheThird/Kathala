# Chapter 5: The Price of a Name

*The Concord · 336 AL*

"No," said Archkeeper Sallow.

He said it gently. He was sitting in the high-backed chair by the window of his study, at the foot of the Spindle, in the Keepers' chapter-house, with a rug over his knees and his stick across the rug and a cup of something steaming on the sill beside him, and he had listened to the whole of what Idris had come to say without interrupting once, his long sorrowful face turned toward the window and the Eye beyond it. When Idris had finished, he had let the silence go on for a while. Then he had said no, gently, the way you would say it to a child who has asked whether a dead dog might come back.

"She could take the habit," Idris said. "As a novice. A novice is essential service. It's in the Levy Statute—"

"I know what is in the Levy Statute. I helped the Prelate's clerks draft the last revision." Sallow turned from the window. "Essential service, Idris, is a privilege the Concord extends to the Keepers because the Concord needs the Eye kept, and the Eye cannot be kept by children dragged out of the Square with a red cord on their wrists and shoved into a habit to save their skins. If I let one Keeper's sister take the habit with a cord on her wrist, I will have forty Keepers' cousins at my door by nightfall, and the Square will know by morning, and the Square will say what it has always said under its breath and has never quite dared to say aloud: that the Light's servants do not pay the Light's debt. And then we will have riots in the Sump, and the Wardens' Guard will put them down, and people will die." He lifted his cup and did not drink from it. "Two thousand go every year. Every year, Idris. Some of them are someone's sister. Most of them, I'd imagine."

"She's my sister."

"I know." Sallow set the cup down. "And I am sorry. Believe that I am."

Idris stood in the middle of the study and did not know what to do with his hands. The room was lined with books, real ones, paper, the Keepers' library of three centuries—transit logs and phase tables and commentaries on the Rule, the old maintenance manuals in their cracked leather—and they went up to the ceiling on every side, and the window was the only gap in them, and the Eye filled the window, breathing.

"You could ask the Prelate," he said.

"I could. And she would say no, more pleasantly than I have. And she would remember that I had asked." Sallow looked at him under his heavy lids. "This is not the year to be remembered by the Prelate, Idris. Nor by the Factor."

"Why not this year?"

Sallow did not answer at once. He looked out at the Eye.

"The Factor's people have been asking questions," he said at last. "This month and last. About the Eye's tolerances. How much mass she'll take in a single transit. How many ships abreast. How quickly the governor recovers after a heavy load." He was quiet. "Engineering questions. Polite ones. Through the proper channels, to me, in writing, with the House's seal on them. I've answered them. Of course I've answered them. The Houses hold the Debt, and the Concord serves the Houses, and the Keepers serve the Light, which is to say all of them." He turned his cup on the sill, a quarter turn, the way a Keeper turns a dial. "But it's an odd thing, to be asked how many ships abreast. Nobody's ever needed to know that before."

"What does it mean?"

"I don't know what it means. That's what I'm telling you. When I don't know what a thing means, I keep my head down until I do, and I don't give anyone a reason to look at me, and I advise my Keepers to do the same." He reached out and touched Idris's hand, as he had on the gallery, dry and light. "There are things we don't touch, my boy. You know the first verse of the Rule."

"The Knot is the Founders', and shall not be turned."

"Just so. There are a great many Knots in the world. Most of them aren't made of steel. The Levy is one." He took his hand back. "Go home. Be with her. You have forty-eight days. Don't spend them in the Hub chasing clerks."

Idris bowed, as a Keeper bows to the Archkeeper, from the waist with his hand on his line. Then he went out, and down the long stair of the chapter-house, and out through the Keepers' Spoke into the Hub, and went directly to the Levy Office to chase clerks.

* * *

It took him four days to find the right one.

The Levy Office occupied six levels of the Hub's eastern quarter, a warren of counters and corridors and waiting halls with long benches where people sat with numbered slips in their hands, sometimes for days. Idris went from counter to counter in his grey habit. He filled out a hardship petition, and a dependent's petition, and an essential-service query, which a young clerk with an elaborate hairstyle took from him with an expression of such delicate pity that he wanted to put his fist through the counter's glass. He waited on the benches. He listened.

People talked, on the benches. Not loudly. Not to him, at first; the habit made them careful. But on the third day an old man sitting beside him with a slip in his hand and a red cord on his wrist—a man of forty, he must have been, the last year of eligibility, though he looked sixty—leaned over and said, without looking at him, "You're asking at the wrong windows, Keeper."

"Which are the right ones?"

"There's no right windows." The man looked at his slip. "There's a desk. Fourth level. Reconciliations. You go there and ask for Mister Oake, and you don't ask about anything at all, you just say you've come about a reconciliation, and he'll know." He turned the slip over in his fingers. "If you've got the money."

"How much money?"

The man laughed. It was not a happy sound. "More than me," he said, and a Guard at the end of the hall called a number, and the man got up and went.

The Reconciliations desk was at the end of a corridor on the fourth level that did not look as though it led anywhere, behind a frosted door with nothing on it but a number, and behind the door was a small warm room with a desk and two chairs and a window onto a light-well, and behind the desk was Ferrant Oake.

He was a large soft man of perhaps fifty, in the brown coat of a senior clerk of the Concord, with a round pink face and very small pale eyes and hair that had been arranged with great care across a skull it no longer covered. There was a bowl of candied kelp on his desk, the expensive kind from the Brine vats, dusted with sugar, and as Idris came in he was eating one, delicately, with two fingers, and he finished it before he looked up.

"Keeper," he said. "Sit, sit. You've come about a reconciliation."

Idris sat.

"I'll tell you how it works," said Oake. He had a light, pleasant, wheezing voice, like a small bellows. "So there's no confusion. People come in here confused, and it makes everything harder, and I don't like things to be hard. The Levy is two thousand. Not one thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine. Two thousand. The House counts them as they go through the Eye, and if there's one short, the Concord answers for it, and then the Office answers to the Concord, and then I answer to the Office, and I don't like answering. So a name can't come out of the Levy. Never. Not for love or money." He took another piece of kelp. "But a name can be *reconciled*. Do you see? There's an error. A disk was miscast, or misread, or two disks stuck together in the Drum, it happens, it's a very old Drum. The wrong name was called. The Office corrects the error. The right name is substituted." He ate the kelp. "And the wrong name goes home."

"The right name," said Idris.

"There's a list. The reserve. Don't concern yourself with it."

"Who's on it?"

Oake looked at him with his small pale eyes. "Keeper," he said, gently, "you came in here to buy your sister's way out of the Levy. Did you think it came out of nowhere? Did you think the House would take nineteen ninety-nine and send a nice letter?" He shook his head. "Don't concern yourself with the list. That's my advice. That's free."

Idris said nothing.

"Normally," said Oake, "the fee is forty thousand."

It was more than Idris earned in eight years. It was more than his father's pension and his mother's and the rooms on the forty-first level and every book he owned and the line round his neck, all together, sold.

"I don't have that," he said.

"No," Oake agreed. "Keepers don't. It's a vocation, not a living. I've always admired that." He brushed sugar off his fingers onto a cloth. "But a Keeper has other things than money. A Keeper has a rota. You're on the Transit Register, Keeper Vey, aren't you? The Third Spoke's register desk. Two days in seven."

Idris went cold.

"How do you know that?"

"I know a great many things. It's the only way to get anything done." Oake folded the cloth. "A ship is coming through the Eye in eleven days. Inbound. Her name's the *Clemency*. She's a private vessel, chartered out of Ostrava, and she'll be coming through on your shift, at the fourth bell of second watch, on the Third Spoke's lane. And when she comes through, the governor will tell you her mass, and you'll write it in the Register, as you write every mass." He smiled. "And you'll write forty thousand tonnes. Ore barge, empty, returning. Forty thousand tonnes. That's all."

"What will the governor tell me?"

"I've no idea," said Oake. "I'm not a Keeper. I'm a clerk. I'm only telling you what you'll write."

The Transit Register was the oldest book in the Concord. Older than the Book of the Lighting. The first entries in it were in a hand nobody could read any more, in the first year of the Light, when the Founders were still alive, and every ship that had come through the Eye since was in it, its name and its lane and its mass to the tonne, written by a Keeper and initialed and sealed, in three hundred and thirty-six years of unbroken entries in a library of volumes that filled a hall under the Spindle. A Keeper could be stripped of his line for a blot. For a false entry—there had never been a false entry. Not that anyone knew of. It was not a thing anybody had thought to make a rule against, because it was not a thing anybody had thought could happen. It would be like falsifying the tide.

"It's a number in a book," said Oake gently, watching his face. "That's all. It's a number in a book, Keeper, and your sister comes home."

Idris thought of Lark on the window ledge, with her forehead against the glass. *Everybody's brother says that.*

"Yes," he said.

* * *

He had not meant to follow her.

That was what he told himself afterward, and it was nearly true. He had come home from the Hub that evening sick and light-headed, as though he had been fasting, and found the rooms dark and Lark's coat gone from its hook; and he had stood in the cook-niche for a while not doing anything; and then he had gone out again and down the stairs and across the Third Spoke's main gallery, toward the lifts, with no idea where he was going. And at the lifts he had seen her, a long way ahead, the ragged dark head with the red lock in it, going not into the lifts but past them, to the old service stair at the gallery's end, the one with the rusted gate that nobody used, that went down.

He followed her down.

The service stair went down a long way. Past the middle wards, past the lower wards, past the water levels where the air was thick and warm and smelled of algae, through a door with a broken lock and a sign on it that said NO ACCESS—SUMP in Concord letters, and below the sign in older letters, half scraped off, something else he could not read. Then the stair ended, and he was in the Sump.

He had been born in Tithe and lived all his life in it and he had never been to the Sump. Nobody he knew had. You knew it was there the way you knew your own bones were there: underneath, holding everything up, not to be looked at. The Sump was the old platform. Everybody knew that, too. The first thing the Founders had built, before the city, before the Spokes, when Tithe was a single station moored to the Ring—its hab rings and its spine and its docks were still there, under everything, the whole city grown over them and round them the way a tree grows round a nail, and the poor lived in them, and the bond-families, and the families of Levy-dodgers who had lost their ward-numbers, and anyone else the city did not have room for higher up.

The first thing he noticed was the floor. It curved. Not much, but you felt it, after the flat decks of the Spokes: you were walking along the inside of a great wheel, and the floor ahead rose gently and went up out of sight round the curve, and the ceiling came down to meet it. The ceilings were low. The corridors were narrow. The walls were grey, under paint—under many paints, under three centuries of paint, flaking, so that in places you could see layer under layer of color, ochre and green and white and blue, like the strata in a cliff—and in some places the paint had flaked right down to the bottom, and there on the grey were letters, old letters, stenciled in a yellow gone brown. *B-9*. *B-11*. *SPINE TRANSFER*. *NO HELMETS PAST THIS POINT*.

It was crowded. It was warm. Every corridor had people in it, sitting in doorways, cooking on little plates, washing clothes in buckets, arguing, sleeping. There were stalls selling fried krill and kelp cakes and something grey in bowls, and stalls selling salvage, wire and bolts and old circuit boards, and one stall selling nothing but buttons, thousands of them, in heaps, of every color. Lamps hung from hooks in the ceiling, little oil lamps and salvaged ship's lamps and in one corridor a string of tiny lights that flickered on and off in sequence, red and green and gold, for no reason that he could see except that somebody had thought it would be beautiful. People looked at him as he passed. At the habit. Some of them stopped talking. One woman spat, not at him, just near him, on the curving floor.

He lost Lark at a junction where four corridors met under a dead light fixture. He stood there turning round, looking down each of them, his habit drawing eyes. Then he heard the singing.

It came from the left-hand corridor, round the curve. Children's voices, and a rope: a slap, and a slap, and a slap, steady, on the deck. He went toward it because he did not know where else to go.

There were six of them in the corridor beyond the curve, small, ragged, the eldest perhaps ten. Two at either end of a long rope made of knotted wire, turning it, and one in the middle, skipping, a little girl with her hair in a hundred tiny braids that flew up and came down every time she jumped, and three more lined up against the wall waiting their turn, clapping. They were singing. They did not see him. He stood at the bend of the corridor and listened.

*One for the hook and two for the line,*
*three for the dog and four for the time,*
*five for the air and six for the seal,*
*seven the hand on the cold steel wheel,*
*eight for the dark and nine for the drop,*
*ten for the Comp'ny, ten and*—STOP!

The rope stopped dead on the deck, and the little girl stopped dead on top of it, with both feet, exactly on the beat, and the others shrieked. Then the rope went round again, and she skipped again, and they sang on, faster, a new verse he did not know, the rope slapping, the braids flying.

*Sun on your left and the kettle below,*
*dog in the box where the old men go,*
*twice to the dark and once to the light,*
*seat it and pin it and kiss it goodnight!*

On *goodnight* the girl jumped out of the rope sideways and the next child jumped in, without breaking the beat, and they all shouted the last lines together, very loud, delighted, the way children shout the part of a song they know best:

*That is the way that you lock the door—*
*count it back down and you're HOME once more!*

The rope came down. The child in the middle crouched, and the rope-turners swung it low, and the child hopped it backward, and they all chanted together, slower, in a kind of sing-song countdown as she hopped—*ten, nine, eight, seven*—back down to one, and on *one* she jumped clear and everyone cheered.

Idris stood in the corridor with his hand on the cold grey wall.

He had said the first two lines of that every morning of his life. He had said them with his palm on the brass, in the gallery, with the Eye breathing below him. He had been told that they were the oldest words in the Concord, carried down from the hands that had raised the Ring, and that a Keeper did not need to understand them to say them truly. And here they were, in the Sump, being skipped to by children, with a whole song wrapped round them like a body round a heart, a song nobody had ever taught him.

"What are you doing here?"

He turned. Lark was standing behind him at the bend, with her arms folded, and her face was so white with anger that the red lock in her hair looked like a wound.

"I followed you," he said. There did not seem much point in saying anything else.

"I can see that. I asked what you're doing here."

"I don't know."

"You don't *know*." She came up to him. She came right up, until her face was a hand's width from his, and he saw that she was shaking. "You don't come here. Nobody like you comes here. Do you know what they'll think? A Keeper, in the Sump, in Levy season, following people? Do you know what they'll think you are?" Her voice dropped. "Do you know what they'll *do*?"

The children had stopped skipping. They were standing in a row along the corridor wall, staring at him, the little girl with the braids at the front with her thumb in her mouth.

"I'm sorry," Idris said.

Lark looked at him for a long moment. Then something went out of her shoulders, and she shut her eyes, and opened them.

"Well, you're here," she said. "Somebody's seen you. Half the Sump will know by now. If I send you back up on your own you'll get a knife in the back on the stair." She turned. "Come on. You'd better meet Rook."

* * *

Mother Rook lived at the end of a short dead-end corridor off a bigger one, in a room that had once been something else—a store, maybe, or a pump room; there were pipes running across its ceiling, painted the same grey as everything, and a big valve-wheel on one wall, rusted solid—and was now so full of things that there was barely space for the three of them to sit. There were shelves on every wall, made of salvaged plating, and on the shelves there was everything: jars of buttons, coils of wire, old slates with cracked faces, a ship's compass, a box of keys, a stuffed bird with one glass eye, a child's shoe, a tangle of red cords—Levy cords, Idris realized, dozens of them, cut through—a lamp, a bell, a model ship carved out of packing foam. There was a narrow cot, and a hot plate, and a stool, and a chair; and in the chair, small as a child, wrapped in shawls, Mother Rook.

She was the oldest person Idris had ever seen. She might have been eighty. She might have been a hundred. Her face was folded in on itself like a dried fruit, and her eyes were nearly lost in the folds, but they were black and bright and missed nothing. Her hands, on the arms of the chair, were all knuckle. She watched him come in and duck under the pipes and sit, at Lark's nod, on the stool; and she did not say anything at all until he was sitting. Then she said, in a voice like a hinge that wanted oil:

"So this is the brother."

"This is the brother," said Lark.

"The Keeper."

"Yes."

Mother Rook looked at him for a long time. At his habit, at the white hook on his breast, at the line round his neck.

"Pretty," she said at last. "Your rope. Real cord, is it?"

"It's my father's."

"Is it now." She did not explain what she meant by that. "You'll have tea."

It was not a question. Lark got up and went to the hot plate. There was a kettle on it already, and she took it off and reached up to a shelf over the hot plate and took down a flask, and unscrewed it, and poured something dark into it from the kettle, and screwed it shut again, and shook it, and then unscrewed the cup off its top and poured from the flask into the cup, and handed it to Idris. All of it quick and practiced, as though she had done it a hundred times. He understood, watching her, that she had.

"Drink," said Mother Rook.

He drank. It was hot and black and very bitter, and it was not tea. It tasted of something burnt, and of earth, and of metal.

"Chicory," said Rook, watching his face. "Grown in the vats down on Nine. Sump drinks chicory. Always has. Don't know why. Tea's for the Spokes."

"It's—" He did not know what it was. "Thank you."

"It's foul," said Lark, sitting down on the cot. "You can say it's foul. Everybody does, the first time."

He drank some more. It was foul. It was also, after a while, not, in some way he could not have explained: it was warm and it was strong and it tasted of a place. He held the cup in both hands. The flask Lark had poured it from was standing on the edge of the hot plate shelf, and he looked at it because it was in front of him, and because it was not like anything in the Spokes. It was steel, old, heavy, dull grey, worn smooth and bright at the shoulders where hands had held it, with a dent in one side.

"Your sister says you heard the children," said Mother Rook.

"Yes."

"And you knew the words."

"The first two lines," Idris said. "We say them. The Keepers. In the morning, at the rite. It's the invocation."

"I know what you say." Rook's black eyes did not move from his face. "Everybody knows what you say. You think the Sump doesn't hear what goes on in the gallery? Half your novices come from down here, Keeper, they just don't tell you." She lifted one knuckled hand an inch off the chair arm and set it down again. "You say two lines. We say all of it."

"Where does it come from?"

"From before."

"Before what?"

"Before me. Before my mother. Before hers." The old woman's face did not change. "It's old, Keeper. It's older than the Sump. It's older than anybody's name. That's all anybody knows, and anybody who tells you more is selling something." She paused. "My gran used to say it was a working song. A song you'd sing to keep the time, doing a job of work. But she didn't know what job. Nobody does."

"*Lock the door*," Idris said. "What door?"

Rook shrugged, a tiny movement under the shawls. "What door is ever locked against the Sump?" she said. "Pick one."

He did not have an answer to that. He drank the chicory. It was nearly gone. He tipped the cup to get the last of it, and the bottom of the cup came up, and he looked at it without meaning to; and then he looked past it, at the flask on the shelf, and leaned forward, and turned the flask a little with one finger so that its base caught the light from the lamp.

There were letters stamped in the steel of the base. Old letters, worn nearly flat, in a square plain face like the stencils on the corridor walls. He had to tilt it twice before he could make them out.

"What's this?" he said.

"Lucky," said Mother Rook.

"I mean the letters. What do they mean?"

"Nobody knows that either." Rook watched him turn the flask in the lamplight. "It's been passed hand to hand down here longer than anybody can say. Never stays with one family. It goes where it's wanted. When somebody's in trouble, it goes to them for a while, and when the trouble's done they pass it on. That's the custom." Her black eyes went to Lark, and stayed there. "I've had it a while now. Longer than I ought. I'm waiting to see who wants it next."

Lark looked at the floor.

Idris set the flask back on the shelf, carefully, the way he would have set down an instrument in the gallery. He did not know why he did it so carefully. It was only an old flask, dented, with numbers on the bottom. His hand did not want to let go of it.

"More?" said Mother Rook.

He held out his cup, and Lark took the flask down, and poured.
