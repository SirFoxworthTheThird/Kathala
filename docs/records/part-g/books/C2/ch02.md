# Chapter 2: The Lottery

*The Concord · 336 AL*

Every morning of his working life Idris Vey had begun in the same way, with his palm flat on the cold brass rail of the Spindle's outer gallery and the words of the Keepers' invocation in his mouth.

"One for the hook," he said, "and two for the line. Three for the dog and four for the time."

It was not a long prayer. Novices joked that it was the shortest in the Concord's whole liturgy and therefore the most efficient, which was the kind of joke Keepers made, being engineers first and priests a long way second whatever the Lightwardens liked to pretend. Nobody knew what it meant. The hook was the Keepers' sigil, worked in white thread on the breast of every grey habit in the order and cast in steel above the door of every Keeper chapel in the Reach; the line was the braided cord Idris wore round his neck and crossed over his chest, the stole of his office. The dog and the time were mysteries. Archkeeper Sallow said they were the oldest words in the Concord, older than the Book of the Lighting, older than the Founders' Roll, carried down from the hands that had raised the Ring, and that a Keeper did not need to understand a prayer to say it truly, any more than he needed to understand Kettle to orbit it.

Idris said it, and took his hand off the rail, and looked down.

From the outer gallery of the Spindle you could see the Eye. That was what the gallery was for; it had been built, three hundred years ago, so that the Keepers could stand where they stood now and look into the thing they tended. The Spindle rose nine hundred meters off the rim of the Ring like the gnomon of a sundial, and the gallery was a band of glass three-quarters of the way up, and below it and beyond it the Ring curved away on either hand, thirty-eight kilometers of dark metal crusted with three centuries of city—Tithe, nine million souls, spokes and wards and towers and docks grown along the rim like coral on a reef—until the two arms of the curve met again on the far side, small with distance, a bracelet of lights.

And inside the bracelet, the Eye.

People who had never seen it asked what it looked like, and people who had seen it all their lives could never tell them. Idris had tried, once, writing to a cousin on Brine. *Like a well*, he had written, *if you looked down a well and saw the sky at the bottom of it, a different sky, and the water were made of light.* That was not right either. It was a disk of light filling the Ring from rim to rim, blue-white at the edges and deepening toward the center into a color there was no word for, a color that was not quite violet and not quite black, and it moved: not as water moves, nor as fire, but as a held breath moves in a chest, a slow swell and settling that the Keepers called the Eye's respiration and measured to the ten-thousandth of a cycle. Through it, if you looked long enough, you could see other stars. Not Kettle's stars. The stars of the Inward, a thousand light-years off: the sky over Ostrava, where the Eye's sister burned in her own ring and the Houses kept their courts.

Ships went through it. That was the point of it, and you forgot that, looking. At this hour of the morning there was an ore-train going outward, nine linked barges from the Gall mines riding down the approach lane on their tug's plume, small and black against the light; and coming the other way, just surfacing from the Eye's center like a fish rising, a House courier, white and slender, its running lights winking gold. Idris watched it rise. He could feel it on the governor before he saw it on the gauges: a tiny change in the Spindle's voice, a deepening, the whole tower taking the mass of the courier the way a bridge takes the weight of a cart. Then the courier was clear and the Eye settled, and the tower's voice went back to its morning note.

He checked the gauges anyway. The gallery's inner wall was a long bank of them, brass-cased and glass-faced, polished every morning by novices who were not allowed to touch anything else: the phase meters, the respiration count, the mass-balance dials that told the Keepers how much the Eye had swallowed and spat out since the last bell. He read them all in order, as he had been taught, and wrote them in the day-book in his small square hand, and initialed the page. *Phase nominal. Respiration nominal. Mass-balance within limits. Light holds.* Then he turned, as every Keeper turned at the end of the morning rite, to face the grille.

It was set into the inner wall at the end of the gallery, where the gallery curved inward to meet the Inner Housing: a panel of brass lattice a little taller than a man, green at its edges, bright as gold in the middle where three centuries of Keepers' hands had touched it. Behind it, mounted in the steel face of the Housing, was the Knot.

It was not much to look at. Idris had thought that the first time he saw it, at fifteen, a novice in a habit too long for him; and he had thought it with a small private shock, because he had been expecting something like the Eye, something that would make his heart turn over. The Knot was a lump of old steel. The head of something, a cylinder as thick as a man's thigh, standing a hand's width out from the Housing's face and ending in a squared boss with a deep socket in it, the kind of socket a heavy wrench would fit. Its surface was pitted and dark. There were marks on it, small fine scratches, too regular to be accidental and too worn now to read. Beside it in the Housing face was a round plate the size of a cart wheel, held by a ring of ten bolts, painted over and over in the Keepers' grey until the bolt heads were soft lumps under the paint, and sealed across each bolt with a disk of lead stamped with the hook.

*The Knot is the Founders',* said the Rule of the Keepers, chapter one, verse one, in letters cut into the lintel above the grille, *and shall not be turned.*

Nobody knew what it did. Nobody had ever asked, in Idris's hearing, except himself at fifteen, and he had asked the Keeper who was training him, and the Keeper had looked at him with such quiet horror that he had never asked anybody again.

He put his palm flat on the grille where the brass was brightest, and bowed his head, and said the invocation once more, and took his hand away.

"You're early."

Idris turned. Archkeeper Benedek Sallow was coming down the gallery from the lift, slowly, as he did everything now. He was seventy-one and had been a Keeper since before Idris was born, and his habit hung on him like a sail on a windless day, and his line was the old kind, real braided tether cord, grey-white and furred with age, that he said had been his own master's and his master's before that. He walked with a stick of black Brine-ice wood. His face was long and pouched and sorrowful, like an old dog's, and he was the cleverest man Idris knew.

"Levy Day," Idris said. "I wanted to finish before the Square."

"Ah." Sallow came to the rail beside him and looked down at the Eye. "Yes. The girl's eligible this year?"

"She was eligible last year."

"So she was. And not drawn." Sallow nodded slowly. "The Light provides."

Idris said nothing. Sallow glanced at him sideways from under his heavy lids.

"Two thousand from nine million and the moons," the old man said. "Your sister's chances are better than the courier's of coming out of the Eye in pieces. And that has not happened in three hundred and thirty-six years."

"I know the figures."

"I know you know them. I am telling you that I know them too, so you needn't go on carrying them alone." He put his hand on Idris's shoulder, a dry light hand, all bones. "Go to the Square. Stand with her. Come back to me tomorrow and we'll laugh about how pale you looked."

* * *

Basilica Square lay on the old Founders' deck, at the heart of the Hub, where the first platform had been moored to the Ring before there was a city to moor it to. It was not, strictly speaking, a square. It was a vast oval floor of white stone—real stone, quarried on Gall and shipped up at a cost no one would ever name—open to a dome of glass so high that clouds formed under it in the dry season, and at its northern end the Basilica of the Light rose in tiers of white and gold toward the dome, and before the Basilica, on a plinth taller than three men, stood the Founders.

There were nine of them, cast in bronze gone black and green with three centuries of breath. Men and women in the long coats of the old Combine, standing shoulder to shoulder, their faces turned up and outward toward the glass, toward the Eye that you could see through the dome if you stood in the right place; and in the hands of the foremost of them, raised over all their heads, a lamp. Not a real lamp, not the kind anybody had used in a thousand years. A storybook lamp, a lantern, with a flame of gilt bronze. The Light.

Under them, cut deep in the plinth's face, the Founders' Roll: forty-one names, the directors and officers and engineers of the Combine who had raised the Ring and lit it. Schoolchildren in the Reach learned them by heart and recited them on Firstlight, the anniversary of the Lighting, which was the new year. Idris had learned them, and recited them, and forgotten most of them as adults did, but some stayed. There was a Castellane, whose name was first. There was a Haddad, whom the Book of the Lighting called *the Lightbringer's Hand*, near the bottom. There was a Lind. He could see them from where he stood, a long way off over the heads of the crowd, cut in letters the size of his forearm and gilded, catching the light.

And under the Roll, cut smaller, the words every child in the Reach knew before they knew their own name: *THEY LIT THE EYE, AND THE REACH WAS BORN INTO THE LIGHT. FOR THE LIGHT, WE OWE.*

The crowd filled the Square from wall to wall. Every soul in Tithe between sixteen and forty who was not a Lightwarden, a Keeper, or a House servant was required to stand in the Square or a ward chapel on Levy Day, and the Square was where the Third Spoke stood, and the Fourth, and half the Sump. They stood in their ward blocks between lines of white cord strung on brass posts, as though for a procession, with Wardens' Guard in their white-and-gold half-armor posted every twenty paces along the cords. They stood quietly. That was the thing that always struck Idris, every year, because he was not required to be there and came anyway: the quiet. Two hundred thousand people in one place, and you could hear the air plant breathing in the vents under the floor.

Lark stood beside him with her hands in her pockets.

She was seventeen and she was nearly as tall as he was now, which still surprised him every time he stood beside her; in his head she was six, small and furious in a black mourning smock that had been let down twice, refusing to hold his hand at their mother's pyre. She had their mother's long jaw and their father's black eyebrows, which met in the middle when she frowned, which was often. She had cut her hair herself the week before, with a kitchen knife, he suspected, short and ragged so that it stood up at the crown, and she had dyed one lock of it the red of a Levy cord, which in the Third Spoke on Levy Day was the kind of joke that got you looked at. People were looking at her. She was ignoring them, which was the thing Lark did best in the world.

"You don't have to stand with me," she said, without looking at him. "You're not eligible. You can go and stand with the Keepers on the steps."

"I want to stand here."

"You'll get your habit dirty."

"It's grey. It doesn't show."

She almost smiled, and didn't, and looked up at the Basilica steps instead.

The Prelate was there. Most Luminous Ottilie Marsh, head of the Concord of the Reach, Lightwarden of Lightwardens, a small round woman in white and gold vestments so heavy with thread that two acolytes walked behind her holding up their hem. She had a pleasant motherly face and a pleasant motherly voice and Idris had heard her, once, at a Keepers' council, order a man's pension stripped from his widow for a clerical error in his transit logs, in exactly that voice. She stood at the top of the steps, beside the Drum.

And a little behind her and to her left, in black, stood Factor Severs.

Idris had seen Lucan Severs perhaps a dozen times in the four years since House Ostrava had sent him through the Eye to sit in the Factor's House in the upper wards. He was a tall narrow man, younger than you expected, with a long pale face and pale hair cut close, and he wore the plain black of the Inward Houses, unornamented except for a small gold disk at his throat: the House's seal, a ring with a line through it. He did not stand like a man at a ceremony. He stood like a man watching an inventory. He had a slate in one hand, and once, while the Prelate was speaking, he looked down at it and touched something on its face, and then looked up again.

The Prelate spoke for a long time. She spoke about the Founders, and the Lighting, and the Debt. She spoke of the Founders' sacrifice, and of the great cost of raising the Ring, which the Founders had borne out of their own substance, out of love for the Reach that did not yet exist, so that it might be born into the Light; and of how the Reach, which owed its every breath to the Eye, owed also its share of that cost, the Founding Debt, which the Houses of the Inward held in trust; and of how the Levy was the Reach's honor, the means by which its children went Inward in service to that Debt, and returned—she said *returned*—enriched in skill and grace. Idris had heard it every year of his life. He watched Lark's face while the Prelate spoke. It did not move at all.

Then the acolytes turned the Drum.

The Drum was a sphere of Gall crystal two meters through, clear as water, hung in a gilt frame on a horizontal axle, and inside it were four million small brass disks, one for every soul in the Reach of Levy age, each stamped with a name and a ward-number. When it turned, the disks inside it rolled and slid over one another with a sound like surf, a long hissing roar that filled the Square, and that was the only loud sound on Levy Day, and every year Idris felt it in his teeth. They turned it nine times. Then they stopped it, and a Lightwarden reached into the opening at its base, where the disks fell one at a time through a gate the width of a single coin, and took out the first disk, and handed it to the crier.

"Ward Nine of the First Spoke," said the crier into the voice-horn. "Annora Weil."

Somewhere off to the left, in the First Spoke's block, there was a small sound, not a scream, a kind of gasp. Then a Guard was walking through the cord toward it.

It took a long time. Two thousand names. The crier read them in a level unhurried voice, and after each name there was the small sound somewhere in the Square, or sometimes no sound, and the Guard walking. Some of the drawn walked out to meet the Guard themselves, with their chins up; Idris saw one young man in a dock-loader's vest walk out and hold out his wrist for the cord before the Guard had reached him, as if to say, *there, I'm not afraid of you*, and saw his hand shaking as he held it out. Some had to be fetched. Twice there was shouting, and a scuffle, and the Guard closed in and the shouting stopped. Once, near the Sump block, someone ran: a girl, Lark's age, ducking under the cords and running for the eastern gate with her hair flying, and the whole Square seemed to lean toward her, holding its breath, and two Guards caught her twenty paces short of the gate and brought her down on the white stone, and the Square let its breath out, and the crier, who had paused, read the next name.

The red cords went on wrists. Idris watched them go on. Each Guard carried a hank of them on his belt, bright red braided silk, and he tied it round the drawn wrist in a particular knot, quick and neat, the way a Keeper ties off a line. It was not a binding. It came off with a sharp knife. But no one in the Reach would sell you food or rent you a bunk or let you onto a ship with a red cord on your wrist, and if they found you without it they would put your name back in the Drum for the rest of your life, and your family's names with it.

The Third Spoke's turn came in the second hour.

"Ward Two of the Third Spoke," said the crier. "Benedikt Aro."

A sound, on the far side of the block.

"Ward Six of the Third Spoke. Mallory Teng."

Lark had taken her hands out of her pockets. Idris did not know when she had done it. She stood with her arms loose at her sides and her chin a little raised, and looked at the Drum.

"Ward Four of the Third Spoke. Calloway Hesk."

Their ward. A man in front of them, three rows forward, went slowly to his knees, and the woman beside him put her arms round his shoulders and held on.

"Ward Four of the Third Spoke," said the crier, and Idris knew, he knew before the crier's mouth moved, the way he knew a ship's mass on the governor before the gauges showed it, a deepening, a weight arriving. "Lark Vey."

There was no sound from Lark at all.

He heard a sound, and realized after a moment it had come from him.

"Lark—"

"Don't." She did not look at him. "Don't, Idris."

The Guard was coming. A big man, older, with a grey moustache, the hank of red cord on his belt half used up. People got out of his way. He came to the cord in front of them and stopped and looked along the row until he found the girl with the red lock in her hair, and Idris watched his eyes go to it, and narrow, and something in his face go hard.

"Lark Vey," he said. "Ward Four."

"I heard."

"Your wrist."

Lark held it out over the cord. Her left wrist, thin and brown, with the little scar on the inside of it where she had cut herself on a tin at nine. Idris saw that her hand was perfectly steady, steadier than the dock-loader's, steadier than his own, and that she was looking the Guard straight in the face.

The Guard tied the cord. Quick and neat. He gave the knot a little tug to set it, and as he did he looked at the red lock of hair again, and said, low, not unkindly, "Bit early for that, girl."

"Not really," Lark said, "as it turns out," and laughed.

It was not a loud laugh. But in the silence of the Square it carried, and Idris saw heads turn. Saw, on the Basilica steps far off, a small pale face in black glance down from its slate, as though at a column of figures that did not quite balance, and then glance back.

* * *

Their rooms were on the forty-first level of the Third Spoke's middle wards, two rooms and a cook-niche with a window that looked out, not at the Eye, but at the back of the Fourth Spoke's water tower, a great rust-streaked cylinder sweating condensation, on which someone a generation ago had painted a Founder with a lamp, now so faded that only the lamp remained, a smear of gold on the rust. Idris had been born in these rooms. So had Lark. Their father had carried them up the stairs from the Keepers' clinic, both times, wrapped in his habit, their mother walking behind.

Lark sat on the window ledge with her knees drawn up and the red cord on her wrist and looked at the gold smear on the tower. She had not spoken on the long walk back from the Square, through the corridors full of quiet people, through the lift where an old woman had looked at the cord on Lark's wrist and then at Idris's habit and then very carefully at nothing. She had not spoken while he made tea, nor when he put it beside her on the ledge. It was going cold there now.

"I'm going to fix it," he said.

She didn't answer.

"I mean it. There are ways. You know there are ways. The Levy Office takes petitions for hardship, for dependents—"

"I'm not a dependent. I'm seventeen."

"For essential service, then. The Keepers can claim essential service. If you were a novice—"

"You want me to take the habit." She looked at him at last. "To get out of the Levy. You want me to take vows to the Light so I don't have to go and pay the Light's bill."

"I want you not to go Inward for twenty years."

"Twenty years and then I come back enriched in skill and grace." She said it in the Prelate's voice, the motherly voice, and it was so exact that in another hour he would have laughed. "Do you know anybody who came back, Idris? Anybody? You know everybody. You know all the Keepers and all the Keepers' families and half the clerks in the Hub. Name me one person who went in the Levy and came home."

He thought. He tried to think.

"Calloway Hesk's uncle," he said. "I think—"

"Calloway Hesk's uncle came back with no hands," said Lark. "He works the Fourth Spoke's water gate. He opens and closes it with his elbows. You've seen him."

He had seen him.

"That's one," she said. "In my whole life. One."

She turned back to the window. Outside, on the water tower, the gold smear of the Founder's lamp caught the light of someone's window across the shaft and gleamed for a moment and went dull.

"I'll find a way," he said. "I'll go to the Archkeeper tomorrow. And if he won't, I'll go to the Levy Office myself. There are people there who—there are ways it's done, Lark. Everybody knows there are."

"Everybody knows," Lark agreed.

"So I'll do it."

"Everybody's brother says that."

He did not know how to answer. He stood in the middle of the room in his grey habit with the line round his neck and his hands empty.

After a while she began to sing. Very low, to herself, with her forehead against the window glass. It took him a moment to realize what it was, because he had never heard it sung. He had only ever heard it said, in the gallery, in the dawn, with his hand on the brass.

*One for the hook and two for the line,*
*three for the dog and four for the time—*

He stood still and listened. She did not stop where the invocation stopped. She went on, low and quick, in the sing-song of a child's game.

*—five for the air and six for the seal,*
*seven the hand on the cold steel wheel,*
*eight for the dark and nine for the drop,*
*ten for the Comp'ny, ten and* stop.

She hit the last word hard, a little bounce on it, like a skipping rope coming down.

"Where did you learn that?"

She glanced at him over her shoulder, and for a moment she was six again, caught at something.

"What? It's a skipping song."

"That's the invocation. The first part. That's the Keepers' invocation."

"Is it?" She seemed genuinely surprised, and then not. "Well. Everybody knows it. Everybody below the Spokes. The kids skip to it in the Sump." She looked at him with an odd expression, half pity and half something he could not name. "There's more."

"More?"

"Lots more." She turned back to the glass. "You never asked."

"Where did it come from?"

"How should I know? It's a skipping song, Idris. It came from where skipping songs come from. Somebody's grandmother." She drew her knees up tighter. The red cord on her wrist was very bright against her skin, the brightest thing in the room. "You say it every morning and you don't know what it is. That's funny. Isn't that funny?"

"Lark—"

"I'm tired," she said. "I want to go to bed. You go to the Archkeeper in the morning. You go and fix it."

But she did not go to bed. She sat on the ledge with her forehead against the glass, and he sat at the table behind her with his cold tea, and neither of them moved, and the Third Spoke's lights went down for the night, level by level, until the only lights left were the little gold smear on the water tower and, very faint, reflected in the glass so that it seemed to be inside the room with them, the slow cold breathing of the Eye.
