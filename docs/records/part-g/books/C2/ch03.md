# Chapter 3: The Count for the Dead

*The Dark · Year 419*

We sent Pim out the lock on the ninth day of the cold month, and I sang him home.

That is the work. I am the Tally of Hold Fourteen, which is to say I am the one who remembers, and when a child dies of the rot in Hold Fourteen I am the one who stands at the inner door of the lock with my hand on the wheel and counts him down. That year I was not the Tally yet. I was nineteen and I was the Tally's girl, and the Tally was Quill, who was old and blind and whose voice had gone to a thread that winter, so that for two months now I had done the singing while he stood beside me with his hand on my shoulder and moved his lips along with mine, to keep me true.

Pim was six. His mother had wrapped him in the good cloth, the blue cloth with the gold edge that the Hold keeps for the dead, which is older than anyone and has been patched so many times that there is more patch than cloth. We are not wasteful in Hold Fourteen. The cloth comes back. Before we cycle the lock we unwrap them, at the last, and fold the cloth, and send them out in what they wore. Pim wore a jersey his mother had knitted from unpicked suit-liner, grey, with a red stripe she had got from somewhere, and his feet were bare. They were very small. I tried not to look at them, and then I made myself look at them, because Quill says that is also the work: to see what there is to see and carry it.

The lock at the end of the Fourteen corridor is the old kind, from before the Dark, a round inner door of steel as thick as my arm is long, with a wheel on it the size of a cart's. The outer door you cannot see. Between them is a little round room with a bench, where the dead wait. Pim's mother had carried him in there herself and laid him on the bench and come out again, and now she stood beside me with her hands pressed flat together and pressed against her mouth, and the Hold stood behind her down the corridor, all of us who could stand, which was eighty-one that morning. It had been eighty-two the morning before.

Through the little round port in the inner door you could see him on the bench, in his jersey with the red stripe.

Quill's hand was on my shoulder. It squeezed once. I touched the string at my throat, the Tally's girl's string, white, with no knots in it yet, the way you touch it before every Count so that it knows to listen. Then I put my hands on the wheel.

There is a right way to begin. You take a breath all the way down, into your belly, the way Quill taught me, and you let it out, and on the next breath you begin, and you do not begin loud. You begin as if you are speaking to someone just beside you. Because you are.

"Ten," I sang, "for the Company. Ten, and stop."

Behind me eighty voices came in under mine, low, a sound like the air plant when it is working well.

"Nine for the drop and eight for the dark."

I turned the wheel a quarter turn. That is also the way it is done. A quarter turn for each line, so the door seals as the Count goes down, and by the end it is shut fast and the lock is ready to cycle.

"Seven the hand on the cold steel wheel."

I had my hand on the cold steel wheel. You always do. You feel it every time.

"Six for the seal and five for the air.
Four for the time and three for the dog.
Two for the line and one for the hook."

The low numbers do not rhyme. Nobody knows why. It is the oldest part of the Count, the numbers, older than the Dark, older than the Light maybe, and Quill says that is how you know it is true: that the old true things never quite fit together, because they were made for some purpose we do not remember, and only the new false things are smooth. When I was small I used to try to make them rhyme. *Three for the dog and one for the log.* Quill hit me with his stick for that, not hard. He said a Tally keeps the Count. A Tally does not mend it.

Then the turning. The second part, which we call the Door.

"Once to the dark and twice to the light,
pin it and seat it and hold it tight,
dog to the notch where the old ones go,
Kettle beside you and sun below."

The wheel was nearly home. Through the port Pim lay on the bench with his face turned a little toward the door, as if he were listening. His mother made a sound against her hands.

"That is the way that we close the door."

The wheel went home. I felt the dogs of the inner door slide into their seats all round the rim, a deep shudder through the steel and into my hands, into my arms, into my chest.

"Count it back down," I sang, and eighty-one voices sang it with me, and Pim's mother sang it too, because you do, because it is the last thing you can do, "we are home once more."

Then I pulled the lever and the lock cycled, and there was a long sigh behind the door as the air went out of the little room, the Hold's air, the air we make and clean and breathe and breathe again; and then the outer door opened, which you cannot see but you can feel, a kind of lightness, a drop; and then through the port the bench was empty, and beyond the port, if you put your eye close to the glass, there was the Dark.

* * *

The Tallies' room in Hold Fourteen is the oldest room in the Hold. Quill says it may be the oldest room in all of Tithe, older even than the Spindle, and I believe him, because he has never told me a thing that was not true, though he has refused to tell me a great many things. It is small, four long steps by six, with a round port at one end so scratched with age on the outside that it is white as milk and you can see nothing through it at all, only a glow when Kettle is on that side. There are shelves on the long walls, three high, deep, like bunks, which is what Quill says they were once: bunks, for sleeping in, before the Light, when people came out here to build things and slept six to a room. Now they are full of what a Tally keeps. Old slates that do not light. Books, real ones, paper, that I am allowed to touch only with my hands wrapped in cloth. Bundles of string with knots in them that are older than the books and that Quill is the last person in Tithe who can read, and was teaching me to. Bones, some of them. The finger bones of Tallies, which we keep, so that the Count has something to hold onto when we are gone.

Over the door, in yellow paint gone brown, there are some old letters: C-14. That is why we are Hold Fourteen. Under them, scratched into the wall so small you have to stand on the stool to read them, there are two more words in the old letters. Quill says they say *abandon hope*. When I asked him why, he said that the people who made the room were people who had a sense of humor, which he said was a kind of courage, and which he said I did not have yet but might grow into.

And on the wall by the bottom bunk on the right, nearest the port, are the marks.

We call them the First Count. They are rows of little lines scratched into the old grey wall, hundreds of them, in long rows and blocks, very neat, the way you would scratch them if you had time and a good point and nothing else to do. And some of them have a line through, and some do not, and down at the bottom there is a second set, smaller, finer, in rows of ten. Nobody knows who made them or what they count. The Lamplighters say they are the days until the Light comes back. The hullwalkers say they are some old prisoner's sentence. The Tallies say only that they are the First Count, and that every Tally's girl's first lesson is to put her fingers on them, one by one, row by row, and count them, out loud, until she can do it without missing, and then to do it in the dark.

I did that lesson at seven. I can do it now asleep.

After the lock we went back there, Quill and I, because after a lock there are things to write, and because Quill was tired. I helped him down onto the bottom bunk, where he sleeps, under the marks. His hand found them without looking, as it always did; he could not see anything any more, not even the glow of the port, but his fingers went to the wall the way a child's hand goes to its mother's sleeve. He was very thin that winter. I could feel his shoulder-bones through his coat.

"You were good," he said. His voice was all breath now. "The Door was good. You were a little fast on the dog."

"I know."

"You know. Good. Then you know." He lay back. "Get the string."

I got the string. It is Hold Fourteen's death-string, a long cord of braided wire and fiber, very old, wound on a bobbin of black wood, and every soul that goes out the lock from Hold Fourteen has a knot in it. A knot for a child is small. A knot for a grown person is bigger, and a knot for someone who has done something the Hold should remember has a twist in it, and Quill can tell you, from feeling a knot, who it was and how they died and what year of the Dark. He held his hand out, and I put the end of the string in it, and he ran his fingers back along the knots, the last ones, last month's and the month before, eight in the cold month alone, and I watched his lips move as he named them.

"Pim," he said, and held out his other hand for me to tie it, because his fingers would not do a knot any more. I tied it. A small knot. "Nine days into the cold month. Year four hundred and nineteen of the Dark. Six years. The rot."

"The rot," I said. I said it after him so the string would hear it twice.

"How many does that make? This year?"

He knew. He was asking so that I would say it.

"Thirty-one."

"And last year?"

"Nineteen."

"And the year before?"

"Twelve."

"Nineteen to thirty-one," Quill said, "and it is only the cold month." He lay with his blind eyes open, looking at nothing. "The lungs are dying, Tally's girl. The Hold's lungs. Veska knows it. She knows it better than I do, and she won't say it, because if she says it she will have to do something about it, and there is nothing to do."

The lungs are what we call the scrubbers, the great old machines in the deep levels of the Hold that clean the air. They are from before the Dark, like the lock, like the walls, like nearly everything in Tithe that works. Nobody alive knows how to make them. We know how to mend them, a little, with parts from other scrubbers in Holds that died, and the hullwalkers bring back what parts they can find from out on the hull and in the dead districts; but the parts are running out, and the lungs are running down, and when the lungs run down the black comes up out of them, a fine black mold that floats in the air too small to see, and gets into the chest. The children get it first. Then the old. Then everybody. That is the rot.

"There are lungs on Brine," I said.

"There are lungs on Brine," Quill agreed. "And water on Brine. And the Reeve of Brine sits on all of it like a hen on an egg." He turned his head toward me, a little. "Say the line for me."

He meant the lineage. He made me say it every day that winter, and I knew why, and I said it anyway.

"I am the Tally's girl of Hold Fourteen," I said, "taught by Quill, who was taught by Sorrel, who was taught by Abel the Lame, who was taught by Hanne, who was taught by the twins Ruth and Ruth, who were taught by Matthias Long-Arm," and on, and on, all of them, thirty-four names, back through four hundred years of the Dark, every Tally of Hold Fourteen, "who was taught by the First Tally," I finished, "who came up out of the Dark when the Dark was new, and would not give his name, and taught the children to count."

"Good," said Quill. "And what did he teach?"

"The Count. The Door. The marks."

"And?"

"Never sing the Count on the Halo."

"Why?"

"Nobody knows."

"Nobody knows," Quill said, "is not the same thing as there being no reason." He was quiet for a while. I thought he had gone to sleep. Then he said, "There is another rule."

I waited.

"Not today," he said. "I'm tired today. Ask me again."

* * *

There are three stories about how the Dark came, and every child in Tithe knows all three, and believes whichever one their Hold believes.

The Lamplighters say the Light was stolen. They say that once the Halo burned, and the Eye inside it was full of light, and the light poured down into Tithe like water into a cup and made everything go: the lungs, the lifts, the great lamps in the ceilings of the dead districts that are dark now and full of frost; and that the Founders who had made it lived among us, and were kind, and kept the Light. And then the Houses—which are some kind of devils in the Lamplighters' stories, I have never understood exactly what kind—the Houses were jealous of the Light and came and stole it, and the Founders went away after it to bring it back. And one day they will. One day the Halo will burn again, and the Founders will come back through it with the Light in their hands, and everything will go again, and the rot will be gone. That is why the Lamplighters keep lamps burning in their chapels in the Holds that have let them in, little oil lamps, wasting oil, so that the Founders will see them coming home.

The hullwalkers say the Light died. Just died, the way a star does, the way a lamp does when the oil's gone. They say the Halo was a machine like the lungs, and nobody looked after it, and it ran down. The hullwalkers do not have much patience for stories.

And the Tallies sing that the Light went home.

That is all. It went home. The Count says so: *count it back down, we are home once more*. Where home was, the Count does not say, and if Quill knew, he did not tell me. I asked him once if it meant that the Light was dead, like Pim. He said, *that is one way to hear it*, which is the kind of thing Quill said when he did not want to say anything.

I did not believe any of them, that winter. I was nineteen. I believed in the rot and the lungs and the water ration and the cold, which were what there was to believe in. The Light was before. Before was a story.

Ness came in off the hull that night.

You can hear the hullwalkers come in, all over the Hold, because the hullwalkers' lock is right at the top of the Hold under the old dome, and when it cycles the whole Hold rings with it like a bell. I was in the Tallies' room with a lamp, picking out a knotted string for Quill that he wanted to show me in the morning, and I heard the lock, and I put the string down so fast that I tangled it, and I had to sit and untangle it before I could go, because you do not leave a knotted string tangled. It is disrespectful to the knots. So by the time I got up to the dome they were out of their suits already, the three of them, Ness and old Barrow and Barrow's boy, and sitting on the bench by the lock with their hair stuck to their heads with sweat, steaming in the cold, eating the hot gruel that Veska keeps for walkers coming in.

The dome of Hold Fourteen was a garden once. You can tell. There are beds of old soil in long rows, gone grey as ash, and the ribs of the dome overhead are hung with the dead stems of something that climbed, and in the very top the glass is still whole, most of it, and that night Kettle was up and filling it, so the whole dome was full of Kettle-light, purple and brown and that sick slow yellow. It made everybody look dead. Ness looked up when I came in, her face purple and yellow in it, with the burn scar along her jaw gone black, and grinned.

"Tally's girl," she said. "You sang the boy out?"

"This morning."

"I heard you. I was at the top lock, suiting up. I heard you through the whole Hold." She held out her bowl. "Want some?"

I sat by her and ate some of her gruel. It is the best gruel in the Hold, walkers' gruel; Veska puts salt in it.

Ness was twenty-four. She had been walking the hull since she was twelve, when her father, who walked before her, did not come back one day, and the Hold needed somebody to bring in parts and she was the only one the right size for the suit. She was small and wiry and had arms like knotted cord and a laugh like something breaking. She had shaved her head that winter because of lice, and it made her look like a novice in an old picture, except for the scar and the grin. She had been my friend since I was five and she was ten and she had pulled me out of a vat of algae I had fallen into while trying to catch a fish that turned out to be a sock. She had been something else to me since the summer before, which we did not speak of in front of other people, though everybody knew. Quill knew. Quill said it was good for a Tally's girl to have something in her life that did not want to be remembered.

"What did you get?" I said.

"Not much." She nodded at the sack by the lock. "Two filter housings from the Ninth Spoke. A valve. Some cable. The Ninth's nearly picked out. We'll have to go further."

"Further where?"

"Out along the Halo." Barrow said it, the old walker, not looking up from his bowl. He was the oldest walker in Hold Fourteen, fifty, which is very old for a walker, and he had lost three fingers on his left hand to the cold, and he did not talk much. "Toward the Spindle. There's districts out that way nobody's walked since before my father."

"There's a reason nobody's walked them," I said.

"There's a reason for everything," Barrow said. "The reason is there's nothing left anywhere else."

He got up and went to put his suit away. His boy went after him. Ness and I sat on the bench under the dome with Kettle filling it, and she put her shoulder against my shoulder, and I put mine against hers.

"Tally's girl," she said, after a while, low. "Can I tell you a thing."

"You can tell me anything."

"Don't write it on a string."

"I won't."

She was quiet. Then she said: "The Halo's knocking."

I did not know what she meant, so I waited.

"I was out on the rim today," Ness said. "Way out, past the Ninth, right out where the city stops and it's just the Halo. Just the bare ring, you know, the old metal, with the gold lines on it. I was walking it to look for a way in to the dead districts on the other side. And I was standing there. On the Halo itself. With my boots locked down. And I felt it."

"Felt what?"

"Knocking." She put her hand on her own leg, just above the knee, and tapped it with her fingertips. "Up through my boots. Through the metal. Like somebody hitting it a long way off with a hammer. Not hard. Just—" she tapped. *Tap tap. Tap tap.* "A rhythm."

"It's the cold," I said. "Metal ticks in the cold. You know it does. The whole Hold ticks at night."

"Not like this. Ticking's ticking. This was—" She stopped. "It was *counting*, Tally's girl. It went in runs. A run of beats and then a stop, and then another run. Over and over. Like somebody saying something."

"Barrow felt it?"

"Barrow says he's felt it for years. On and off. He says all the old walkers have. He says his father used to call it the Halo singing, and that it means bad weather coming off Kettle." She shook her head. "But he says it's getting louder. He says when he was a boy you had to stand still for an hour to feel it. And today I felt it the second I locked down."

I did not say anything. Up in the top of the dome, through the old glass, Kettle turned. A long sheet of lightning went across its face, slow, the way it does, from one band to the next, and lit the whole dome for a moment white, and then it was gone.

"You think I'm making a story," Ness said.

"No."

"I'm not a Lamplighter."

"I know you're not."

"I just want somebody to know," she said. "In case. Somebody who remembers things."

"I'll remember," I said.

And I meant to say something else, something that would make her laugh, because she was frightened, I could feel it through her shoulder, and Ness was never frightened of anything. But before I could think what it was, the Hold rang.

Not the walkers' lock. The big lock. The dock.

Hold Fourteen has a dock, down at the bottom of the Hold, a great hangar of a place from before the Dark, where in the Light ships used to come in and tie up. It is mostly dark now and full of junk, and we use it for trading, when the water-boats come from Brine twice a year with ice, and when other Holds' walkers come round the hull to trade parts. It has a lock big enough to take a ship. When the big lock cycles, the whole Hold doesn't ring like a bell. It booms like a drum. You feel it in your feet and in your teeth and in the roots of your hair.

It was not the season for the water-boats. Nobody had sent word.

We went down. Everybody went down; you could hear the whole Hold going down, doors and feet and voices in the stair-wells. Veska was at the front with her big lamp and her iron bar. When we came out onto the gallery that runs round the top of the dock, there were already forty people at the rail, looking down.

The big lock had opened. Somebody had opened it from outside, which nobody in my lifetime had done, because the outer controls had been dead since before Quill. And through the lock, with frost smoking off its hull in the Hold's warm air and its lights burning so bright that we all put up our arms against them, a ship was coming in.

I had never seen a ship up close. Only the water-boats, which are tubs, ice-haulers, squat and ugly and slow. This was not a tub. It was long and lean and scarred all over, patched and repatched like the funeral cloth, black where it had been burned and silver where it had been mended, with a nose like a knife and gun-turrets on its flanks that were not for show, because one of them turned, slowly, as it came in, and looked up at the gallery where we stood. Its engines were low and loud and filled the dock with a growl you could feel in your stomach. It came in with a kind of contempt, as though the Hold were nothing, as though the dock had been built for it alone and it had only now, after four hundred years, got round to arriving.

On its flank, under the scorch and the patches, there were letters. Old letters, the kind Quill was teaching me, from before the Dark. Faded nearly to nothing and painted over and then painted again, by someone who had wanted them kept.

*PATIENCE*, they said.

"That's the Reeve's ship," Ness said beside me, very quietly. "That's the Reeve of Brine."

Down on the dock floor, a hatch opened in the ship's flank, and a ramp came down, and men in armor came down it, a lot of men, with guns. And after them, slowly, leaning on one of them, a big man in a long coat, coughing.

He stood at the foot of the ramp and looked up at the gallery. At all of us standing there in the lamplight with our mouths open. He looked along the rail as though he were counting us. Then his eyes came to the end of the rail where I was standing, beside Ness, and went to my throat, to the white string there, and stopped.

And I understood, I do not know how, the way you understand a thing in a dream: that he had not come for the Hold, or the walkers, or Veska, or our parts or our water or our dead. He had counted us, and he had found what he was counting for.

He was looking for the Tally.
