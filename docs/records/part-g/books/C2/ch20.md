# Chapter 20: The Quench

*The Concord · 337 AL*

The Firstlight procession went up the Keepers' Spoke at the seventh bell, as it had gone up every Firstlight morning for three hundred and thirty-six years, and Idris walked in it between two men in armor.

Novices first, with lamps. Twelve of them, in white over their grey, carrying the little brass storybook lamps lit from the chapter-house altar, two by two up the long stair; Tobiah among them, with his long neck, not looking at anybody. Then the four Keepers of the Firstlight rota in their full habits, with their lines crossed over their chests: old Holloway, and Marra from the First Spoke, and Desmond Vane from the Fourth, and Idris. Then the Archkeeper, alone, in the grey and silver, slowly, leaning on his black stick with one hand and carrying in the other, upright against his shoulder like a staff, wrapped in a strip of white altar cloth so that only its squared head showed, the Founders' Wrench.

The House's men walked where they liked. Two of them behind Idris, close, in their dark faceless armor, with their long weapons across their chests. More of them on every landing of the stair, standing against the wall as the procession passed, their helmets turning to follow it. Nobody in the procession looked at them. Nobody spoke. That was the rite: the procession went up in silence, with the lamps, from the chapter-house to the gallery, nine hundred meters of stair, and only at the top did anybody speak.

Idris climbed. He had climbed it every Firstlight since he was fifteen. His legs knew it. His heart did not; his heart was going so hard that he thought the men behind him must hear it through their armor. Inside his habit, against his chest, the flask was cold and heavy and its dent fit under his arm where he pressed it.

At the top, the gallery.

It had been dressed for the feast. Garlands of white silk along the brass rails, and the lamps lit, all of them, in their chains, and the gauges polished until they shone, and the long window onto the Eye washed so clean that it seemed there was no glass in it at all. And above the gallery, set into the Spindle's curved wall where a gallery of the Light's servants had no business to be, a box of glass. New. The Factor's people had built it in the last month. Through its windows Idris could see them already in their places, looking down: the Prelate in her white and gold, small and round, with her acolytes; and beside her, in black, tall and narrow, Factor Severs, with a slate. Oake was not there. Oake was a clerk; Oake had lists to keep.

Sallow went down the gallery to the north quarter, slowly, on his stick, with the procession behind him. He stopped in front of the grille. He did not look at Idris. He lifted the Founders' Wrench off his shoulder, and unwrapped the white cloth from it, and held it up, a meter and a half of dark old steel, so that the high gallery could see it; and then he bent, very slowly, with his old joints cracking, and laid it on the gallery floor at the foot of the Housing's face, under the grille, under the Knot.

"Where it was found," he said. It was the first thing anyone had said since the chapter-house. It was the rite. "Where the Founders laid it down."

Then he straightened, and turned, and said the invocation, and the four Keepers of the rota said it after him with their hands on their lines.

"One for the hook, and two for the line. Three for the dog and four for the time."

Sallow put his hand flat on the grille, where the brass was brightest. He held it there a moment. Then he took it away, and went on down the gallery to the east quarter, leaning on his stick, without looking back; and the procession broke, and each Keeper went to his quarter, and Idris went to the north quarter's bank of gauges by the Housing, with his two men behind him, and put his hands on the brass.

The Founders' Wrench lay on the floor a pace from his feet.

* * *

The transit began at the eighth bell.

He had the order in his head. He had written it out twice: once for himself and once for Dagny Shrike, who had never got it, who had—he did not know what had happened to Dagny Shrike. The *Remittance* first. Outbound, alone, in the Third Spoke lane, at the eighth bell exactly: two thousand and some souls in her holds, the last Levy, going Inward. Then, as she cleared the far side, the Squadron's van. Nine abreast. Inbound. Entering from the Ostrava side as the *Remittance* left it; so close behind her, the order said, that her beacon and theirs would cross on the Keepers' boards within the same minute. The House did not like to waste the Eye's time. Then the second rank, and the third, and so on, forty-one hulls in six hours, the governor tuned by hand at four quarters all through it.

Through the long window, the *Remittance* was moving.

She was the largest ship he had ever seen go through his lane. A Levy hauler, built for it, three hundred meters long, blunt and grey, with her holds along her flanks in rows like the cells of a comb, and her running lights burning gold and red and white all down her length. She went down the lane toward the Eye's center, slowly, on her tugs' plumes, getting smaller; and the Eye took her. He felt it on the governor before he saw it: a deepening, a weight arriving, the whole tower taking her mass. He put his hands on the north quarter's dials and tuned it, as he had always tuned it, the tiny turns of the bezels that held the phase. The Eye swelled. Its color at the center went darker. And the *Remittance* went down into the light as though into water, her lights going pale, and paler, and then gone.

He looked at the board. Her beacon, a small gold mark, was moving down a line that ran from the Eye's face toward its center. The board counted. Twelve minutes, to the far side. He had tuned ten thousand transits; twelve minutes was the Eye's time for a ship that size.

The men behind him did not move. He could see them in the gauges' glass, reflected, dark.

The Keepers' channel was in his ear. A little brass button in a cup, on a cord, that every Keeper wore at the quarters to hear the others; and through it he could hear Holloway at the south quarter breathing heavily, and Marra at the west humming to herself the way she did when she worked, and Sallow at the east saying nothing at all.

Then, under them, faint, another voice.

It was very faint. It crackled. It was coming in on the Keepers' frequency from somewhere that should not have been able to reach it. A girl's voice.

"—third Spoke Adjunct. Third Spoke Adjunct, are you there. Idris. It's me."

He did not move. He did not let his hands move on the dials. He felt the blood go out of his face and kept his hands still.

"I don't know if you can hear me. Benno's rigged the hold panel. He says it's on your channel but he's never done it before and the hold panel's not meant for it." A crackle. "He says to say he's good with machines."

Idris looked at the board. The *Remittance*'s gold mark. Three minutes in. Nine to go.

"Idris. If you're where I think you are. If you're doing what I think you're doing." Lark's voice was very steady. He had heard it steady like that once before, holding out her wrist in Basilica Square for the cord. "Don't you dare. Not yet. Not till we're through. There's two thousand of us in here. Do you understand me? Two thousand. Not just me. Benno and his mum's neighbors and the old docker with the hook, and Hesk's sister, and half the Sump. They put everybody from Fourteen in here. And all the drawn. Two thousand." A crackle. "Let us through. And *then* lock it."

He said, very quietly, with his lips barely moving, into the brass button on its cord: "Lark."

"Oh," she said. "Oh, there you are."

"I can't hear you very well."

"I can't hear you at all. Benno says you're coming in like a ghost." Something in her voice moved. "Idris. Listen. Dagny's out here."

He stared at the board.

"Her boat. The *Patience*. She came out of Gall Quay at the eighth bell with every House picket in the sky shouting at her, and she's flying alongside us in the Eye right now. Right now. I can see her out of the hold port. She's so close I could touch her. She says if I want, she'll cut through the hold wall and take me off. Me and Benno. And as many as she can carry, which is about twelve." A crackle. "She says say the word."

He did not breathe.

"I told her no," said Lark.

The board. Five minutes in. Seven to go.

"I'm not getting off," said Lark. "Not with twelve. Not with two thousand behind me. I told you. I'm not going to be a thing somebody does something for. Not ever again." Her voice did not shake. "So I'm going Inward. With all of them. And you're going to wait till we're through. And then you're going to do it. And I'm going to teach them, Idris. Inward. Wherever we end up. All of them. Everybody I meet. I'm going to teach them the song. All of it. So that whoever's over there knows it too." A long crackle. "So it's not just in one place. So it's never just in one place."

He heard, behind her voice, very faint, through the hold panel, a sound like the sea. It took him a moment to understand that it was people. Two thousand of them, packed into the holds of the *Remittance* like the cells of a comb. And then he understood that they were singing.

*One for the hook and two for the line—*

"Benno says to tell you," said Lark, "write him down. Big letters."

*—three for the dog and four for the time—*

"I love you," said Lark. "You idiot. I always did. Do it."

The panel crackled, and the sea of voices swelled, and went out.

* * *

The board said the *Remittance* had six minutes to go.

He did not have six minutes. He knew it the way he knew a ship's mass before the gauges showed it. The Squadron's van would enter from the far side as she left it, inside the same minute; and the plate had ten bolts, painted over three hundred times, and eight lead seals on the grille, and he did not know what was behind the plate. He did not know if there was anything behind it. He did not know if the song was anything but a song.

"*The light holds*," he said, into the brass button.

There was a pause, on the Keepers' channel, the length of a breath.

Then, at the east quarter, at the far end of the gallery, Archkeeper Benedek Sallow began to scream.

It was not a man's scream. It was the governor's. Sallow had his hand on something at the east quarter's bank, something Idris could not see from where he stood, and he had opened it, and out of the east quarter's gauges came the phase alarm: a deep rising whoop, so loud it shook the lamps on their chains, that Idris had heard only once in his life, in a drill, as a novice. It went up and up. And under it Sallow's voice, high and cracked and old, on every channel at once, the gallery's horns and the Keepers' buttons and the high gallery's speakers: "*Phase run at east! Phase run at east! Clear the quarter! The governor's running at east! Clear the—*"

The men behind Idris turned. Both of them. Their faceless helmets swung toward the east quarter at the gallery's far end, where the old man in grey and silver was standing in front of his gauges with his arms spread wide, as though to hold something back, and the alarm whooping, and the lamps swinging on their chains. In the high gallery the Prelate was on her feet. Severs was on his. Somebody up there was shouting.

One of the men went. He ran, down the gallery, toward the east quarter, with his weapon up. The other stayed. He took a step after the first, and stopped, and half turned back toward Idris, his helmet swinging between the north quarter and the east, uncertain.

Idris bent and picked up the Founders' Wrench.

It was heavier than he had thought. A meter and a half of old steel, worn bright at the grip by somebody's hands three hundred years ago. He had never in his life hit anyone. He held it the way you would hold a pole, across his body, and he swung it the only way he knew how, the way a novice swings the long-handled scrub-brush in the chapter-house, in a long flat arc from the shoulder with all his weight behind it; and it hit the man's helmet from the side with a sound like a bell, and the man went down on the gallery floor and lay there.

Idris stood over him with the Wrench in his hands. He was shaking. He could not stop.

The alarm whooped. The board said five minutes.

He turned to the grille.

He had his Keeper's knife in his sleeve. He cut the lead seals he had set himself thirteen nights ago, eight of them, the new lead soft under the blade. He did not take the bolts out one by one. He did not have time. He put the Wrench's squared head through the lattice and levered, and the grille's old brass frame screamed and bent and came away from the Housing's face, and he dragged it clear and let it fall, and it rang on the gallery floor like a dropped bell, and there was the Knot, bare, in the light of the swinging lamps.

And the plate beside it. Ten bolts, under three hundred years of grey paint. In a star.

He put the Wrench's head on the first bolt. It fit. To a hair. Sallow had said so. It fit as though it had been made for it, which it had.

"One," he said, out loud, "for the hook."

He leaned on the crossbar. The bolt did not move. He leaned harder, with his whole weight, his feet braced on the gallery floor, and the paint cracked round the bolt-head in a ring, and something deep in the steel gave with a squeal that he felt in his teeth, and the bolt turned.

"Two for the line."

The one across from it. The paint cracked. The squeal.

"Three for the dog. Four for the time."

He did not know if he was doing it right. He did it the way the children did it, skipping in the corridor of the Sump, the way they did not know they were teaching anybody anything. The star. Across, and two round, and across. *Five for the air. Six for the seal.* The alarm whooped. Somebody was shouting his name from the high gallery; he did not look. *Seven the hand on the cold steel wheel.* His hands were bleeding inside his sleeves where the crossbar had taken the skin off. *Eight for the dark and nine for the drop.*

"Ten," he said, "for the Company. Ten, and stop."

The plate was loose. He put his hands on its edge and pulled, and it swung back on a hinge he had never known it had, heavy, slow, grinding, and as it swung its inside face turned toward him in the swinging lamplight, and he saw that it was covered in writing.

* * *

It was cut into the steel. Not painted, not written. Cut, in big square letters, deep, by someone with a sharp point and a lot of time, in the plain face of the old stencils on the walls of the Sump.

IF YOU ARE READING THIS YOU OPENED IT RIGHT.

He read it standing, with his bleeding hands on the plate's edge. He read it in the time it takes to read it, which was not long; and it seemed to him afterward that he had stood there for a year.

WE BUILT THIS. 412 OF US. BONDED. THE COMBINE MEANT TO BURN US AT FIRST LIGHT FOR THE INSURANCE AND WE MADE THEM STOP. THEY PUT US ON FOR LIFE INSTEAD.

THIS IS A LOCK. IT WORKS FROM THIS SIDE ONLY. COUNT IT UP TO LOCK. COUNT IT BACK DOWN TO OPEN. THE SONG IS THE KEY. WE ALL HAVE IT.

DON'T LET THEM OWN THE DOOR. LOCK IT IF YOU HAVE TO.

FIRST LIGHT IS A WAVE. GET BEHIND THE STEEL.

And under that, the song. All of it. The numbers and then the Door. *Sun on your left and the Kettle below. Dog to the notch where the old marks show.* Not *old men*; *old marks*. Not *kiss it goodnight*; *turn it tight*. And beside the Door, two small curved arrows cut into the steel, one marked DARK and one marked LIGHT, the way a Keeper would mark a valve.

And under that, a name.

M. DACE. 0417. GANG FOUR. RIGGER.

*TTH-9 · 0417.* On the base of a flask. In Mother Rook's room, at the end of a short dead-end corridor in the Sump. Against his chest, now, under his habit. *Take it back where it came from.*

AND DOV DACE 0418. AND KOSTYA LEM. AND ABRAM TEAGUE. AND BRACKEN. WHO HELD ON.

And under the names, wired to the steel with a twist of old grey cord through two small burned holes, a short fat pin.

He stood with his hands on the plate.

Four hundred and twelve of them. Bonded. *They put us on for life instead.* The Sump. The oldest decks, the old platform's hab rings, the curving floors and the stencils on the walls under three hundred years of paint, *C-14, ABANDON HOPE*. The bond-families. The marks on the wall in the old bunkroom, row on row, counting the days somebody owed; and the one at the bottom with the deep line cut under it, where Lark put her fingertip for luck. *Teodor says it's where they stopped.* Forty-one names on the Founders' Roll in Basilica Square, gilded, the size of his forearm. *Castellane. Haddad. Lind.* And not one of these. *We built this.* And in the Sump, in the dark, in a skipping song that nobody up the Spokes had ever bothered to listen to, every one of them had been saying so, for three hundred and thirty-six years.

*Don't let them own the door.*

The board said a minute.

He untwisted the pin from the plate. His fingers would not work properly; the cord was old and stiff and his hands were wet. He got it free. He put it in his mouth to hold it, the steel cold and bitter on his tongue. Then he turned round, where he stood, in front of the Knot.

Until the long gallery window was on his left. And through it, past the curve of the Ring, small and dim and red as a banked coal, Hearth.

And below him, through the gallery's floor of glass, beyond the Ring, beyond the Eye's breathing light, filling everything, Kettle.

He looked at the three short lines scribed in the Housing's face beside the Knot. He looked at the arrows on the plate. DARK. LIGHT. He put the Founders' Wrench into the Knot's socket. It went in to the shoulder, snug, as though it had come home.

On the board the *Remittance*'s gold mark touched the far side of the line and went out.

Clear.

And within the same minute, as the order had said, at the Eye's far side, nine new marks came up on the board, side by side, red. Inbound. And under his feet, before he saw them, he felt them arrive: the governor taking the weight of nine warships at once, nine abreast, a weight so great that the whole Spindle groaned, and the lamps swung on their chains, and through the long window the Eye swelled and darkened at its heart, and its breath caught, the way a man's breath catches when he lifts something too heavy for him. Something he should not have lifted. Something he is going to drop.

*Dog to the notch where the old marks show.* He leaned on the Wrench, and the Knot moved. It had not moved in three hundred and thirty-six years, and it moved, a hand's width, inward, grinding, into the Housing's face, until its head lay level with the three scribed lines, and stopped against something deep inside. A step. A shoulder of steel, in the dark, in the notch.

*Twice to the dark.* He turned the Wrench the way the arrow said. Once. The governor's voice under his feet changed. Twice. He felt the dog go down a step, into the key of the notch, and lock there.

*And once to the light.*

He turned it back. Once. The other way. And something in the Housing went *clunk*, very deep, a sound he would hear every night for the rest of his life, and the Knot sat down into the Housing's face, flush, a seated thing.

*Seat it.* It was seated. *And pin it.* He took the pin out of his mouth. There was a hole through the Knot's head, crosswise, that he had never seen under the grille, because it had never lined up with anything until now. Now it lined up with a hole in the Housing's face beside it. He put the pin through both. It went in to its head.

*And turn it tight.*

A quarter more.

He turned it.

* * *

The Eye closed.

Not at once. Haddad—*the Lightbringer's Hand*, the Book of the Lighting called her, fourth from the bottom on the Founders' Roll—Haddad would have said forty seconds, if anyone had ever asked her, and it was forty seconds. Idris stood at the window with the Wrench still in the Knot and his bleeding hands still on the crossbar and watched every one of them.

It went from the edges. That was the first thing. The blue-white at the Eye's rim, where it met the Ring's gold, brightened, and then began to move inward. Not like water. Not like fire. Like an iris. The light folding in on itself from the edges toward the center, smoothly, evenly, all the way round, the disk getting smaller, and the Ring's gold lines that had been lit for three hundred and thirty-six years going dark behind it as it went, a ring of dark following the light inward, so that for a moment the whole Ring was a black wheel with a shrinking disk of light at its heart.

And the nine ships were in it.

He saw them. He would see them for the rest of his life, too. Nine shapes, side by side, abreast, rising out of the center of the light like fish rising, long and dark and armored, with their running lights burning: the House's van, coming home to the Reach. They had just begun to surface. Their noses were through, into this side, into the light, and the rest of them was still in the throat, in the place that was neither here nor Ostrava. And the iris closed on them.

There was no sound. There was never any sound, out there. He saw the light reach the nearest of them, at the edge, and the ship's nose go on rising for a moment while the rest of it stopped; and then there were two pieces of it, the nose and the rest, and the rest was gone, folded into the closing light, and the nose went on alone, turning, very slowly, trailing a long bright cloud of something that glittered. And the next. And the next. All nine. Shorn off at the light's edge like stalks under a scythe. Their noses turning slowly in the dark over the Ring with the glittering clouds behind them, and the rest of them—thousands of men, he thought. Thousands. In armor, in their holds, in lines of four, coming down into the city—the rest of them simply not there any more. Folded into nowhere, with the light.

The light went to a point. A star, very bright, in the middle of the black wheel.

Then the star went out.

And the governor stopped breathing.

He felt it stop. Under his feet. Under his hands on the Wrench. For three hundred and thirty-six years, every second, every day, a hair one way and a hair back; the Spindle's voice, that he had heard every morning of his working life and felt in his teeth and known the way he knew his own heart. It stopped. And the Spindle's voice stopped with it. And in the silence, in the gallery, the alarm at the east quarter that Sallow had opened whooped once more, and died; and then every lamp on every chain along the whole length of the gallery went out at once, and he was standing in the dark.

* * *

Not quite the dark. There was a little light. Grey, and very dim, from somewhere. It took him a while to understand where. It was coming through the long window, from outside, from the city. From Tithe.

He went to the window and looked down.

The city was going out.

It went the way the Eye had gone, from the edges. The Spokes first: their lights going out from the tips inward, ward by ward, level by level, a long rolling tide of dark coming in along each Spoke toward the Hub, smooth and even and unhurried. He watched the Third Spoke's lights go. The forty-first level of the middle wards, where a gold smear on the side of a rust-streaked water tower had been the Founder's lamp. Gone. The Fourth Spoke's. The Ninth's. The towers. The docks. The Basilica, its tiers of white and gold—he saw it go dark from the top down, the great dome with the clouds under it, and the plinth beneath, where nine black bronze Founders held up a lamp over forty-one gilded names. The Hub. The Factor's House, its tall windows, its floor of black stone. Gone. Nine million people, and the lungs and the lifts and the pumps and the lamps they lived by, all of it drawing off the Eye through the phase-taps, for three hundred and thirty-six years; and the Eye was shut.

And as the tide of dark went over each ward, something came up in it.

Small. Gold. Scattered. One, and then another, and then hundreds, thousands, all across the dark city, in window after window after window. Little points of flame. Every household in Tithe that could afford it had bought a lamp for Firstlight, a little brass storybook lamp like the one in the Founder's hand, and filled it, and lit it at the twelfth bell; and the city had been sleeping off the feast, and the lamps were still burning.

He stood at the window and watched them. Ten thousand little flames in the dark, in the shape of a city. His father's voice, very quietly: *there's the Light.*

"Idris."

He turned. Sallow was coming down the gallery in the dark from the east quarter, slowly, leaning on his stick, with a lamp in his other hand—one of the novices' storybook lamps, its little flame swaying. His face in its light was grey and wet and very old, and he was smiling.

"You did it," said the old man.

"I did it."

"I felt it stop. At the east. I had my hand on the gauges and I felt it stop." He came up to the window and stood beside Idris and looked out at the dark city with its lamps. He did not say anything for a long time. "Holds it down," he said at last, quietly. "It held it down. All along. I wondered all my life."

"There were people on the *Remittance*," said Idris. "Lark."

"I know."

"She's through. She's on the other side. She's—" He stopped. "I let her go through. I let two thousand of them go through and then I locked it and they're on the other side and they're never coming home. And nine ships—Archkeeper, there were thousands of men on those ships. I watched it take them. I *watched*—"

"I know." Sallow put his dry light hand on Idris's wrist, where it was bleeding. "I know, my boy. I know."

Somewhere behind them, at the gallery's far end, there was shouting. Boots on the stair. The high gallery's door, banging. Lamps.

"They'll be here in a minute," said Sallow. "The Factor's people. What's left of them. They'll want whoever did it." He looked at Idris in the lamplight. "Go."

"Go where?"

"Down. The Keepers' Spoke has a service stair behind the chapter-house. The novices use it to steal cakes. Nobody else knows it's there." He began to unwind the old furred line from round his own neck. "Take off your habit. Leave it here. Go down in your shift, in the dark, and nobody will know you from anybody. Nine million people in the dark this morning. Nobody's looking at anybody." He held out his line. "And give me yours."

"Archkeeper—"

"I opened the phase alarm at east," said Sallow. "Everybody heard me. Everybody saw me. I'm the Archkeeper of the Keepers of the Light, and I've had the keys to the Knot's grille for nineteen years, and I'm seventy-one years old and I've the rot in my chest that I haven't told anybody about." He smiled. "I turned it, Idris. That's what I'll tell them. I've been wondering all my life whether it held the Light up or down, and this morning I couldn't bear not knowing any more, so I turned it. They'll believe it. They'll want to. It's a better story than a Keeper-Adjunct from the Third Spoke with a sister in the Levy." He pushed the line into Idris's hands. "Let me have this. Please. I've done nothing my whole life. Let me have this."

Idris looked at him for a long time.

Then he took off his habit. The grey habit with the white hook on its breast. He took his father's line from round his neck, real braided cord, and gave it to the old man, and Sallow put it round his own neck, over the grey and silver, and smoothed it with his hand.

"One more thing," said Sallow. "Before you go." He nodded at the plate, standing open on its hinge beside the Knot in the lamplight, with its cut letters. "I can't read it in this light. I don't want to. It's not mine. But someone wrote that for whoever came. Didn't they?"

"Yes."

"Then you should write under it," said Sallow, "for whoever comes next."

He held out the little storybook lamp.

Idris took it. He went to the plate. He held the lamp up to it and read it again, all of it, quickly, the square deep letters, *WE BUILT THIS*, *LOCK IT IF YOU HAVE TO*, *M. DACE*. Then he took the Keeper's knife out of his sleeve. It was not a scribe. It was a Keeper's knife, for cutting lead seals and trimming lamp wicks, and the steel of the plate was hard. He cut anyway. Below her names. Smaller than her letters. Not as deep. The knife's point squealed on the steel and he felt it in his teeth and did not care.

I READ YOU, M. DACE.

THE HOUSES CAME TO BOND US ALL, EVERY CHILD BORN. I LET 2,000 OF OURS GO THROUGH FIRST ON THE LEVY SHIP AND LOCKED IT ON THEIR WARSHIPS. NINE. THE CITY IS GOING DARK AS I WRITE.

MY SISTER LARK WENT THROUGH. SHE HAS THE SONG. SHE WILL TEACH IT ON THE OTHER SIDE.

I AM GOING DOWN TO TEACH IT ON THIS ONE. THE WAY BACK.

FORGIVE THE DARK.

I. VEY. KEEPER. FIRSTLIGHT 337.

And then, after a moment, under it, because Lark had asked him to, in letters as big as the knife would cut:

BENNO KASK. PILOT.

He stood back. The boots were on the gallery now, at the far end, and lights, and voices.

He took the flask out of his shift, where it had been against his chest all night and all morning, cold and heavy, with its dent; and he turned it over in the lamplight one more time and read the letters on its base. *TTH-9 · 0417.* Then he set it down on the steel lip at the foot of the Housing's face, under the seated Knot, beside the Founders' Wrench. Where it had come from. Where it had always wanted to go.

Then he swung the plate shut. He did not have time for all ten bolts. He put four in, at the four points of the star, and turned them hand-tight, and left the rest lying on the lip beside the flask.

"Go," said Sallow.

He went.

* * *

The service stair behind the chapter-house was narrow and steep and black, and he went down it in his shift with one hand on the wall and the other holding the little storybook lamp, its flame swaying, for nine hundred meters. He did not know how long it took. Somewhere far above him he thought he heard voices, and a man's voice raised, and then an old man's, high and cracked and calm, saying something he could not make out; and then nothing.

At the bottom the stair let him out into the Hub, into the dark.

It was full of people. He had not thought about that. Nine million of them, and every one of them awake now, and in the dark, and out of their rooms, in the corridors, in the galleries, in the squares, with their little Firstlight lamps in their hands. Crying. Shouting. Calling names. Standing still and staring up at the dead ceiling lights. A woman went past him carrying a child and a lamp and saying over and over *what is it, what is it, what is it*. Nobody looked at him. He was a man in a shift with a lamp. Nobody was looking at anybody.

He went down.

He did not decide to. His feet took him: the service stair with the rusted gate at the end of the Third Spoke's main gallery, the door with NO ACCESS—SUMP and the older letters under it, the curving floors and the low ceilings and the paint flaking off the old grey walls in strata. It was darker in the Sump than anywhere. The Sump had never had much light. But it had lamps: oil lamps, fish-oil lamps, salvaged ship's lamps, hanging from their hooks in the low ceilings, the way they always had; and they were still burning, because they had never drawn anything off the Eye in their lives.

At a junction where four corridors met under a dead light fixture, there were children.

Six of them. Small, ragged, the eldest perhaps ten. Sitting on the curving floor in a huddle against the wall under a hanging fish-oil lamp, with their arms round each other. He knew one of them: a little girl with her hair in a hundred tiny braids. She had her thumb in her mouth. She was looking at the dark end of the corridor, and she was shaking. A long rope of knotted wire lay on the floor beside them where they had dropped it.

He sat down on the floor across from them, with his back against the cold grey wall, and put his lamp down between them.

They looked at him.

"It's dark," said the little girl round her thumb.

"I know."

"Is it going to be dark always?"

He thought about it. About the plate. *Count it back down to open.* About Lark's voice through the hold panel, steady, saying *so it's never just in one place*. About nine ships, and their noses turning slowly in the dark over the Ring, with the glittering clouds behind them. About who would be standing at the door if it opened tomorrow, or next year, or in a hundred years; and what would come through it, from the other side, to a city that had nothing left to hold it with.

"For a long time," he said. "Yes."

The little girl took her thumb out of her mouth.

"I don't like it," she said.

"No," said Idris. "Neither do I." He looked at the rope on the floor beside them. "Do you know the skipping song? The one about the hook and the line?"

"Everybody knows that."

"I know a new way to sing it," said Idris. "A secret way. Backward. You start at ten and go down. It's harder. It doesn't rhyme as well, at the bottom. That's how you know it's the true way." He looked at them. "Do you want to learn it?"

They looked at each other. Then the little girl with the braids nodded.

"All right," said Idris. "But there are two rules. You have to promise."

"What rules?"

"You never sing it out on the Ring. Never. Not even if somebody asks you. Not even if somebody gives you something." He held up one finger. "And you never let only one person know it. If you're the only one who knows it, you teach somebody else. Before you do anything else. Before you eat." He held up the second finger. "Promise."

"Promise," said the little girl. And then the others, one by one, solemnly, round the lamp.

"Good," said Idris.

He put his back against the old grey wall, and took a breath, all the way down, into his belly, and let it out; and on the next breath he began. Not loud. As though he were speaking to someone just beside him.

"Ten," he sang, "for the Company. Ten, and stop."
