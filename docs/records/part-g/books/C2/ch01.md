# Chapter 1: Sixty-One Days

*The Combine · FY 2291*

Kostya's tether let go in the forty-first minute of second shift, and Sol Dace, who was eleven meters away with both arms inside a phase coupling, heard it go before she saw it: a small dry tick in her helmet, the sound a cheap clip makes when the spring inside it has finally had enough.

She did not look up at once. Twelve years on the Ring had taught her that a sound in a suit could be anything. Ice crystals off your own visor. Your own heart. The hardsuit's joints settling as the cold got into them. You looked up for every tick and you never got a coupling seated, and the Company docked you for that too.

Then Kostya said, quite conversationally, "Oh," and she looked up.

He was a little above her and to the left, where he had swung out on his line to guide the cassette in. The cassette was forty tonnes of gold-laced ceramic and steel the size of a shuttle hangar, and it hung in the cradle of the tug's arms a hand's width off the Ring's inner face, waiting for them to seat it. Kostya had been riding its leading edge, calling the gap. He was still close enough to touch it. He reached for it. His glove went past the edge by about the width of a finger.

"Line," Sol said. "Kostya. Line."

"It's on," he said. "It's—" and she watched him look down at his own chest, where the tether ran to the clip on his harness, and watched the tether float there in a slow loop with nothing on the end of it, the clip still closed on the hardpoint behind him, the line's eye torn clean out of the clip's throat.

He was moving away from the cassette at less than a meter a second. It did not look like falling. It looked like a man who had decided to step back and get a better view of the work.

"Teague," Sol said. "Teague, I need a line on him."

"I see him." Teague was at the far end of the cassette, the gang's tether man, sixty-three years old, and he had a coil of spare line on his hip because Teague always had a coil of spare line on his hip. She heard him breathing hard, the wet catch in it that he said was nothing. "Kostya. Arms in, son. Don't spin."

"I'm not spinning."

"You will if you flap. Arms in."

Teague threw. Sol watched the line go out, a thin pale snake uncoiling across the black, the weighted end turning over once, twice, and she watched it reach the end of its length and stop, three meters short, and come back, and Teague say a word she had never heard him say in eight years.

"Juno," Sol said. "Bring the tug."

"I've got the cassette."

"Drop it on the face. Set it down and come get him."

"If I set it down crooked it'll crack the—"

"I don't care what it cracks. Set it down."

There was a pause on the channel that was not Juno thinking. Sol knew Juno's pauses. This one was Juno's hands already moving on the board.

"Platform, this is *Patience*," Juno said on the open channel, in her calm pilot's voice, the one she used when she was most frightened. "I've got a man off the line at Segment Two-Eleven. I'm setting down cassette Two-Eleven-B on the face and breaking to retrieve. Advise."

The answer took eleven seconds, which was the time it took for the duty officer on the platform to call someone who could say no.

"*Patience*, Platform. Negative. Hold the cassette."

"Platform, say again. I've got a man off the line."

"We heard you, *Patience*." It was not the duty officer now. It was a man's voice, unhurried, with the flat clipped vowels of the Proctor school on Ostrava. Sol had heard it on the channel a hundred times and in person twice, both times at a disciplinary. Proctor-Captain Lusk. "Release of cassette Two-Eleven-B outside of a seating cycle is not authorized. Retrieval in free drift requires a certified retrieval crew. A retrieval crew is being tasked."

"How long," said Sol.

"Identify yourself."

"Dace. Gang chief, Four. How long."

"The retrieval crew is on rest cycle. They'll be tasked at shift change."

Shift change was nine hours away. A rigger's hardsuit carried six hours of air and a forty-minute reserve, and Kostya had been out for three of them already.

"Juno," Sol said, on the gang channel.

"I'm trying." Juno's voice had lost its calm. "They've got the drive. Sol, Control's got the drive. I've got the arms and I've got attitude and I've got nothing else. They've locked me out."

Kostya was twelve meters out now. Fifteen. He had his arms in, as Teague had told him. He was turning very slowly, not a spin, just the long patient roll of a body with nothing to push on, so that now they saw his visor and now the back of his helmet and now his visor again, the platform lights sliding across it gold and white.

"Chain," Sol said. "Everybody. Chain."

They did it the way they had drilled it and never once used it, because the Company's retrieval protocol said it was not to be used: Teague's spare coil and the two spares Juno kicked out of the tug's locker and their own working lines unclipped one at a time, each of them hanging on a single short lanyard while they did it, which the protocol also said was not to be done, all of it clipped end to end, Bracken anchored on the cassette cradle with his boots locked and his great arms wrapped around a strut, then Teague paying out, then Sol on the end. Five lines of thirty meters each, minus the bights for the clips. A hundred and twenty meters, if everything held. She went out along it hand over hand with her heart going so hard she could hear it in her ears over the hiss of the suit fans, and she did not look at the clips as she passed them, because she knew what they were. Lindqvist clips. Thirty marks a hundred from a factory on Halvard Four. Dov had had a Lindqvist clip.

At the end of the chain she let herself float out to the full stretch of it and held out her arm, as though that would add anything.

Kostya was sixty meters past her fingers.

"Hey, chief," he said.

"Hey."

"I can see the whole thing from here. I never saw the whole thing."

She turned her head, because he was right, and from the end of the chain she could see it too. The Ring. Thirty-eight kilometers across, and from here, out from the face, you could see the curve of it running away on both sides, a band of dark metal with the inner face laced over in phase-lines like frost on a window, if frost were made of gold, and lit along its length by the work lights of the other gangs, little stars crawling on it. And inside the Ring, filling it, filling everything, Kettle.

Kettle was a brown dwarf, which the Combine's brochures had described as *a failed star*, and which the riggers called a lot of things, none of them failed. It was the color of a bruise four days old: purple and oxblood and a deep sour yellow, banded, turning, with storms in its bands the size of worlds. Lightning ran under the cloud tops in long slow sheets you could watch with the naked eye. When Kettle flared, which it did every few weeks, the radiation alarms went in every suit on the Ring and the gangs went in behind the steel and sat in the dark and played cards until it passed. When it did not flare it simply hung there, two hundred thousand kilometers down, and filled half the sky, and you worked with it at your back, and you did not think about it, any more than you thought about the Company.

Far off past Kettle's limb, very small, the system's real star burned, a dim red dwarf the survey had named Hearth, the color of a coal someone had forgotten to bank.

"It's ugly," Kostya said. "I always thought it was ugly. It's not."

"No," Sol said.

"Chief, how much air have I got."

She could see his suit telemetry on her visor. Everyone's suit sent to the gang chief. She had looked at it the second his clip went.

"Two hours forty," she said. "And reserve."

"Retrieval crew's in nine."

"They might come sooner."

"Chief."

"Yes," she said.

He laughed. It was a good laugh, a little shaky. "That's what I thought."

* * *

They seated cassette Two-Eleven-B at the fifty-third minute of the third hour of second shift, because the cassette was still there and still hanging a hand's width off the face and the Company had not tasked anyone else to seat it, and because Sol could not think of anything to do with her hands that was not that.

They seated it the way they seated everything, which was by the Count.

A coupling on the Ring was held by ten bolts in a star pattern around a collar a meter across, and if you torqued the bolts in order round the circle the collar went on crooked and the phase-lines would not meet, and the Ring would not light, and some Company engineer would come out in a clean suit with a clean tablet and tell you so and dock you for the rework. So you torqued them across the star: one, then the bolt opposite, then the one two places round, then its opposite, and so on, so the collar came down even. Every gang on the Ring had its own way of keeping the order. Gang Four sang it.

"One," Sol said.

"For the hook," said Teague, and Bracken, and Juno over the tug channel, and she put the driver on the first bolt and felt it bite.

"Two."

"And two for the line."

It was not much of a song. Somebody had made it up on the Ostrava docks a hundred years ago or a hundred and fifty, nobody knew, and riggers had carried it out to every yard the Combine had ever built, and it had picked up verses and lost them, and in Gang Four it went like this:

*One for the hook and two for the line,*
*three for the dog and four for the time,*
*five for the air and six for the seal,*
*seven the hand on the cold steel wheel,*
*eight for the dark and nine for the drop,*
*ten for the Company — ten, and stop.*

And then the gang, all together, the bit that went between rounds while the driver was reset to the next torque value:

*Count it up and count it slow,*
*count it back down and home we go.*

They did it three times, three rounds of torque, and she called every number and every number got its answer, and on the third round when she said "Ten," Bracken, who almost never spoke, said very loud, "And damn the Company," which was the old way, the dock way, the way you were not supposed to do it on an open channel. Nobody told him not to.

Kostya had been singing with them. Sol had heard his voice the first round, faint and thin behind theirs on the gang channel, a quarter-beat late. The second round he was quieter. The third round she called *eight* and listened and did not hear him.

When the collar was down and the cassette's phase-lines met the Ring's in a hairline seam of gold that you could not have slid a fingernail into, she let go of the driver and let it float on its lanyard and looked up.

He was a long way out now. Past the reach of the work lights. She could only see him because his suit's own lamps were on, two small white points, turning, turning.

"Kostya," she said.

"Nice seat," he said. His voice was very close and very quiet in her helmet. "I heard. I couldn't keep up."

"You did fine."

"I want to say something to my mother."

"Go ahead. I'm recording."

So he talked for a while, to his mother, who lived on a terraced farm on Lemberg Station with the rest of his family, and was owed, he said, sixteen hundred marks of his wages under the remittance clause, which she was to be sure to claim, because they would try not to pay it. He told her about the Ring. He told her Kettle was not ugly. He told her about a girl on Lemberg he had meant to write to and had not, and asked his mother to tell her, and then laughed and said not to, actually, it would only make things strange. He talked about his brothers. He said he was not frightened, and then he said that he was, and then he said it was not as bad as he had thought it would be, and that the stars out here were very bright, much brighter than at home, and that he could not see the Ring's lights anymore but he could see the platform, and Kettle, and the stars.

Sol listened to all of it with her hand on the cassette and the rest of the gang silent on the channel. Juno was crying. You could always tell, with Juno, because she went very precise.

At some point Kostya said, "Chief?"

"I'm here."

"I've got about forty minutes. Of the regular."

"I know."

"I don't want to do the reserve. I don't want to do the bit where it gets thick."

"You don't have to," Sol said. She had known what he was going to ask before he asked it. Everybody on the Ring knew how, and nobody said it out loud. The suit's purge valve. The emergency vent for a fire in the suit. Two turns and a pull, and it was quick, the old riggers said, quicker than you would think. "You don't have to do anything you don't want."

"Would you stay on? Till."

"Yes."

"Would everybody—" and his voice wobbled and then came back. "No. Actually. Can you all go off the channel? Just the chief. I don't want everybody to hear."

They went off. She heard each of them go, a small click: Juno, Bracken, Teague last of all, Teague saying "Good man, Kostya," before his click. Then there was just the hiss of the carrier and Kostya's breathing.

"Chief, you've got sixty-something days, right? Left."

"Fifty-seven."

"Fifty-seven. Christ. You're nearly out."

"Nearly."

"What are you going to do?"

She had not thought about it. That was the truth. For twelve years she had not let herself think about it, because thinking about it made the days longer. She thought about it now, for him.

"There's a bar on the Ostrava docks," she said. "Pier Six, under the old cranes. They do a fish soup. Real fish, from the tanks, with the bones still in. I'm going to sit in there and have a bowl of fish soup and nobody's going to tell me when to get up."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

"That's a good plan," Kostya said. "I like that plan. Have one for me."

"I will."

"Okay," he said. "Okay. I'm going to turn it off now, chief. The radio. I'll do the rest by myself."

"All right."

"Thank you for the chain. That was a good chain."

"It was too short."

"Still," he said, and then the carrier went, and there was only silence, the special silence of a channel with nobody on it, and she looked out at the two small white points of his suit lamps turning, a long way out past the work lights, and watched them until she could not tell them from the stars.

* * *

Quartermaster Fenn came to the bunkroom at the end of the shift.

He did not have to. There was a terminal by the bunkroom door, as there was by every bunkroom door in Hab Ring C, and the Company's adjustments appeared on it like weather, and you read them or you did not. But Fenn liked to come in person when it was a big one. He said it was a courtesy. He stood in the door of Bay Fourteen with his tablet held against his chest like a hymnal and his clean grey coverall zipped to the collar, and he looked at the five of them sitting on the lower bunks still in their suit liners, still smelling of suit, and he cleared his throat.

"I'm sorry about your man," he said.

Nobody answered him.

"The retrieval crew went out at shift change, as tasked. They weren't able to recover. He'd gone further than the projection. The suit's beacon had—" He glanced at the tablet. "The beacon was off."

"He turned it off," said Juno, very precisely.

"Yes, well. That's against protocol, obviously, but under the circumstances, nobody's going to—it's not going in the record against him. I made sure of that." Fenn looked, for a moment, as though he expected to be thanked. "The other thing is the equipment."

Sol had known it was coming. She had known it since the clip went. Still, she heard herself say, "The equipment."

"One rigger hardsuit, type nine, with helmet and pack, unrecovered. Replacement value eighteen thousand four hundred marks. Under the bond schedule, loss of Company equipment in the course of gang operations is charged to the gang."

"The clip failed," Sol said. "The *clip*. It's a Lindqvist. It's thirty marks a hundred. The eye pulled through the throat. You can see it. The line's still on the hardpoint at Two-Eleven; I left it there. Go and look at it."

"There'll be an inquiry. If the inquiry finds a defect in Company equipment, the charge will be reversed. You're entitled to file a request."

"Dov filed sixteen."

Fenn's face did something complicated. He had been on the platform as long as she had. He had come to Bay Fourteen three years ago, too, with his tablet against his chest, and stood in that door.

"I can't speak to that," he said. "The charge is spread across the gang. Five members, including the deceased. The deceased's share is waived. So four shares." He looked at the tablet. "At bonded rates, four thousand six hundred marks a share is four days added to each bond."

"Four days," said Teague, from his bunk. He said it mildly, the way he said most things. He was sitting with his hands on his knees, and every so often he coughed, a dry deep cough, and spat into a cloth he kept for the purpose. Glass lung. The dust off the cassette ceramic got into everything. "Four days each. For a man's suit."

"For the suit," said Fenn. "Not for the man. Obviously."

"Obviously," said Teague.

"It's on your terminals." Fenn hesitated. He looked as though he wanted to say something else, and then he looked at Sol, and whatever it was he did not say it. "There's a replacement coming in on tonight's shuttle. A transfer from the Tamsin yards. He'll take the open bunk."

"The open bunk," said Juno, "is Kostya's."

"Yes," said Fenn. "That's why it's open." And he went.

They sat for a while without talking. The bunkroom was four meters by six, with three bunks against each long wall, three high, a table bolted to the floor in the middle with a cribbage board burned into its top, and a single hot plate in the corner on which Bracken, who had worked the ice mines on Halvard Four before this and could cook anything on anything, sometimes made a stew out of the protein ration that you could almost believe had once been an animal. There was a small round port at the end, triple-glazed, scratched milky on the outside by twelve years of micrometeoroid dust, through which, if you put your face right up to it, you could see a slice of Kettle. Somebody before Sol's time had stenciled C-14 above the door in Company yellow, and somebody after that had scratched under it, very small, *abandon hope*. Nobody had ever painted it out.

Juno got up and went to Kostya's bunk, the middle one on the left, and began to take his things out of the net. There was not much. A spare liner. A comb. A little carved animal, a goat, he had been whittling for his youngest brother out of a scrap of packing foam. A paper book of puzzles, all done. She folded the liner and put the comb and the goat and the book inside it and tied the arms around it like a parcel, and stood holding it.

"Remittance clause," Sol said. "He said his mother's owed sixteen hundred under the remittance clause."

"They'll say the four days come out of it," Juno said. "You know they will. They'll say his share was waived, and then they'll take it out of the remittance. They'll find a line in the schedule."

"Then we write her," said Teague. "We write her and tell her what to ask for."

"The mail's read."

"Then we write it so it reads all right."

Sol stood up. She went to her own bunk, the bottom one on the right, nearest the port, and sat on it, and turned to the wall.

The wall by her bunk was the same pale grey Company polymer as every wall on the platform, but by her bunk, at the height of her head when she lay down, it was covered in marks. She had scratched them with the point of a rigger's scribe on her first night aboard, twelve years ago: a mark for every week of her bond, six hundred and twenty-four of them, in rows. She had crossed one off every week. At the end of last year, when there were few enough of them left that she could bear it, she had started again in a new place lower down, smaller, a mark for every day.

Fifty-seven of them this morning, in neat rows of ten, and seven at the end. She had crossed off this morning's when she woke.

She took the scribe out of the thigh pocket of her liner, where she always kept it, and scratched four more on the end of the last row. They were not as neat as the others. Her hand was not quite steady.

Sixty-one.

"It's four days," Juno said behind her. "Sol. It's only four days."

"I know."

"They do it to everyone."

"I know." She put the scribe away. Then she reached up into the net over her bunk and took down her flask.

It was the one good thing she owned. A rigger's flask, Company issue, the old heavy kind they had stopped making: steel inside and out with a vacuum between, dented on one shoulder from a fall she had taken in her first year, stamped on the base TTH-9 · 0417, her platform and her bond number. They had given it to her the day she signed. She had been twenty-nine. She had thought, at the time, that it was a kind of welcome. It held a liter of anything and kept it hot for a whole shift, and every shift for twelve years she had filled it in the galley with chicory, because the coffee was charged at bonded rates and the chicory was free, and drunk it through the feed in her helmet during the dark part of the shift when the cold got into her hands.

She unscrewed the cap. There was a mouthful left in it, gone cold since morning. She drank it. It tasted of what it always tasted of, which was burnt roots and steel.

"He wanted me to have a fish soup for him," she said. "At Pier Six."

"I'll come with you," Juno said. "When you go. I'm only eleven months behind you. I'll be there before the soup's cold."

"It'll be cold."

"Then I'll have mine cold."

Bracken got up and went to the hot plate and began, without a word, to make something.

* * *

The shuttle came in at the second hour of third shift. Sol heard it dock through the floor, the long grinding shudder of the clamps, the hollow boom of the lock cycling. She was lying on her bunk with her face to the marks. She did not sleep. Nobody in Bay Fourteen slept, except possibly Bracken, who could sleep anywhere and did so with his arms folded on his chest like a corpse in a church.

At the fourth hour there was a knock on the door.

Nobody knocked on bunkroom doors. Proctors did not knock and riggers did not need to. Sol lay and listened to it and thought that she was not going to get up, and then Juno said "Oh for God's sake," and Sol got up.

The boy in the corridor was nineteen if he was a day, and he looked younger. He had a kit bag over one shoulder and a Company-issue liner on that was too big for him in the shoulders and too short in the legs, and his hair was cut very close to his skull in the transport-ship way, so you could see the shape of his head, which was long and narrow and somehow anxious. He was dark-skinned and thin and had the kind of hands Sol had learned, over twelve years, to look at first: big hands, out of proportion with the rest of him, the knuckles cracked from cold. Farm hands. Somebody had put him to work outside, young.

"Is this Fourteen?" he said. "C-Fourteen? They said Gang Four was C-Fourteen."

"It says so over the door."

He looked up at the stencil, and under it at the scratching, and she watched him read it and decide not to say anything about it.

"I'm Okafor," he said. "Pell Okafor. They told me to report to the gang chief. Dace?"

"That's me."

"I'm assigned to you."

"I know."

"From Tamsin's Reach. The yards there. I was on the—I did pressure work at the grain silos before that, and then six months at the Tamsin yards in a rigging school, and I've done forty hours outside, suited, and I know—" He stopped. "They said there was an opening."

"There's a bunk," Sol said. "Middle on the left. It was emptied about six hours ago."

He looked past her, into the bunkroom, at the middle bunk on the left, which Juno had stripped and Bracken had wiped down, so that it was just a pale grey shelf with a thin pad on it and the net above it empty. He looked at it for long enough that she knew somebody on the shuttle had told him.

"I'm sorry," he said. "About your man."

"So's the Company. They put four days on all our bonds to show how sorry they were."

He did not know what to say to that, so he did not say anything, which she gave him a small mark for.

"Come in," she said. "Don't touch the hot plate. Don't touch Bracken. Shift's at the sixth hour, you'll be on the Ring with us, and if you do one thing on the Ring I don't tell you to do I will cut your line myself. You understand?"

"Yes, chief."

"Do you know what a Lindqvist clip is?"

"It's a—it's the standard harness clip, the—"

"In the morning I'll show you how to check one. You'll check yours every time you clip in. You'll check it if you unclip to scratch your nose. You'll check it in your sleep." She stood aside. "Go on."

He went in with his kit bag and stood by the empty bunk as though waiting for it to give him permission. Then he put the bag in the net and sat down on the edge of the pad and put his big cracked hands on his knees, and she saw that they were shaking a little, and that he was trying to stop them.

Juno looked at him from her bunk, up top on the right, with her chin on her arms.

"Tamsin's Reach," she said. "That's the agri-world, the one with the—what is it—"

"Orchards," said Pell. "Mostly orchards. Pears."

"Pears," said Juno. "God. What did you sign for?"

"Twelve years."

"No, I mean what for. What's the debt."

"Oh." He looked at his hands. "My father's land. There was a blight. The note came due."

"So it's twelve years on the Ring for a pear orchard."

"For my family's place in it," he said, and his chin came up a little, which Sol also gave him a mark for. "My sisters are still there. They get to stay."

"Good," Juno said, and meant it, and put her head down.

Sol lay down again with her face to the wall and her flask beside her on the pad. After a while the lights went down to the night setting, blue, and the platform made its night sounds: the far deep hum of the reactor, the ticking of the hull, the air plant cycling, and once, a long way off, the clang of a door. Bracken snored. Teague coughed, and spat, and coughed. Somebody was crying very quietly, and she thought it was Juno, and then she realized it was not; it was the new boy, in the middle bunk on the left, with his face pressed into the pad so that no one would hear.

She did not say anything to him. It would not have helped. The first night was the first night, and everybody had one.

After a while the crying stopped and was replaced by something else, so faint she thought for a moment it was the air plant. Then she realized the boy was singing. Under his breath, to himself, the way you sing to keep the dark off when you are small. It was a farm song, something about a cart and a river. She did not know it. It had a good tune.

She reached out in the blue dark and found the marks on the wall with her fingertips, and ran them along the rows, ten and ten and ten, the way she did every night, feeling the little ridges the scribe had raised in the polymer. Sixty-one. She found the four new ones at the end, rougher than the rest. She left them alone.

Sixty-one days. Then the hauler. The *Ostrava Mercy*, due on Day Three Hundred, to take the finished crews home before the Lighting. Then eleven weeks in the hauler's hold, and then the docks, and Pier Six under the old cranes, and a bowl of fish soup with the bones still in it.

She thought about it. For the first time in twelve years she let herself think about it all the way through, the way Kostya had asked her to. It did not make the days longer. It did not make them shorter either. It only made her very tired.

"Chief?" said the boy, in the dark.

"What."

"Sorry. I didn't know you were awake."

"I'm not."

"Is it true you can see the whole Ring from out on the face? All of it at once?"

She thought about Kostya, sixty meters past the end of the chain, saying *I never saw the whole thing.*

"Only if you're a long way out," she said. "Go to sleep, Okafor."

He went quiet. In the blue dark the marks were under her fingers. Somewhere above the platform, two hundred thousand kilometers over Kettle and turning slowly in the dark, there was a type nine hardsuit with its lamps still burning, and it would go on burning, the riggers said, for about a year, until the cells ran down.
