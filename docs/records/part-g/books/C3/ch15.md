# Chapter 15: Assets

*The Door · 3190*

The breath-seller on the Number Three Kettle was a man called Teague, and he'd sold to the reeves under the counter for twenty years, whenever the Margrave's ration came up short and somebody's boat needed to be somewhere it wasn't supposed to be. He was a fat soft-spoken man with a lazy eye and a shop at the bottom of the Kettle's well, behind a door with no sign, and he owed me because I'd once looked the other way when looking the other way was worth a great deal to him. I'd never collected. I'd always thought I'd keep it for something.

When I got to his door the Kettle was being inventoried.

The Kettles are three old fuel tanks the size of small moons, lashed in a triangle off Tarrow's inner ring, hollowed out and lived in for longer than anyone remembers. Nine thousand people in them, last count. Traders, mostly, and the people who live off traders. I came in through the Number Three's lower lock in the *Mendicant*'s shadow with my drive dark and found two tenders already on the tank's skin, sitting like ticks, with that red line where their legs touched the hull; and inside, in the great hollow of the well, I could hear it before I saw it. Not screaming. Not much. That long, orderly, scraping quiet.

Teague's door was shut. I knocked the way the reeves used to knock. After a long time it opened a crack, and his lazy eye looked out at me, and then the other one.

"Rook," he said. "God help me. Come in."

He had breath. Of course he had; men like Teague always have. He had a room behind the shop stacked to the ceiling with full-draw bottles that he'd been holding back from the Margrave for years, waiting for the price to be right, and he stood among them in the lamplight wringing his soft hands while the scraping went on somewhere above us.

"They've done the upper wells already," he said. "They've done the market. They took the Hessel family out of the room next to mine an hour ago. Six of them. The little one was asleep. They didn't even wake her up, Rook. They just—" He made a closing gesture with his hands. "And Kask's people standing there with lists. Ticking them off."

"I need breath," I said. "Enough for the Door and back. Maybe more."

"The *Door*?"

"Can you do it?"

He looked at me with both his eyes. Then he laughed, a high, wet, frightened laugh.

"Take it," he said. "Take all of it. What am I going to do with it? What are they going to do with *me*?" He was pulling bottles off the stack already. "Take the lot, Rook. Get the Door. Do whatever it is you think you're going to do. I'll call it paid."

I took enough. Not all of it. I left him enough to run, if he had somewhere to run to; I don't think he did. The last I saw of Teague he was standing in his doorway at the bottom of the well with his soft hands hanging at his sides, looking up into the dark of the tank where the scraping was coming down, level by level, toward him.

* * *

I'd said two days. It took me two and a half to get back to the Hulk. The Kettles to Hesper is a long run, and I had to go the long way round, through the dark of the inner ring, because there were tenders everywhere now — coming and going between the habitats and Hesper in long black processions, each one with a grey cluster of pods hanging from its belly like eggs.

They were all going to the Hulk.

I saw it from a long way out. I'd brought the *Mendicant* round Hesper's limb low and dark, and as the station came up over the moon's edge I cut everything and let her drift and just looked, because I couldn't do anything else.

The Hulk was lit.

For two hundred years it had been dead and black. I'd known it dead and black my whole life. Now there were lights on it — not its own lights, not windows, but the hard white work-lamps of the Margrave's crews, strung along the spokes and the rings and the docking arms; and round it, thick as gnats round a lamp, the tenders, coming and going, settling on the outer docks and lifting off again. Where they settled they left the pods. I could see them, even from that far: grey clusters, piled along the outer rings and the docking arms, hundreds of them, thousands, clinging to the old station's skin the way frogspawn clings to a reed. And among them the little red sparks of the Margrave's cutters, moving from cluster to cluster.

The collection depot. Everybody they'd taken from Lanthorn and Sallow and the Kettles, brought here and stacked, waiting.

Somewhere inside it, in the core, in the old round rooms with the seats, Haddie and Wren and forty-odd children were hiding in the cold.

I'd been gone two and a half days.

* * *

I went in through a picker's way. There are a hundred of them in the Hulk, if you've picked it long enough — holes and cracks and dead vents and half-collapsed corridors that the station's builders never meant for anybody to use — and I knew most of them, and the Margrave's crews, who'd never picked anything in their lives, knew none. I left the *Mendicant* tucked into the shadow of a dead tower on the station's dark side, where the work-lamps didn't reach, and went across in my suit on a line, and in through a crack in the tower's skin I'd found when I was thirty-four and never told anyone about.

From there it was three hours to the core. I'll not tell you about them. Dark and cold and slow, crawling through places a man my size shouldn't fit, listening. Twice I heard the drones — the scrape and click of their jointed legs on a deck somewhere above me — and lay still in the dark until they'd gone. Once, through a cracked port, I looked out onto one of the outer docking arms and saw the pods up close. They were the size of a person standing, grey and hard and faintly glistening, stacked three and four deep along the arm, held in place by some kind of webbing. I couldn't see in. I don't know if I'd have wanted to.

When I got to the hall, the lamps I'd strung were dark.

That was all right. I'd told them to go deeper. I went down the hall in the dark with my helmet lamp on low, past the benches, past the railings, to the slope of fallen plates at the far end, and found the gap behind them where the gate was, and the stair. The gate was open. I'd expected that.

What I hadn't expected was that it had been cut.

It had been an iron gate, old and heavy, with a lock as big as two fists. Somebody had taken a torch to it. The bars were sheared through in a neat curve and bent back, and the edges of the cuts were still bright.

I went down the stair.

* * *

The place with the seats was empty.

I'd found it once, years ago, picking — a long low room at the bottom of the stair, through a round ribbed corridor, all curved metal like the inside of a pipe, with rows of rotted padded seats bolted to the floor and dead consoles along one wall, and at the far end a little platform with a plain chair on it. I'd thought it was a chapel. It had that feel. It was the only part of the Hulk I'd ever been in where I didn't want to pick anything.

They'd been there. I could see it. Blankets on the floor between the seats. The spare scrubber I'd left them, still ticking. Water bulbs. A little pile of the white numbers from the fallen plates up in the hall, that the children must have carried down with them to play with — a nine, a two, a nought, a seven — stacked into a tower on one of the seats. The suit patches I'd left over the round corridor hatches, sealing the cold out, all of them torn away.

And the floor. The floor in the aisle between the seats was smeared with something dark, in a long trail, toward the corridor.

I stood there a long time with my lamp on the floor.

"Absalom."

It came from the wall. From the left-hand wall, where — I'd never looked at it properly, picking — there was a row of square plates set into the curved metal, a dozen of them, each the size of a cot, each with a line of the old hand cut into it. One of them, near the end of the row, was hanging open. Not much. A hand's width. And in the gap, in the dark, something moved.

"Haddie?"

"Get me out of here," said Haddie Coyle. "I've been sharing with a dead man for a day and a half and he's terrible company."

* * *

She'd pried the plate off with a cutting bar when she heard them coming, and climbed into the hole behind it, and pulled the plate to after her. There was a body in there. Not much of one. Bones, mostly, in the rags of a grey coat, lying on its back on a shelf of metal with its hands folded on its chest. She'd lain down beside it in the dark and held her breath. They hadn't looked.

She was very cold. She was very old. When I got her out she couldn't stand at first, and I sat her on one of the rotted seats and gave her water and put my own coat round her, and she let me, which frightened me more than anything else had.

"Tell me," I said.

"They came on the second day." Her voice was a thread. "Not long after you left. I heard them in the hall above. The drones, first. Then people. Kask's reeves. And then her." She looked at me over her spectacles; one lens was cracked. "She came herself, Absalom. The Margrave. In her red coat, with that braid. I saw her through the gap. She walked in through the round corridor with six reeves behind her and two of those black things, and she stood there in the middle of this room and looked round at the children, and she said: *Well. Here you all are.*"

"How did they find you?"

"I don't know. Heat. Breath. Those things can see in the dark, I think, like the herald could. Or someone told her. I don't know." She closed her eyes. "Wren fought. Kesse fought. Some of the bigger ones. They had cutting bars, they had the knife you left. It didn't matter. It didn't last a minute." She was quiet. "The black things took some of them. The little ones, mostly. The ones who couldn't run. Put them in the pods, right here, in front of everybody. The rest the reeves took. Marched them out up the corridor."

"And Wren?"

"Kask took her." Haddie opened her eyes. "Personally. Had her held. Walked up to her and looked at her a good while. She knew who she was. She said: *You're the one who took my seed.* And Wren spat at her." A ghost of a smile. "She'd have liked you to see that."

"Where?"

"I don't know. Up. To wherever Kask is. She said—" Haddie stopped. She put her hand inside the coat I'd wrapped round her, and drew something out, and held it in her lap.

It was the herald. Black and warm and purring in its slow breathing way, in its felt blanket.

"She asked for this," said Haddie. "She asked Wren where it was. And Wren said you'd taken it with you." She looked at me. "Wren knew I was in the wall, Absalom. She saw me go in. She looked straight at that plate and told the Margrave of Kell Deep that you'd taken it to the Kettles to sell."

I didn't say anything. I couldn't.

"So Kask doesn't know it's here," said Haddie. "She thinks you've got it. She'll be waiting for you to bring it back."

"Then I'll bring it back," I said.

* * *

Haddie argued. I'll give her that. She sat on the rotted seat with my coat round her and the herald in her lap and argued at me in a voice like paper tearing for a quarter of an hour, and every word she said was right. Kask would kill me. Kask would take the herald and kill me and keep Wren and the children anyway, because why wouldn't she? I had nothing to trade but a thing she already wanted and a life she didn't. I should take the breath I'd brought and the *Mendicant* and go to Gallows, now, alone, the way we'd planned, and shut the Door if I could, and maybe — maybe — if I did, the big thing behind it would never come through, and the pods would stay where they were, and one day somebody would cut them open. That was the only sum that came out. That was the only thing that might save anyone.

"I know," I said.

"Then *do* it."

"I can't."

"Why not?"

I looked at her. At the old woman in the rotted chapel, in my coat, with her cracked spectacles and her bent hands on the warm black seed of the thing that had come out of the Door. At the tower of white numbers on the seat beside her that some child had built. A nine, a two, a nought, a seven.

"Because I did it before," I said. "The sum. I did the sum and it came out, and I did what it said." I heard my own voice, very flat. "I'm not doing it again."

Haddie looked at me.

"What sum, Absalom?" she said quietly.

I didn't answer her. I took the herald out of her lap, blanket and all, and put it under my arm, and went up the stair.

* * *

I called Kask on the general band from the hall, standing among the fallen plates, so that every cutter and every reeve in range would hear.

"Teodora," I said. "It's Absalom. I've got your seed. I'm coming in."

There was a long pause. Then the voice, low and slow, the hand on the back of the neck.

"*Absalom. How nice. Come up to the main dock. Come slowly.*"

* * *

The main dock of the Hulk had been a great hall once, the biggest room in the station, a long space with a wall of armoured glass that looked out across the moon. I'd picked it a dozen times as a young man, for the scrap in its gantries. It was full now. Full of pods, stacked along the walls three deep under the glass, grey and glistening; full of the Margrave's reeves in their red coats, standing about with lists; full of drones, the black jointed things, standing motionless in a row along the back wall like furniture somebody had forgotten to put away.

And in the middle of it, in a chair someone had dragged out of the old dock control room, with a reeve on either side of her and Benno Strick standing at her shoulder, sat the Margrave of Kell Deep.

Teodora Kask was sixty that year. She was a big woman, heavy and slow, with a long grey braid over one shoulder and a white scar along her jaw from the night she took the Deep, and she wore the red coat with the white open hand on the breast as though she'd been born in it. She had small, clever, light-brown eyes. She had always had the stillest hands of anyone I ever knew. She sat in her dragged-out chair with those hands folded in her lap and watched me walk the length of the dock toward her between the stacks of pods, with the herald under my arm, and she smiled.

"Absalom Rook," she said. "Seven years. You look old."

"So do you."

"I am old. I've earned it." She held out one of her still hands. "Give it here."

I didn't move.

"Where is she?"

Kask looked at me for a moment longer. Then she lifted one finger, and two reeves came out from behind the nearest stack of pods with Wren between them.

They'd hurt her. Not badly — not the way I'd seen the Margrave's people hurt runners, in the Deep, in the old days. Her face was bruised down one side and her lip was split and her burned hands were tied in front of her with wire. She walked between the two reeves with her chin up, the gangway look, and when she saw me she stopped walking, and stood still, and looked at me with no expression at all.

"There," said Kask. "Now give it here."

"And the children."

"What children?"

"The ones you took from this station two days ago. Thirty-two from the Deep and eleven from Lanthorn and a baby. Where are they?"

"Inventoried," said Kask simply. "They're in the depot. With everybody else." She tilted her head. "You didn't think I could give them back, did you? Absalom. Really. They're on the receiver's list now. It knows their numbers. It's counted them. You can't take a thing off the receiver's list once it's on; I asked. It said that would constitute a further default." She shrugged her heavy shoulders. "I'm only the factor. I don't make the rules."

"Then what do you want me for?"

"I don't want you for anything. I want my seed." She held out her hand again. "It's the receiver's property. It came out of the Door, it belongs to the receiver, and when the receiver comes through I'd like very much to be the one who hands it back. It'll look well. It'll be remembered when terms are made." She smiled. "Mine, I mean. My terms. I've got very good ones."

"Passage," I said.

"Passage. For me and my household. And a commission. Factor's rate, the receiver calls it." She seemed genuinely pleased. "It's going to take everybody out through the Door, you know, once it's through. Everybody it's counted. To wherever it came from. It says there's places out there — whole worlds, Absalom, with skies. Most of them'll be put to work, I suppose. The receiver's a little vague on that. But I'll be put on a ship with a cabin and a share, and I'll see a sky before I die." She folded her hands. "Isn't that something? After all this?"

I looked at her. I looked at Wren, between the reeves, watching me with her bruised face and her blank eyes.

"Let her go," I said, "and I'll give it to you."

"No," said Kask. "You'll give it to me and I'll do what I like." She nodded to Benno Strick. "Take it off him, Benno."

He came. He was twenty-five now, I suppose, Benno, a tall lad with an earnest face, and he came across the dock floor toward me with his hand on his belt and his eyes not quite meeting mine, and stopped a pace off, and held out his hand.

"Please, Rook," he said quietly.

I gave it to him. What else was I going to do?

He took it back to Kask. She took it from him in its felt blanket and held it on her knees, and looked down at it, at the black warm seed humming there, and stroked it once with her still hand, the way you'd stroke a cat.

"Good," she said. "Good. Now." She looked up. "Since you're here, Absalom, and since it'll be a while before the receiver's through, I think there's something I'd like to do. For old times' sake."

She looked at Wren.

"Do you know why you were tithed, girl?"

Wren didn't answer.

"I asked you a question. You were Lanthorn's twenty-first year. Four-three-seven. Do you know why?"

"Because the lot fell on me," said Wren. Her voice was flat. "That's how the tithe works. One in ten. By lot."

"That's how it's supposed to work," said Kask. "One in ten, by lot, drawn by the reeve in front of the Lash Council, so everybody can see it's fair. And it was drawn that way. Twenty-first year, Lanthorn. Absalom drew it himself. And do you know whose name came up fourth on the roll?" She was watching my face. Not Wren's. Mine. "Jory Rook. Nine years old. The reeve's own boy."

I didn't move. I didn't look at Wren. I looked at Kask.

"He came to me," said the Margrave. "That night. Up to the house at Kell, with the roll in his hand. I'd never seen him like that. He'd been my reeve for six years — the best I ever had, the one who never asked — and he stood in my study with the roll in his hand and tears on his face and asked me to let him change one name." She smiled, very slightly. "I said yes. Of course I said yes. I liked him. And I knew that if I let him do it, he'd belong to me for the rest of his life. A man who's done that, Absalom, can't ever go back. Can he?"

Wren was looking at me. I could feel it. Like heat on the side of my face.

"He went back down to Lanthorn," said Kask, "and he took his boy's name off the roll, and he looked down it for somebody else to put on. Somebody nobody would miss. Somebody with no father and no friends on the Council. A scrubber's girl from the Number Six hulk, whose mother had nobody to ask questions for her." She turned her head at last, and looked at Wren. "He chose you."

The dock was very quiet. The reeves had stopped moving. Along the back wall the black drones stood in their row like furniture.

"Rook," said Wren.

I made myself look at her.

She was standing between the two reeves with her bound hands in front of her. Her face had changed. Not much. I'd been watching faces my whole life, and I've seen a lot of them change, and I'll tell you that the worst ones don't change much at all. Hers just went still, the way water goes still, all the way down. And her eyes on mine were the eyes of a nine-year-old girl on a gangway, writing me down — except that now, at last, she'd finished. She'd got the whole of it.

"Is it true?" she said.

There was a lie on the bench in front of me. There always is. I'd told myself in that skiff, the day I pulled her out, that I'd tell the truth in this, wherever it cost me. I'd not known then what it would cost.

"Yes," I said.

She didn't say anything for a moment. Then she spat. Not at Kask. At me. Across the two metres of dock floor between us, at my feet.

"Your Jory died anyway," said Teodora Kask softly. "Didn't he, Absalom? Two years later. Red fever. All for nothing." She stroked the herald on her knee. "Benno. Take him somewhere and teach him some manners, and then put him with the others. I'd like to give him to the receiver myself, when it comes. I think it'll appreciate a reeve."

* * *

Benno Strick taught me some manners in a dead storeroom off the main dock with two other reeves holding my arms. He didn't enjoy it. I could tell. He kept saying he was sorry. I'd trained him, after all; he did it properly. When they were done they left me on the floor in the dark and locked the door, and I lay there for a long time with my face against the cold deck and my mouth full of blood and listened to the station.

Through the walls, faint, the receiver was speaking its notice again. All bands, all speakers, the same calm words in the same pleasant voice. *Debtors will be inventoried in order. Debtors will be held in custody pending reconciliation.* And under it, through the deck, through my cheek, a vibration I couldn't place at first: a deep, slow, rhythmic shudder, as if something enormous a long way off were breathing.

I thought about Jory.

I've not said much about him in this account. I've tried not to. He was nine. He had his mother's hair and my hands, and he was frightened of the dark and pretended not to be, and he wanted to be a picker when he grew up, because pickers found treasure. When his name came up fourth on the roll in the cargo pod on Lanthorn, in the twenty-first year, in front of the whole Lash Council, I read it out in my reeve's voice the way I'd read out three hundred others, and nobody in the pod moved, and I rolled the paper up and put it in my coat and went home and sat on the edge of his bunk and watched him sleep for six hours. And then I went to Kell.

He died two years later. Red fever. In my arms, in our cell on Lanthorn, on a hot night when the scrubbers were failing. He was eleven. His mother had already gone by then. I buried him on the Lanthorn lash, the way we bury people, out of a lock into the ring shadow, and Garra Moss put his big hand on my shoulder and said nothing, and I went back to Kell the next week and told the Margrave I was done.

Seven years. Every night. I'd done the sum every night for seven years — Jory's life against a scrubber's girl's — and every night it had come out the same, and every night I'd told myself that it came out, that any father would have done it, that it was a sum, and sums are only numbers, and nobody blames a number.

And it had all been for nothing, because he died anyway.

I lay on the floor of the dead storeroom with the receiver's voice coming through the walls and that vast slow breathing coming up through the deck, and for the first time in seven years I didn't do the sum. I just lay there and let it be what it was.

Some time later, I don't know how long, the door opened.
