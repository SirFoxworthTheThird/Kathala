# Chapter 5: Interest

*The Ledger · 2794*

Every child in the Lungs knew the story of the Interest of Sorrow, because every child in the Lungs had stood on Sorrow Day in the corridor outside the family's cell, in the cold, with their tag held in their fist, and listened to the Turning come down the speaker-wire from the Nave.

Ysolde had stood there herself, before she was taken up. She remembered her mother's hand on her shoulder. She remembered the sound — the clatter of the Count Board, faint and tinny down the wire, going on and on far longer than it did on any other morning, because on Sorrow Day the plates turned *up*. And she remembered the reading afterward, the Prelate's voice, old and slow, telling the story while the Lungs stood in their corridors and wept.

*In the hundred and fortieth year of the Count, in the time of the Third Prelate, the Great Rattle came into the Lungs, and a quarter of the owed died in a single winter, and their shares were left unpaid. And the Third Prelate went into the Reliquary, and fasted nine days, and came out, and said: For every soul that dies owing, a sorrow is added to the Count, that the living may carry what the dead cannot. And this is the Interest of Sorrow, and it is just.*

And then the response, from every corridor at once, a whisper multiplied by a hundred thousand mouths:

*It is just.*

She found the Third Prelate's edict on the morning after the Bailiff-Captain's visit, in the Edict Library under the Second Chancery, in a glass case where, according to the card beside it, it had lain for two hundred years. It was a single sheet of Combine plastic, yellowed and brittle, written over in a careful hand and sealed at the foot with a round black impression: the Prelate's seal. The text was exactly as she had heard it read every year of her life. She stood and looked at it through the glass for a long time, until the librarian, a mild old man named Brother Ruel, came and asked whether he could help her.

"I've taken over the Ninth," she said. "Brother Anselm's ledger. I'm reading back through the volumes, and the Interest lines in the second century are hard to follow. I wanted to see the edict."

"Ah," said Brother Ruel. "Anselm was here about the edict too, you know. Last year." He smiled sadly. "He asked the same thing."

"Did he?"

"He asked to see the binding records." The librarian nodded toward the far end of the library, where the shelves gave way to a row of iron cabinets. "The book-mending ledgers. Every rebinding in the Nave is entered there, you see, by volume and year. We're very thorough." He beamed. "He was very interested in the second-century rebindings. I told him there had been a great many. The old plastic doesn't last."

* * *

It took her four days to be certain.

She did the work in the evenings, after the Chancery had emptied, in the stacks behind Anselm's desk. She had the Ninth Ledger's oldest volumes, the black ones, down off the shelves and open on the reading table under the lamp: Volume Forty-one, which covered the hundred and thirty-eighth to the hundred and forty-second years of the Count, and Forty-two, and Forty-three. She had Brother Ruel's book-mending ledgers, which she had borrowed for *research into the material history of the Ninth*, a phrase that had made the librarian very happy. And she had a jeweller's glass, which she had bought in the Nave market for three days' stipend and which she kept in her sleeve.

What she found was this.

In Volume Forty-one, the entries for the hundred and thirty-ninth year were written in the brown soot ink that the Auditory had made from lamp-black in the first two centuries, before the iron mines on Kell were reopened. The ink had faded to the colour of dried blood. The pages were Combine plastic, like the edict, thin and yellow. The hand was a single keeper's hand, cramped and slanting.

The entries for the hundred and fortieth year were in the same hand. The same slant, the same cramped loops. But under the jeweller's glass the ink was black. Not faded black; black. The black of iron-gall, which the Auditory had begun to make in the two hundred and sixtieth year and had used ever since. And where the hundred and fortieth year began, the pages were not quite the same plastic as the pages before. They were a fraction thicker. A fraction whiter. And at the gutter, if you bent the book back as far as you dared, you could see that they had not been bound in with the rest of the gathering. They had been sewn in afterward — tipped in on a separate thread, where a block of older pages had been cut away close to the spine. She could see the stubs of the old pages, a hair's breadth wide, still caught in the original stitching.

The Interest of Sorrow was on the new pages. All of it. Every Reckoning from the hundred and fortieth year onward, the great upward leap, the sorrow added — every one was in iron-gall ink, in a careful imitation of the dead keeper's hand, on pages that had been sewn into a book that was a hundred and twenty years old at the time they were made.

She turned to the book-mending ledgers. *Ninth Ledger, Third Chancery. Volumes 38 through 71. Rebound for preservation.* There was a date. It was in the same black ink.

*The 279th year of the Count.*

She sat back from the table.

She did the next part slowly, because she did not want to make an error, and because she was afraid of what it would come to. She took the official Founding figure, the one on the first page of every novice's primer — *Four hundred and six million marks, the cost of our bringing* — and she took the tithe totals from the Ninth's volumes, and from the Chancery summaries for the other eight Ledgers, which she could read from the stacks without signing for them; and she took the ordinary interest of the Count, the small fixed rate that every primer taught, the rate that had been charged before the Great Rattle. And she left out the Interest of Sorrow. She ran it, year by year, on scrap sheets that she burned afterward in the Chancery stove.

It took three nights. On the third night, at a little after the middle bell, the column ran out.

With the Founding figure the primers taught — not Anselm's figure, not whatever *per F.B.* meant, but the Auditory's own — and without the Interest of Sorrow, the Count reached zero in the three hundred and second year.

It was close enough to Anselm's *Year 280* to tell her that he had not been mad, and far enough from it to tell her that he had known something about the Founding figure that she did not. And it was thirty-nine years ago. Either way — his number or theirs — every hour of tithe that had gone into the Count since before Ysolde herself was born had gone into nothing. Into a hole. Into the Interest of a Sorrow that had been invented, out of nothing, with iron-gall ink, by somebody sitting at a table in this Nave in the two hundred and seventy-ninth year of the Count, looking at a column that was about to run out.

She thought about that person for a long time. She thought about them sitting at a table like this one, with a lamp like this one, and a dead man's book open in front of them, and a knife for cutting out the pages. She wondered whether they had been frightened. She wondered whether they had thought they were doing good.

She burned the last scrap sheet in the stove and watched the figures go brown and curl and catch. Then she went to the window of the Chancery, the high one, and leaned, and looked across the dark gulf of the Ledger Hall at the Count Board.

*3,907,0—*

She could not read the rest from here. She did not need to. She found that she knew it, to the mark.

* * *

The drop shuttle left the Nave's lower dock at the sixth bell every day and fell for forty minutes to the surface of Hesper, and Ysolde took it on the fifth day with a satchel on her shoulder and an inspection warrant in her sleeve, signed by her own hand as keeper of the Ninth, authorising her to visit Spokes Seventeen through Twenty-two for the purpose of acquainting herself with the households of her ledger.

It was a perfectly ordinary thing to do. New keepers did it all the time. She told herself so all the way down.

The Lungs began where the shuttle doors opened: a cold that came at you like a wall, the smell of the ice, and the noise — not the hush of the Nave but the hum of a hundred thousand people living close together in the dark, pumps and voices and footsteps and the clang of bottles in the racks. The corridors were cut into the ice, as they had been since the beginning, lined with sheeting that had been patched and repatched until it was more patch than sheet. Down every corridor, at every junction, someone had painted the Holy Number in white — HV-V-0001 — and under it, in a hundred hands, the response: *When the Count is zero.*

And among them, here and there, scratched into the sheeting with a knife and ground half-away by Bailiffs' grinders and scratched again: circles.

She went to Piet first. She had not meant to. Her feet took her.

He lived on Spoke Twelve, in a single room he shared with two other divers, and he was home, because it was his rest-shift, and he opened the door in his undershirt with his hair standing up and a cup in his hand and his face went from sleep to astonishment to a grin so wide it made her chest hurt.

"Ysolde!"

"Hello, Piet."

"It's not a feast day."

"No."

"Is something wrong? You look—" He peered at her. "You look like a keeper."

"I am a keeper." She tried to smile. "I've been given the Ninth. Brother Anselm's ledger. I'm on inspection."

"The Ninth! The deep spokes?" Piet whistled. "That's a big book. That's a promotion, isn't it? That's—" His face changed. "Anselm. The one who went to the Breath."

"Yes."

"I heard about that." He stood back from the door. "Come in. Come in, you'll freeze."

She sat on the edge of his bunk while he heated water on a coil and talked. He was twenty-two. He had been diving for five years, since he was seventeen, in an old patched skimmer called the *Dutiful* that belonged to a diving family on Spoke Fourteen and that he crewed for a share. He had their father's long face and their mother's laugh, and he had the beginning of a cough, a small dry catch at the end of every breath that he did not seem to notice and that she noticed every time. He talked about the *Dutiful*, and about a girl on Spoke Thirteen, and about a seam they had found last week that had nearly killed them and had been worth it. He did not talk about his tithe. She looked, while he poured the water, at the slate tacked to his wall where the Lungs folk posted their tally, and saw a red mark on it that she knew the meaning of.

"You're behind."

He glanced at the slate and away. "A bit."

"How much?"

"It's nothing. A bad month. We lost a dive to weather, and then the *Dutiful*'s scoops needed relining, and they put that on the crew share—" He shrugged. "I'll make it up. We've got a good seam."

"Piet."

"It's *nothing,* Ysolde." He put the cup in her hands. "Drink that. You're grey."

She drank it. It was hot and tasted of the coil. Her brother sat down on the other bunk, across from her, and leaned his elbows on his knees, and at his throat, on its cord, his tag swung forward into the light: their father's tag, a half-disc of dull steel with a broken toothed edge, worn smooth.

She looked at it, and thought about the tag inside Anselm's ledger with the circle cut into its face, and did not say anything.

* * *

Spoke Twenty was the second deepest of the Ninth's spokes, two levels above the Drowned Decks, where the cold came up through the floor and the sheeting on the walls was furred with frost. Corin Asch kept a bottle shop there, at the end of a dead-end corridor: a narrow cave with a counter and a back room, lined floor to ceiling with breath bottles in various states of repair, and a sign over the door that said only ASCH — BOTTLES — SEALS.

He was behind the counter when she came in, bent over a scoop valve with a lens in his eye, and he did not look up.

"Shop's shut."

"The door was open."

"Then shut it on your way out." He looked up. He took the lens out of his eye. He was a lean man of about forty-five, with greying black hair tied back and a face that had been handsome before the rattle had hollowed it out, and a long pale scar that ran down the left side of his throat from his ear to his collarbone; and his eyes, when they saw her grey robe, went flat and dark as the bottles on his shelves. "Ah. An auditor."

"My name is Ysolde Marr. I keep the Ninth Ledger."

"The Ninth's kept by Anselm Ferrow."

"Brother Anselm is dead."

"I know he's dead," said Corin Asch. "I wondered if you did."

She took the tag out of her sleeve, the half-disc with the circle cut into it, and laid it on the counter between them.

He looked at it for a long moment. Then he put his hand over it — flat, quickly, the way you'd cover a spark — and slid it off the counter into his own palm and closed his fist on it.

"Where did you get this?"

"It was in the spine of the Ninth Ledger. With a note that said to come to you." She held his eye. "And a reckoning sheet. With a column of numbers on it that comes to zero."

He didn't say anything. He looked at her, and she saw him weighing it — weighing her — and she stood still and let him, because she had been weighed before, at nine, by testers in this same ice, and she had passed.

"Back room," said Corin finally. "Now."

* * *

The back room was smaller than the shop and colder. There was a bench, a lamp, a heater that did not work, and more bottles — big ones, the full-draw pressure bottles that the skimmers carried, each as tall as a woman and wide enough that she could not have put her arms around it. Corin sat on the bench and did not invite her to sit, so she stood.

"He came to me two years ago," Corin said. "Your Brother Anselm. Came down on inspection, like you, with his warrant in his sleeve. Asked a lot of questions about the old diving families. Which ones went back furthest. Which ones still had their sticks."

"Sticks?"

He looked at her as though she had said something stupid, and then, slowly, as though he were remembering what she was. "You're Lungs-born. Aren't you? I can hear it, under the Nave voice."

"Spoke Eleven."

"And they took you up young."

"At nine."

"Then you'd not remember." He reached down beside the bench and lifted something up and held it out to her: a rod of black metal as long as her forearm, its surface covered from end to end with fine notched marks. Thousands of them, in rows. "Mark-stick. The old families keep them. Every dive your family ever made, every tithe paid, notched in. Father to son, mother to daughter. Mine goes back to before the Starving. Some go further." He turned it in his hands. "We don't trust paper, down here. Paper's kept up there."

"And Anselm wanted to see them."

"He wanted to compare them. Our notches against your books. He wanted to know if they matched." Corin's mouth twisted. "Mostly they do. Your people are thorough. But there's one thing on the sticks that isn't on your books, Sister, and one thing on your books that isn't on the sticks."

"Sorrow Day."

He went still. Then he nodded.

"Every family keeps a mark for the Reckoning," he said. "One notch, crosswise, every year, when the Count's read. On the old sticks — the oldest ones, the ones that go back past the hundred and fortieth year — there's a Reckoning notch every year. Regular as breath. And on some of them, round about the hundred and fortieth year, there's a second mark, beside the first. A circle." He touched the place on his own stick. "For the Rattle. For the dead. They marked their dead. But there's no Sorrow mark. Not for a hundred and forty years after. Not on any stick I've ever seen."

"When does it start?"

"Two hundred and eightieth year," said Corin Asch. "Give or take. My grandmother was a girl that year. She told my mother, and my mother told me: one year there was no Sorrow Day, and the next year there was, and the Auditors came down the ladders and said there always had been. That it went back to the Rattle. That it was in the books." He laid the stick across his knees. "And it was in the books. They showed people. Black on white."

"Iron-gall," said Ysolde.

"What?"

"Nothing." She found that she had put her hand against one of the great bottles to steady herself, and that the metal was very cold. "The ink was wrong. He found that. Anselm. He found the ink was wrong."

Corin looked at her for a long time.

"I lost a daughter," he said. "Tamsin. Did he tell you that? No. Why would he. She went down at fourteen to pay her share — they let them dive at fourteen, now, did you know? It used to be sixteen. They lowered it the year I was born. She went down at fourteen because the Interest put our family three years behind and the Bailiffs were talking about taking the shop, and she thought she could make it up, and she didn't come up again." He said it flatly, the way he might have read off a pressure gauge. "So when your Brother came down here and asked me about Sorrow Day, I told him. I told him everything my grandmother told me. And he went away and he did his sums. And a year later he came back and he sat where you're standing and he told me what they came to." He looked at her. "Do you know what they came to?"

"Zero," she said. "Year 280. By his figures."

"And by yours?"

"Three hundred and two."

He laughed. It was a short, terrible bark, half a cough. "You've done it yourself, then."

"Yes."

"And what are you going to do about it, Sister?"

She opened her mouth.

Out in the shop, the bell over the door rang.

* * *

Corin was on his feet before she had turned. He crossed the back room in two strides and put his eye to the crack in the curtain, and she saw his shoulders go rigid.

"Bailiffs," he said, very low. "Four. And Strand."

"Strand?"

"They're sweeping the spoke. They do it every few weeks, looking for circles. But Strand doesn't come on sweeps." He turned. His face was white under its grey. "He's looking for somebody."

"Me."

"Somebody saw you come down. Somebody's been watching you since you left that Nave." He was already moving — to the nearest of the great bottles, a full-draw tank on a wheeled cradle, dented and scarred. He put both hands to the cap and twisted, and it came away; it was not a cap but a lid, and the bottle was not a bottle. It was hollow. Empty. Dark inside, and big enough. "In."

"What—"

"Smuggler's tank. Pressure-rated on the outside, nothing on the inside. In, or they take you up the ladders and you go to the Breath like your Brother did." He held out his hand. "Now."

She climbed in. There was no other choice, and her body knew it before her mind did. The metal was freezing through her robe. She folded herself down into the dark, knees to chin, the satchel crushed against her chest, and looked up at Corin's face in the circle of light above her — and then the lid came down, and the light went out, and she heard it turn, and lock.

The dark was total. It smelled of old breath, struck matches and copper. She could hear her own heart. She could hear, through the metal, very faint and very far away, the shop door open and boots come in.

Voices. Corin's, raised, aggrieved: *—a licensed shop, I pay my tithe like anybody—* Another voice she didn't know. A crash, something falling, glass. Then a voice she did know, quiet and pleasant, that made her shut her eyes in the dark as though that would help.

"Mr. Asch. Good evening."

"Captain."

"We're looking for an auditor. A woman. Grey robe, about so high. Fair hair, worn up. She'd have come down on the sixth-bell shuttle with an inspection warrant." A pause. "She was seen on Spoke Twenty."

"I don't get many auditors."

"No? Brother Anselm Ferrow was seen on Spoke Twenty a number of times last year. On inspection."

"He bought seals. For his lamp."

"A great many seals," said Strand, "for one lamp." Footsteps. Coming nearer. Into the back room; she heard the curtain rings scrape. "What's in these?"

"Breath. Full-draw. Waiting on recertification."

"All of them?"

"All of them."

The footsteps came closer. They stopped. Something knocked against the side of her tank — a knuckle, or the butt of a stick — and the sound boomed in the dark around her like a bell, so loud that she bit through the inside of her cheek and tasted blood and did not make a sound.

"That sounds empty," said Strand.

"It's full," said Corin. "Breath's light. You know that. Your father was a diver."

There was a silence. It went on and on. In the dark Ysolde counted it, because she could not help counting, because counting was the only thing she had: eleven, twelve, thirteen, her blood loud in her ears, her mouth full of copper.

"So he was," said Strand at last, softly. "So he was." The footsteps moved away. "Take him."

"What? I haven't—"

There was a sound she did not want to understand, and then another, and then Corin Asch's voice saying something that was not a word. And then a lot of sounds, close together, for what felt like a very long time, and then boots, and the shop bell, and the door.

Silence.

She stayed in the tank. She did not know how long. Long enough for the cold to go all the way into her and stop hurting. Long enough to think, quite clearly and in order, that they had taken him, and that she did not know how to open the lid from inside, and that she might be in here until someone came to recertify the bottles, which might be never.

Then the lid turned, and scraped, and lifted, and there was light, and a face in it — Corin's face, with blood running from a cut over one eye and his lip split and swelling — looking down at her.

"They didn't take me," he said thickly. "They just wanted to be sure I knew they could." He held out a hand. "Up you come, Sister. They know you came. They don't know you're here. Next time they will."

* * *

She took the last shuttle up. She sat in the back of the cabin among the night-shift refinery hands with her satchel on her knees and her robe stained with the black grease of the inside of a smuggler's tank, and nobody looked at her, and she looked at nobody. Her cheek throbbed where she had bitten it. Corin's last words went round and round in her head like a wheel: *They know you came.*

The Nave was quiet at that hour. She walked the long ribbed corridors to the Third Chancery's dormitory wing without meeting anyone but a lamplighter, and turned the last corner to her own cell, and stopped.

There was a novice outside her door. A boy of eleven or twelve in the grey of the first year, with his hands folded in his sleeves and his eyes drooping, who had plainly been waiting a long time. He jerked upright when he saw her.

"Sister Marr?"

"Yes."

"I have a message, Sister. From the Prelate." He recited it carefully, the way novices recited everything. "The Prelate-Auditor sends his compliments to the keeper of the Ninth Ledger, and asks whether she would do him the kindness of dining with him tomorrow evening, at the eighth bell, in his rooms. He says—" the boy frowned, getting it right "—he says he has been wanting to talk to her about arithmetic."
