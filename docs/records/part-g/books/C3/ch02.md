# Chapter 2: The Ninth Ledger

*The Ledger · 2794*

The Count changed at the first bell, as it had every morning for three hundred and forty-one years, and Ysolde Marr was there to hear it.

She was always there. She had been told once, by a Chancery elder who meant it as a rebuke, that no one was required to attend the Turning, that it was a thing for pilgrims and novices and the sentimental, and that an auditor of the Third Chancery had better uses for the hour. She had agreed, and gone on coming. She could not have explained it in terms the elder would have accepted. It was something to do with the sound.

The Ledger Hall was the oldest enclosed space in the Nave, a long vault of grey metal ribbed like the inside of a whale, and at its far end, filling the wall from floor to ceiling, hung the Count Board. It was made of ten columns of flip-plates, each plate as tall as a man and painted with a single white numeral on black, and it had been built in the second century by people who no longer trusted screens. At the first bell, the night's reckonings having been carried up from all nine Chanceries and summed, the board turned. It began at the right, with the units, and moved left: a clattering rush like rain on a roof, plates falling over plates, the tens and the hundreds and the thousands spinning past their old values toward their new ones, slowing as they went left, until at last only the great digits at the top were moving — billions, hundreds of millions — and those only rarely, and those only by one.

This morning the clatter ran out in the tens of thousands. The big digits did not stir. The board read, when it was done:

**3,907,114,260**

There was a murmur from the pilgrims at the rail — Lungs folk mostly, divers in their grey coats with their tags on cords at their throats, come up the long ladders before their shift to watch the number fall by however much their sweat had made it fall. Some of them touched their tags. Some of them said the response, the novices' response, under their breath.

*What are we?*

*The owed.*

*What do we owe?*

*All that was spent to bring us.*

*Why is the Door shut?*

*Because the account is open.*

*When will it open?*

*When the Count is zero.*

Above the board, carved into the metal of the vault itself in letters a hand high, ran the words the First Auditor had written at the head of the first page of the first Count, three and a half centuries ago, in the Starving, when there was nothing in the world but hunger and arithmetic:

COUNT WHAT IS OWED AND NOT A MARK MORE

Ysolde read it every morning. Every morning it steadied her. There was, she had always thought, a kind of mercy in it that the pilgrims did not see — a promise not only that the debt would be paid but that it would be paid *exactly*, that no one would be asked for one mark more than was owed. That the Count, whatever else it was, was fair.

She went down to the Third Chancery to begin her day.

* * *

The Third Chancery kept the tithe of the Lungs. That was to say it kept the record of every hour of work done by every diver, every refinery hand, every scrubber-tender and hull-welder and ice-cutter of the old warrens on Hesper's surface, converted to marks at the posted rate and entered against the Holy Number, and it was, by volume, the largest of the nine. Ysolde had kept the Third's Seventh Ledger for four years. It was a good ledger, fat with diver families from the deep spokes, and she knew it the way she supposed some women knew the faces of their children: every column, every recurring error, every household that always tithed a little late and every household that always tithed a little early, as though afraid.

She was Lungs-born herself. It was not a secret. The Auditory took up perhaps a dozen children a year from the warrens — tested them at nine for numbers and memory and something the testers called *steadiness*, and took the ones who passed up the ladders into the Nave, to be robed in grey and taught to keep the Count. It was the only way up. It was, the Lungs said, half proudly and half not, the only tithe that took the child instead of the hours. Ysolde had been taken up at nine with a fever of excitement and a new pair of boots, and she had wept for six nights in the novice dormitory without making a sound, and on the seventh night she had stopped, because weeping did not balance.

That was twenty-two years ago. She sent a third of her stipend down to Piet every month. She went down to see him on feast days. She did not, as a rule, think about the rest.

She was at her desk with the Seventh Ledger open before her and her pen moving down a column of figures from Spoke Eleven when the whispering started.

It started at the doors and moved inward, desk to desk, the way the Turning moved left across the board. She saw heads go together. She saw old Brother Callum at the Fourth Ledger put down his pen and put a hand over his mouth. And then Sister Imke, who kept the Second, leaned across the aisle to her with her face gone the colour of paper and said: "Anselm. Brother Anselm. He went to the Breath last night."

Ysolde's pen stopped.

"What?"

"He was down in the Lungs. At the Number Two intake. Inspecting, they're saying — a discrepancy in the refinery tithe — and he went too near the gallery rail, and." Imke made a small falling gesture with one hand. "They found his lamp on the grating this morning."

"That's not possible."

"Ysolde—"

"He didn't go down to the Lungs. He hasn't gone down to the Lungs in ten years, his knees—" She stopped, because Imke was looking at her strangely, and because she had realised she did not, in fact, know where Brother Anselm Ferrow went or did not go. She knew he kept the Ninth Ledger. She knew he was sixty-eight and stooped and had a dry, whispering laugh like pages turning, and that he had been kind to her, in a distant way, for all her years in the Third; that when she had first come down from the novitiate he had taken one look at her figures and said *ah, a counter,* and lent her his own copy of Brenner's *Tables*, with his pencil notes in the margins, and never asked for it back. She knew he ate alone. She knew nothing.

The Bailiffs came at mid-morning.

There were four of them, in the black coats of the Prelate's household, and a fifth in black with the silver chain of a captain across his chest. Ysolde knew him by sight: Mattias Strand, a long-jawed man with pale, patient eyes, who was said to have been born in the Lungs himself and to have chosen the black over the grey. He did not speak to anyone. He went directly to the Ninth Ledger's desk at the end of the Chancery and stood over it while his men opened the drawers and lifted out what they found — pencils, a cup, a folded scarf, a stack of loose reckoning sheets, a little tin of the peppermints Anselm always had and never offered — and placed each thing in a canvas sack. The Ninth Ledger itself, the great book, lay closed on the desk in its grey binding. Strand opened it, turned a few pages, closed it again. He did not take it. A Ledger was the Auditory's, not a man's; it could not go in a sack.

Then the Prelate came, and the Chancery stood.

Aurel Quist was seventy-one years old and walked with a cane of black wood that he did not seem to need, and he wore the white of the Prelate-Auditor as though he had been born in it and found it a little tiresome. He was a small man. Ysolde was always surprised by how small. He had a broad, lined, humorous face, and white hair cut short as a novice's, and when he came into a room the room did not so much fall silent as lean toward him, the way plants lean toward a lamp.

"Sit," he said. "Please, sit. Callum, sit down, you'll do your back an injury. Thank you." He stood in the aisle with both hands folded on the head of the cane. "You've heard. I'm sorry you had to hear it in whispers; I'd have come sooner, but I was with the intake wardens. Brother Anselm was found at the Number Two gallery in the small hours. He'd gone down to look at a refinery discrepancy that had been bothering him — you know how he was — and the gallery there is old, and the rail is low, and the updraft from the intake is very strong." He paused. "It would have been quick. I want you to know that. The Breath is quick."

Nobody said anything. Down the aisle, Imke was crying, without fuss.

"He kept the Ninth for thirty-one years," said the Prelate. "Thirty-one years without an unbalanced Reckoning. I don't think there's a keeper in the Nave who can say that, and I can't say it myself. He'd have hated a speech. So I'll only say that I'll miss him, and that the Ninth will need a keeper, and that he told me some time ago who he thought it ought to be." He turned, and his eyes found her. "Sister Marr."

Ysolde rose, because her body did it for her.

"You'll take the Ninth," said Quist gently. "Hand the Seventh to Brother Tomas; he's ready for it. I know it's a burden on top of a grief. He spoke very highly of you." He smiled, a little sadly. "He said you were a counter. I gathered from his tone that it was the highest praise available."

"Yes, Prelate," she said. "Thank you, Prelate."

"Don't thank me," said Quist. "It's a lot of arithmetic."

He blessed them, the small brisk blessing of the seal hand, the right hand raised with the thumb folded in, and went out. Strand went with him, and the Bailiffs with their sack. And the Chancery sat in the hush they left behind, and then, because there was nothing else to do and because the Count did not stop for grief, the pens began again to move.

* * *

The Ninth Ledger kept the tithe of the deepest spokes of the Lungs — Seventeen through Twenty-two, the old breather warrens down near the Drowned Decks, where the diving families had lived for longer than anyone had kept records of living. It was the oldest of the Third's ledgers in continuous use. The book on Anselm's desk was its two hundred and sixth volume. The two hundred and five before it were shelved in the Chancery stacks behind her, going back in grey and then brown and then black bindings to the first decades after the Starving, when the ledgers had been kept on whatever could be written on: Combine plastic, ration-wrap, the backs of manuals.

Every page of every one of them was headed, in the keeper's own hand, with the Holy Number:

**HV-V-0001**

Ysolde had written it at the top of perhaps sixty thousand pages in her life. She had been taught, as every novice was taught, that it was the number of the account — the one account, the account of all of them, which was held by the Combine beyond the Door and kept for the Combine by the Stewards, and for the Stewards by the Auditors, and which would be closed when the Count was zero. She had been taught that the letters were the Combine's mark and the zeroes stood for the nothing that had been here before the Combine came, and the one for the people the Combine had brought. She had never questioned it any more than she questioned her own name.

She worked late. It was not unusual. The Ninth was in good order, as everything Anselm touched was in good order, but a ledger changing hands had to be read through and signed for, and she wanted, she found, to read it properly — to read it the way he would have read it. She sat at his desk, which still smelled faintly of peppermint, with the lamp drawn down low over the page and the Chancery empty and dark around her, and turned the pages one at a time.

His hand was small, upright and perfectly regular. Each entry was a household, a spoke, a number of hours, a rate, a sum. Each page totalled at its foot. Each total carried. It was as clean a ledger as she had ever seen.

And in the margins, very faint, in pencil, he had been doing something else.

It took her a long time to see it. The pencil was so light, and the marks so small — a single figure here, two there, tucked against the gutter of the page where the binding curved and the lamp did not reach. At first she took them for checks, the little tick-sums every keeper made to catch his own errors. But they did not match the entries beside them. They did not match anything on the page. They were running totals of something else, and they ran, she realised, turning back and back and back, not page to page but volume to volume. He had carried them over from the last book into this one. He had been carrying them for years.

She went to the stacks with the lamp, and took down the two hundred and fifth volume, and the two hundred and fourth. The same faint pencil in the gutters. The same running figure, growing.

Not growing. Shrinking.

She stood in the stacks with a book open in each hand and the lamp hooked over her wrist, and read the pencilled figures at the foot of the last page of each, and did not understand what she was looking at.

It was a balance. It had the shape of a balance. It was a sum that began somewhere and was being steadily reduced, page after page, by the very tithes the ledger recorded — as though Anselm had been keeping, alongside the Count, a second Count of his own. But it was not the Count. The Count was nearly four billion. This was a number in the low millions. And it was not falling by the tithes alone; it was falling faster, because every so often, at each year's Reckoning, where the Count leapt up by the Interest of Sorrow and the Lungs went into mourning, Anselm's pencilled figure did not leap. It went on falling. It ignored the Interest altogether, and added instead, at each Reckoning, a much smaller amount, marked with a tiny symbol she did not know — a little circle with a stroke through it — and the letters *F.T.*

She took the books back to the desk. She did not know how long she sat there with them. She did the arithmetic three times, the way Anselm had taught her to do it when she was twenty, on a scrap sheet that she would afterwards burn: his starting figure, his rate, the tithes. She could not check his starting figure; she did not know where it came from. She could not check his rate. But she could check that if you began where he began and charged what he charged, and credited every tithe the Lungs had paid, then the numbers did what he said they did. They fell. They had been falling for a very long time.

It was past the middle bell when she found the leaf.

She had closed the two hundred and sixth volume and was turning it to put it square on the desk, the way he had always left it, and she felt it shift in her hands: something loose inside the spine, where the boards met the binding. She opened the book flat and bent the cover back, gently, and there it was, a single sheet folded into eighths and slid into the hollow of the spine where no one opening the book to read it would ever see.

She unfolded it under the lamp.

It was a reckoning sheet, the plain ruled kind every Chancery used by the thousand, and it was covered on both sides in Anselm's small upright pencil. It was the same work she had just been tracing through the gutters of three books, but complete: a single column of numbers from top to bottom of one side and halfway down the other, one line for each year, from a year marked simply *Founding* down to the present. The starting figure at the head of the column had a note beside it: *per F.B.* The rate at each Reckoning was marked *F.T.* And the column ran down, year after year, smaller and smaller, until about two-thirds of the way down the second side it reached a line where Anselm had stopped writing numbers.

He had drawn a box around that line, instead. Inside the box, in the same small, steady hand, he had written:

*Zero. Year 280.*

And beneath the box, alone in the white space, one more line, written harder, as though he had pressed down:

*And since?*

Ysolde read it four times. She did the subtraction without wanting to. It was the three hundred and forty-first year of the Count. Sixty-one years. Sixty-one Reckonings since Year 280, sixty-one Sorrow Days, sixty-one times the great digits of the Count Board had clattered upward while the Lungs wept and touched their tags. Sixty-one years of every hour of every diver going down into the red and coming up again, or not coming up, to pay into an account that — if Anselm was right, if *per F.B.* meant anything, if *F.T.* meant anything — had been closed before her mother was born.

She did not believe it. She found that she was shaking, and she did not believe it, and the two things did not seem to have much to do with each other.

There was something else in the hollow of the spine. She had to tip the book to get it out. It fell onto the reckoning sheet with a small flat click.

A tag.

It was a diver's tag, the kind the Lungs folk wore on cords at their throats — a half-disc of dull grey steel with a broken, toothed edge, worn smooth by generations of hands, its old stampings long since rubbed into illegibility. Every diving family had one or two. They were heirlooms, luck charms, things your grandmother's grandmother had worn into the red and come back from; Piet wore one that had been their father's. No one knew where the custom came from. No one knew what the stampings had once said. You simply did not dive without a tag.

But someone had scratched this one. On its smooth face, with the point of a knife, someone had cut a circle. A plain round O, deep enough to catch the lamplight.

Ysolde knew what it meant. Every auditor knew. It was the sign of the Nullists — the heretics of the deep spokes, the ones who said the Count was a lie and the debt was void, who scratched their circles on the walls of the Lungs' corridors faster than the Bailiffs could grind them off. *Zero,* the circle said. *Nothing owed.* Two years ago a Nullist cell had been found in Spoke Nineteen, and the Prelate's court had sent four of them to the Breath, and the Count Board had been draped in black for a day, out of sorrow, the Chancery had been told, for the souls of the faithless.

Tucked through the hole in the tag where the cord should have been was a slip of paper rolled tight as a pin. She unrolled it with fingers that would not quite work.

*Corin Asch,* it said. *The Lungs, Spoke Twenty. Ask him about the Interest of Sorrow.*

Somewhere above her, through the metal of the Nave, she heard the middle bell begin to ring: the long slow strokes that meant half the night was gone and half remained. And under it — so faint that at first she thought she was imagining it — she heard boots in the corridor outside the Chancery doors. More than one pair. Not hurrying.

She folded the reckoning sheet into eighths again. She put the tag inside it and the slip of paper inside the tag. She opened the collar of her grey robe and slid the whole small bundle down against her breastbone, where the wool was tight, and buttoned the collar again, and drew the two hundred and sixth volume of the Ninth Ledger square on the desk, the way Anselm had always left it.

The doors opened. Lamplight, and the shape of a man in black with a silver chain across his chest.

"Sister Marr," said Bailiff-Captain Strand pleasantly. "Working late."

"The Ninth changes hands tomorrow, Captain. I wanted to read it through."

"Very diligent." He came down the aisle without hurrying, and stood at the end of the desk, and looked at the closed book, and then at her. His eyes were the palest she had ever seen, nearly colourless in the lamplight. "We missed some of the Brother's personal papers this morning, I think. Loose sheets. Notes. He was known to make a great many notes." He smiled. "You haven't come across any?"

"Only the Ledger, Captain," said Ysolde, "and it's in very good order."

He looked at her for what felt like a long time. Above them the bell went on ringing, stroke after slow stroke, and she counted them — she could not help it, she counted everything — and kept her hands still on the grey binding and her face as steady as the testers in the Lungs had once, long ago, written down that it was.

"I'm glad to hear it," said Strand at last. "Good night, Sister."

He went out. She heard the boots go away down the corridor, not hurrying.

She sat at Anselm's desk until the lamp burned low, with the dead man's sums lying against her heart like a second, colder skin, and in all that time she did not look up at the high Chancery window where, if you leaned, you could see across the gulf of the Ledger Hall to the Count Board, black and white and enormous, keeping its number in the dark.
