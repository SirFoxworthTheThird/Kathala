# Chapter 9: Kell Deep

*The Door · 3190*

Kell is the colour of a scab.

That's the first thing anyone says about it, and the last thing I'll say, because there's not much else to it from the outside. It's a moon of black rock about a quarter the size of Hesper, close in to Tarrow, and the giant's pull kneads it the way a baker kneads dough, so that it's hot inside — hot enough that the rock cracks, and the cracks glow, a dull rust-orange that never goes out. From space it looks like something that was burned and hasn't healed. There's no ice on Kell. There's no water. There's rock and heat and, down in the biggest crack of all, a chasm forty kilometres long that the first refinery-builders called the Deep, the Margrave's house and the Margrave's refinery and three hundred and twelve children, give or take whoever had died that week.

I'd been there more times than I could count. I'd stood on the gangways at the chasm's lip with a bag of blank tags on my shoulder and a line of children behind me holding hands. I'd never once gone in the back way.

"Here," said Wren. "This one."

She had her finger on the scope, on a blister of rock about sixty kilometres north of the Deep's lip where the surface was dark — not cracked, not glowing, just a dead grey dome with a black hole in the top of it like a pockmark.

"That's a vent?"

"It was. Number Forty. The Old Chimney." She was leaning over my shoulder in the cramped cockpit, so close I could smell the refinery on her still, under the soap from my boat's basin; it gets into the skin. "It's the oldest vent in the Deep. It runs all the way down from the surface to the dormitory galleries on Level Nine. It used to be a cooling stack, a hundred years ago, before they cut the new ones. Then the rock shifted and the bottom half went hot and they sealed it." She shrugged. "They sealed it from the top. They forgot about the bottom. There's a grating at the Level Nine end, behind the old latrines, that's been rusted through since before I came. We used to go up it on dares."

"How far up?"

"Eleven of us got to the top, in ten years. I was one." She held up her hands — those shiny ridged palms. "It's hot in the middle. You have to go fast."

"And down?"

"Down's easier," said Wren. "You just let go."

* * *

I put the *Mendicant* down in the shadow of a crater wall a kilometre from the Old Chimney, with her drive cold and her lights out and her hull pressed so close to the rock I heard it scrape. Haddie stayed with her. She'd wanted to come — she'd argued for the whole of the six-hour run from the rings, in that dry, furious, reasonable way she argued — and in the end it was Wren who'd said no. *You can't go fast,* Wren had said. *I'm sorry. It's the only thing that matters in there.* And Haddie had looked at her for a long moment and then nodded, and gone and sat down in the back of the cabin with the herald in her lap and a Count-book open on top of it, and not spoken to either of us again until we were suiting up.

Then she'd said, "Absalom."

"What?"

"How many will you bring out?"

"As many as fit."

"The boat's rated for six." She was looking at me over those wire spectacles. "Air for six. Seats for six."

"I know what she's rated for. I flew her."

"Then you know," said Haddie, "that you're going to have to choose." She looked down at her book again. "You did it before. For the other side. I suppose you'll remember how."

I didn't answer her. I sealed my helmet and went out.

* * *

Wren went up the side of the Old Chimney's dome like a spider. I went up after her slowly, the way a fifty-two-year-old man goes up a rock wall in a suit at a quarter of a gravity, which is to say badly and with a lot of swearing on the closed channel. At the top she was waiting for me, crouched on the lip of the hole with her helmet lamp off, looking down.

It was a shaft about a metre and a half across, its walls smooth and black and glassy where the heat had once run up them. It went straight down. My helmet lamp found the walls for twenty metres or so and then just found dark.

"How far?"

"Eleven hundred metres to the hot section. Then four hundred of hot. Then three hundred to the grating." She was clipping a line to an old iron staple driven into the lip. "There's staples all the way down. Every two metres. Somebody put them there when it was a stack. Use them, don't use the walls. The walls in the hot section will cook your suit."

"How hot?"

"You'll know," said Wren, and swung herself over the edge.

I'll not tell you much about the climb down. I don't like thinking about it. It took us nearly two hours, staple to staple to staple, my lamp swinging over the black glass and Wren's below me, a little bobbing point in the dark that got smaller and smaller and then stopped and waited for me to catch up, every time, without a word. About seven hundred metres down I started to feel it: a warmth coming through the soles of my boots and the palms of my gloves, faint at first, the way you feel the sun come round on a hull. By a thousand metres it wasn't faint. By eleven hundred my suit's alarms were singing, and the black glass of the walls had a dull red glow in it, not from my lamp but from inside, and I could hear the staples ticking under my hands as they expanded.

"Fast now," said Wren on the channel. "Don't stop. Don't stop for anything."

I didn't stop. I went down those four hundred metres hand over hand with the alarms screaming in my ears and the sweat running into my eyes inside the helmet, and the heat coming through the suit like a living thing, a hand on my back pressing, and at the bottom of it I was shaking so hard I could barely work the staples. And somewhere in the middle of it I understood, all at once and very clearly, that Wren had done this when she was eleven. On a dare. With her bare hands, probably; the tithed don't have suits. And that she'd done it ten more times after that, going up, for the sake of standing at the top of a dead vent on a dead moon and looking at the stars.

The last three hundred metres were cool. At the bottom there was a grating, rusted to lace, and beyond it a smell I'd know anywhere: hot rock, and latrines, and children.

* * *

The dormitory galleries of Kell Deep are on Level Nine, which is the lowest level the Margrave's people bother to heat with anything but the rock itself. They're long tunnels cut through the basalt, maybe four metres wide, and down both sides of each one are bunks — shelves, really, cut into the wall, three high, each with a felt blanket and a number painted over it. There's a lamp at each end of the gallery that never goes out. There's a reeve's post at the far end where a reeve sits all night and watches. There's no door. Where would they go?

There were sixty bunks in Wren's gallery. Forty-one of them had children in them. The rest were on shift.

I'd known it would be like that. I'd planned it with her, on the run in from the rings. The vents ran day and night, and a third of the children were always down in them, and the shifts changed at the twelfth hour and the fourth and the eighth. We'd come in at the half past three, when the shift going down was gone and the shift coming up wasn't back. The best we could do was forty-one.

And the boat was rated for six.

Wren went down the gallery like a ghost, touching shoulders, whispering. I stood in the shadow by the latrine grating with my helmet off under my arm, and watched them wake. They woke the way the tithed wake — all at once and silent, the way small animals wake, with their eyes open and their bodies still. They saw Wren and their faces changed. They saw me behind her and their faces changed again.

I knew some of them. I don't know why I hadn't expected that. A girl of about eighteen with a burned ear, sitting up in a top bunk near the lamp, staring at me — Kesse, Lanthorn twenty-two. A boy of seventeen with a squint — Odo, Sallow twenty-three. I'd held their hands on the gangways. They knew me. I watched them know me.

"It's all right," Wren was whispering. "It's all right, he's with me, he's — never mind what he is, he's with me. Get your boots. Don't talk. Kesse, get the little ones. Odo, get Mattie, he's in the end bunk, he sleeps like a stone."

"Wren," Kesse whispered. Her eyes were on me. "Wren, that's—"

"I know who he is."

"He's a *reeve*."

"He was a reeve," said Wren. "Now he's a boat. Get the little ones."

They got up. Thirty-odd of them, in their felt shifts and their bare feet with their boots in their hands, and they came down the gallery toward the grating in a silent shuffling crowd, the big ones carrying the small ones. Some of them couldn't have been more than nine. I counted them as they came — I couldn't help it, it was what I'd done for nine years, it was what my hands knew — and I got to thirty-three and stopped.

"There's more," I said to Wren, low. "There's eight still in the bunks."

"They can't come."

"Why not?"

"Because they're sick," said Wren flatly. "Or they're too small to climb. Or they're the ones who'd tell." She didn't look at me. "I've had ten years to think about this too, Rook. I know which ones can come."

So she'd chosen. And I hadn't had to. I'll not pretend I wasn't grateful.

"That's still thirty-three," I said. "The boat's rated for six."

"Then you'll have to fly carefully," said Wren, and turned away.

* * *

We had them at the grating, and the first of them climbing — Kesse first, because she knew the way, with a little girl clinging to her back like a monkey — when the reeve at the far end of the gallery woke up.

I hadn't seen him. That's the plain truth of it. The post at the gallery's end was in shadow past the lamp, and I'd seen a chair and a shape in it, and Wren had said *he sleeps*, and I'd believed her because she'd had ten years to know. But reeves get old and reeves get tired and sometimes reeves get up in the night to piss. I heard a scrape. I turned. And there was a man coming down the gallery between the bunks, a big grey-bearded man in a reeve's red coat with the white hand on the breast, with his trousers half-buttoned and a lamp held up in one fist, saying, "What the hell—"

He saw the children at the grating. He saw Wren. And then he saw me, and stopped dead, five metres off, with his lamp held up.

"Absalom?"

It was Garra. Garra Moss. He'd been a reeve when I was a reeve. We'd ridden the tithe runs together for six years. He'd been at my boy's burial. He'd stood beside me at the graveside on Lanthorn and put his big hand on my shoulder and said nothing, which was the right thing, and the only kind thing anybody said to me that day.

"Garra," I said.

"What are you—" His eyes went to the children. Back to me. I watched him understand. I watched his face go from bewilderment to something like grief. "Oh, Absalom. Oh, you bloody fool."

"Go back to your chair, Garra."

"I can't do that."

"Go back to your chair and sit down and go to sleep. You never saw anything. You never woke."

"*I can't do that,*" said Garra Moss. "You know I can't. You know what she does to a reeve who loses a gallery." He was backing up. His free hand had gone to his belt, where the alarm was. "Put them back, Absalom. Put them back in their bunks and go back up whatever hole you came down, and I'll give you an hour. For Jory's sake. I'll give you an hour before I—"

"Garra—"

His hand closed on the alarm.

I'd like to tell you I thought about it. I'd like to tell you I weighed it, the way Haddie weighs the Door: Garra's life on one side and thirty-three children on the other, and the sum came out the way it had to, and I did what the sum said. That would make me sound like a man who'd thought about something. I didn't think. I'd been a reeve for nine years and I'd been trained by Margrave Kask's own people, and my body went across those five metres before my mind had done anything at all, and my knife was in my hand and then it was in Garra, low, under the ribs, the way I'd been taught, and he made a sound like a man surprised by a stair that isn't there, and the lamp fell and broke on the floor.

He took a long time to go down. He held on to me. He was a big man. His face was close to mine and his eyes were open and he was trying to say something, and I held him up while he tried, the way you'd hold up a drunk friend, until he stopped.

Then I put him down on the floor between the bunks.

Behind me, very quietly, one of the small children was crying.

"Rook," said Wren.

I didn't turn round.

"Rook. The alarm. Did he—"

I looked at his belt. His hand was still on it. I took his fingers off it, one at a time. The little light on its face was dark.

"No," I said. "He didn't."

"Then come on."

I came on.

* * *

I'll tell you about Mattie, because if I'm telling the truth I have to tell all of it.

He was ten. He was Odo's friend from the end bunk who slept like a stone, a skinny little boy from the Kettles with a burned cheek and a cough, and he went up the Old Chimney behind Odo in the middle of the line, holding on to the staples the way Wren had shown them all at the bottom: *don't touch the walls, don't stop, don't stop for anything*. We'd made them strip off their felt shifts and wrap them round their hands, for the hot section. It wasn't enough. I knew it wasn't enough. I went up last, behind all of them, in my suit with its screaming alarms, and I could hear them above me on the channel Wren had rigged from her helmet speaker — the children, gasping and sobbing and climbing, and Wren's voice saying *go, go, go, don't stop* over and over like a prayer.

Mattie stopped.

I don't know why. Maybe his hands gave out. Maybe he looked down. He was a little above the middle of the hot section and I was about thirty metres below him, and I saw his small shape in my lamp stop moving, and cling, and then I heard him on the channel — not crying, just a small, high, surprised noise, like a bird — and he let go of the staple with one hand and put it against the wall. To rest. I suppose. Just to rest for a moment.

The wall in the hot section was hot enough to cook a suit.

He came down past me. I reached for him. I touched him — I touched his shoulder as he went by, I felt it, through my glove — and I didn't hold on.

I want to say that I couldn't have. That the weight would have pulled me off. That I'd have gone too, and then who would have flown the boat? I want to say all that, and it's probably true. But I've thought about it every night since, and I'll tell you the thing I actually remember, which is that my hand closed on air because I'd hesitated. Half a second. Because I'd thought: *that's one less.*

I've had a lot of thoughts I'm ashamed of. That's the one.

Wren went back down for him. When we were all at the top, all thirty-two of them, huddled on the lip of the dome in their bare feet and their burned hands with the stars over them — the first stars some of them had seen since they were nine — she clipped her line back onto the staple without a word and went back down the shaft. I didn't stop her. It took her an hour. When she came back up she had him over her shoulder, wrapped in her own coat, and she carried him across the kilometre of rock to the *Mendicant* with the rest of us, and she wouldn't let anybody help her, and she didn't say a word.

* * *

Thirty-two children, a girl of nineteen, an old woman of seventy-four and me, in a reeve's cutter rated for six.

I'll not describe it. You can imagine. They were in the cabin, the cargo bay, the lock, the cockpit footwell, three to a seat and two to a locker. The air went bad inside the first hour, and the scrubbers screamed, and I turned the heat down to nothing to spare them and the children shivered and pressed together for warmth and nobody complained. Not one of them. I've carried a lot of tithed children in my time, and I'd always thought the silence was because they were frightened of me. That night I understood it was just what they'd learned. You don't complain in the Deep. Nobody's listening.

Haddie moved among them in the dark with a water bulb, going from one to the next, letting each one take a mouthful. She didn't speak to me. When she got to the cockpit she held the bulb to Wren's lips, and Wren drank, and Haddie put her old bent hand on the top of Wren's cropped head for a moment, and left it there.

Mattie was in the cargo bay, wrapped in Wren's coat, between two crates.

"Where?" said Haddie, eventually. To me. "Where are you taking them?"

"Somewhere she won't look."

"There's nowhere she won't look."

"There's one place." I had the boat on a long, slow, dark arc away from Kell, curving out round the giant's limb, burning as little as I dared. "She won't look there because she doesn't think anybody's mad enough to go. It's dead. It's been dead for two hundred years. There's nothing left to pick. Everybody knows it's haunted." I glanced back at her. "I know a part that's still got air."

Haddie looked at me for a long moment. Then I saw her understand.

"The Hulk," she said.

* * *

The Hulk orbits Hesper, the way it always has, the way it did before there was a Lanthorn or a Margrave or anybody at all to remember what it was before it was the Hulk. It's the biggest made thing in the Tarrow system. It's a great dark chandelier of a station, spokes and rings and docks and towers, all dead, all black, half of it open to the vacuum and the other half sealed and cold and full of nothing. Pickers have been stripping it for two hundred years. There's nothing left in it worth the fuel — no metal you can easily cut, no machine that still turns. There's paper, in some of the deep rooms, rotting in heaps. There's the old hand written on everything. There are, if you believe the pickers, voices in the dark, and lights where no lights should be, and once in a while a picker who goes in and doesn't come out.

There's also a room I found when I was thirty, picking alone in the deep core where nobody goes, and patched myself, and put a scrubber in, and never told a soul about. I used to go there, after Jory died. I used to go there and sit.

I brought the *Mendicant* in to a dead dock in the core, and we got the children out and through the lock and down a long cold corridor in the dark, and I opened the last door, and turned on the lamps I'd strung up years ago, and stood back.

The children stopped in the doorway, all together, and stared.

It's a hall. That's all I've ever been able to call it. A long, high, curving vault, ribbed with grey metal like the inside of a great animal, so big that the lamps only light the near half of it and the far end goes off into the dark. There are railings along one side, worn smooth. There are rows of something that might have been benches. And at the far end, where the lamplight just reaches, the whole wall is a heap of fallen plates: hundreds of them, each as tall as a man, black with white marks painted on them, tumbled over each other in a great slope like a rockfall, where whatever they'd once hung on had given way.

The marks on them are numbers. The old numbers, the ones Haddie can read. A one, a seven, a nought, a four. Jumbled, upside down, sideways, half-buried. They don't make anything. They're just there.

"What was it?" whispered Kesse.

"I don't know," I said. "Nobody does."

Haddie had gone past me, into the hall. She was standing in the middle of it with her hand-lamp up, turning slowly, looking up at the vault. Her face had a strange expression on it.

"There's writing," she said.

I looked up. I'd never noticed. I'd sat in that hall a hundred nights and never once looked up. But there it was, high on the curve of the vault above the heap of fallen plates, cut into the metal itself in square letters a hand high, so worn and dark you'd never see it unless you held a lamp up and looked for it. Half of it was gone, where a section of the vault had buckled. Half was still there.

Wren had come up beside Haddie. She was still in her suit. Her face was grey with exhaustion and smeared with soot and with something else I didn't want to think about, and her eyes were on the letters.

"Can you read it?" said Haddie softly. "Go on. You know the letters now. You've been learning them for three days. Try."

Wren looked up at the vault. Her lips moved.

"*Count,*" she said slowly. "*Count. What. Is.*" She stopped. Frowned. "*Owed.*"

"Go on."

"*And not.*" Her voice cracked. "*A mark. More.*"

She stood under it with her burned hands at her sides and the dead boy's blood on her suit, and said it again, to herself, the whole thing, slowly, as if it were a sum she was checking. *Count what is owed and not a mark more.* And then she sat down, very suddenly, on the cold metal floor of the hall, among the children, and put her face in her hands, and for the first time since I'd pulled her out of that skiff, she wept.

* * *

Haddie found me later, at the dock, where I'd gone to check the *Mendicant*'s scrubbers and to be somewhere the children weren't. She'd brought the herald. She'd brought her glass, too — the little portable scope she carried in her coat — and she'd set it up at the dock's cracked port, pointed out past Hesper's limb at the place where the Door hung.

"Look," she said.

I looked. I didn't know what I was meant to see. It looked the way it always looked: a smudge of wrong stars.

"I measured it at the seventh hour," said Haddie, "on the way in from Kell. And again just now. Five hours apart." She took her spectacles off. Her hands were shaking. "It's moved, Absalom. In five hours. As much as it used to move in a year."

"That's not possible."

"No."

"Why would it—"

"It's like something's pushing," said Haddie. "From the other side."

Between us, on the dock floor in its blanket, the herald stopped humming.

We both looked down at it. It had been speaking its notice every hour or so, the same pleasant calm words in the same order, for four days. I'd stopped hearing it. Now it was silent, and the silence went on longer than it ever had before. Long enough that I'd started to think it had died.

Then it spoke. But not the notice. Something else. Something short — a dozen words, no more — in that same patient voice, once, and then again, and then it went back to its humming.

Haddie had gone very still.

"Well?" I said.

She didn't answer for a moment. Then she put her spectacles back on, and looked at the herald, and then out through the port at the Door, and when she spoke her voice was barely above a whisper.

"*Passage at threshold,*" she said. "*Receiver attending.*"
