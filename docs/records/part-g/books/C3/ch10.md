# Chapter 10: Inversion

*The Bond · 2431*

The Number Four head went dark at first shift on the eighteenth day, and the silence was the loudest thing Maren had ever heard.

She had never heard the head silent. Nobody had. The intakes had roared since before she was born — the great exhale of the separators drawing breath off the bottles, the hiss of the condensers, the deep thudding pulse of the pumps that pushed it all up the long pipe through the ice to the tanks at High. It was the Sump's heartbeat. You stopped hearing it at about three years old. And at first shift on the eighteenth day the refinery hands on the separator floor reached up, one after another, and threw the bank switches, and the roar went down through every note it had and stopped; and in the silence that came after, four hundred people standing in the domed cavern of the head heard their own breathing, and the drip of the ice, and somewhere far up in the dark of the roof a single bird-like creak of cooling metal; and nobody said anything at all.

Then Ruth Calloway, standing on a crate by the separator base with her arms folded, said in her enormous voice, "Well. That's that, then," and somebody laughed, and the laugh went round the cavern like a wave.

Maren was on the breathers' rack. She had been there since before first shift, with the *Gannet* racked and cold behind her and her suit hung on its hook, and as the breathers came in for their dives — Farrant, Moss, the Ellery sisters, a hundred and forty of them from all four heads — she had met each one at the rack gate and said the same thing. *We're not diving.* Some had known already. Some hadn't. A few had argued. One, a young breather from Number One head named Corley, had stood in front of her for a long time with his helmet in his hands and tears running down his face and said, *I've got a ward too, Tull, she's on the list, I can't, they'll put it on the bond,* and Maren had said, *I know. I've got one too. Rack your bottles,* and after a long moment he had.

Siggard came at the second bell. He walked down the empty weighing line with his soft careful hands at his sides and his slate tucked under his arm, and stood at the breathers' rack gate in front of her, and looked at her for a while without speaking.

"Tull," he said at last.

"Tally-master."

"You'll want to dive."

"No."

"It's going on the bond. All of this. Every hour the heads are dark is product not delivered. You understand that? They'll charge it. Every hour, to every bond in the Sump, at full valuation."

"Let them."

He looked at her. And then, to her astonishment, he sighed — a long, tired, human sigh — and took his slate from under his arm and put it face down on the rail.

"My father was a breather," he said. "Did you know that? Number Two head. He went down in a sink in the ninety-eighth year. They charged my mother for the skimmer." He picked up the slate again, and tucked it back under his arm, and turned away. "Count what's owed, Tull."

"And not a mark more," she said, to his back.

* * *

The Sump sent its terms up to High at the third bell, over the head's link to the Factor's office. Hatch spoke them herself, standing at the old comm board in the tally master's office with Maren and Pym and Ade Okoro behind her and her hands flat on the board's edge. She did not raise her voice.

"This is Agathe Hatch, for the bonded community of Vigil. Every refinery head in the Sump is shut. No breath will come up until the wards on the Scholarship list are returned to their parents, every one of them, in the Sump, before the *Grace* departs. We know about Schedule C. We know about Brannock. We know about the Clause. That's all. We'll be here."

She let go of the board.

There was no reply. They waited an hour, and then two. Hatch went back to the clinic. The bonds in the head sat down on the floor of the cavern, and on the separator housings, and on the empty weighing line, and somebody brought food from the hydroponics on Five and passed it round, and somebody else began to sing, and for a while — Maren would remember this afterward, and hold on to it — for a while it was almost cheerful. Four hundred people in the cold, with the machines quiet and nothing to do but wait, singing the breathers' hymn in a dozen keys while the old ones corrected the young ones on the words.

*Down in the red where the weight comes on,*
*down where the light's a bruise,*
*bring up the breath for the ones who are owed,*
*and bring yourself up if you choose.*

The reply came at the sixth bell. It was not a voice.

It was the fans.

Maren did not notice at first. Nobody did. The circulation fans in the Sump ran all the time, a hum even more constant than the head's roar, so constant that you did not hear it even as a child. It was only when someone near her — one of the Ellery sisters — said, *Is it cold?* and someone else said, *It's always cold,* and the first one said, *No, listen,* that they all stopped singing and listened, and heard what was not there.

The fans had stopped. Not here — the head had its own plant — but everywhere else. Up through the ice from the levels below, from Seven and Eight and Nine, where the poorest of the Sump lived and the clinic was, came a silence that should have been a hum.

"They've cut the air," said Ade Okoro.

Nobody answered him.

"They've cut the air on the bottom levels." He was on his feet. "That's where the old ones are. That's where the clinic is. That's—"

"That's where they'll do it first," said Maren. "Where it'll hurt most."

* * *

She went down to the clinic at the eighth bell, because there was nothing she could do at the head and because she could not stand to be there, and she found it already full.

Hatch had moved every cot together at the near end, by the door, where the air from the corridor still moved a little, and had put two people in every cot and more on the floor between them. They were the old and the very young and the sick: the rattle cases, mostly, breathers and ex-breathers whose lungs were already half-full of whatever Tarrow had put in them, who could manage in the Sump's thin air on an ordinary day and could not manage now. The room was very cold. The light was very dim — Hatch had turned the lamps down, to save power, though there was no reason to think the Combine would cut power too, or perhaps every reason. And the sound in it was the sound Maren had last heard nineteen years ago in this same room, sitting by her mother's bed: a wet, slow, clicking labour, many times over, like a room full of clocks running down.

Hatch was kneeling by a cot near the curtain. She had a mask in her hand, connected by a hose to a small grey cylinder on the floor, and she was holding it to the face of a boy of about fifteen — the same boy, Maren realised, who had been on the far cot the night she first came down here with the paper. His chest was heaving. His lips were the colour of slate.

"How long?" said Maren.

"For him? I don't know. I've got eleven cylinders of oxygen and forty people who need them." Hatch did not look up. "For the rest of us, on Nine? Without the fans the carbon dioxide builds. Two days before it's dangerous for the healthy ones. Three before it starts killing them. For this lot—" she glanced along the cots "—tonight. Some of them, tonight."

"Ruth's crews are trying to hotwire the local scrubbers on Seven."

"Ruth's crews are very good," said Hatch, "and the local scrubbers on Seven haven't worked since the hundred and sixtieth year." She moved the mask, gently, and the boy's chest heaved and caught and heaved. "It's a contest, Maren. I told you. Who can wait longest. I didn't think they'd start with the children." She was quiet a moment. "I should have."

Maren knelt down on the other side of the cot. The boy's eyes were open, very wide, fixed on the ceiling. She took his hand. It was cold and wet and it closed on hers so hard it hurt.

"What's his name?"

"Teo Brand. Number Three head. His mother's a breather. She's up at the head with you." Hatch looked at her across the cot. "She doesn't know he's down here. I sent her up there myself this morning. I told her he'd be fine."

* * *

Teo Brand did not die that night, though four others did. Two of them were in the clinic, old men with the rattle who went in the small hours, one after the other, without making much noise about it. One was a baby on Level Eight whose mother brought her to the clinic door at the second bell of the morning, already gone, and stood in the doorway holding her, and would not come in, and would not go. And one was a man on Level Seven, a scrubber-tender on Ruth Calloway's crew, who had crawled into the old local plant with a lamp and a cutting bar to try to make it run and been found at dawn with the lamp still burning beside him, in the airless dark at the plant's heart where the carbon dioxide had pooled, like water in a sump.

By the morning of the nineteenth day there were people in the Number Four head who wanted to go up and fight.

Farrant was the loudest. He stood on the separator platform where the Factor had stood ten days before and shouted it at the crowd: *They're killing us. They're killing our parents and our babies in the dark. Are we going to sit here on the floor and sing while they do it?* And there was a roar back at him, a real roar, ugly and frightened, and Maren saw people she had known all her life with their fists up and their mouths open. She saw Corley, the young breather from Number One, among them. She saw a woman with a cutting bar.

Hatch came up from Nine at the third bell and stood on a crate by the separator base and waited for them to stop, the way she always did. It took longer than it ever had. When they did, she said only, very quietly:

"There are three hundred and forty marines on Hesper High. They are waiting for you to come up. They're *hoping* for it. And when you do, they will kill you in the shuttle locks, every one of you, and then they won't have to cut the air any more, will they? There'll be nobody left to strike." She looked at Farrant. "Is that what you want? To save them the bother?"

Farrant said nothing. But he did not get down.

* * *

The Combine's answer, the real one, came down at the fifth bell on the nineteenth day, on a single shuttle under guard, and it came with Lucan Vey in it.

He was the clerk. Maren saw him as the shuttle's party came through the gallery lock into the head — eight marines, a grey-coated woman Maren did not know, and behind her, carrying her slates, with his narrow face as blank as a wiped board, Lucan. The woman was Deputy Factor Annis Vole. She was perhaps fifty, small and plump and pleasant, with the manner of someone who negotiated for a living and was very good at it. She walked into the domed cavern of the silent head between her marines as though she were walking into a dinner, and smiled at the four hundred bonds sitting on its floor, and asked, in a clear carrying voice, for Sister Agathe Hatch.

They met in the tally master's office: Vole and Lucan on one side of Siggard's desk, Hatch and Pym and Ade Okoro and Maren on the other, and marines at the door. Vole did not sit. She laid out the Combine's terms standing, briskly, as if reading a menu.

The strike would end. The heads would reopen at first shift tomorrow and remain open until the convoy's departure. In exchange, air circulation would be restored to the lower levels within the hour, and no bond would be charged for the hours lost. No disciplinary action would be taken against any individual.

"And the wards," said Hatch.

"The Scholarship stands," said Vole pleasantly. "That isn't negotiable, I'm afraid. The contracts are committed. But the Factor is very conscious of the distress this has caused the families, and he wishes to make a gesture." She glanced at Lucan, who lifted a slate and read from it, in a flat clerk's voice that did not sound like his own.

"The Factor will permit the families of Scholarship wards to attend a farewell on Hesper High, at the convoy docks, before embarkation. Parents will be brought up by shuttle. They will be permitted to see their children in person, without the Nursery glass, for one hour, before the wards are embarked on the *Grace*."

There was a silence in the office.

"Without the glass," said Ade Okoro.

"Without the glass," said Vole. "It's a great kindness, I think. It's never been done." She smiled. "Of course, it will need to be soon. I should tell you that the convoy schedule has been revised."

Maren's hands, under the desk, closed on her knees.

"Revised how?" said Hatch.

"The *Grace* departs in four days," said Deputy Factor Vole. "On the twenty-third. The farewell will be on the twenty-second. That gives the families time to prepare." She tilted her head. "The Combine thanks you for your patience."

* * *

Hatch asked for an hour to put the terms to the Sump. Vole granted it graciously and sat down, at last, in Siggard's chair, and asked whether there might be anything hot to drink. And in the confusion of the bonds leaving the office and the marines shifting at the door, Lucan Vey picked up one of his slates and said to no one in particular that he needed the latrine, and went out, and Maren gave it a count of twenty and followed.

The latrine for the tally house was a cold cell cut into the ice off the back corridor. He was standing in the middle of it with his back to the door, and when she came in he turned round and she saw that the blank board of his face had cracked straight across.

"I've got two minutes," he said. "Maybe less. Listen to me."

"Four days," she said. "You didn't tell me."

"I didn't know. It was decided last night. The strike frightened them — not because of the breath, because of what it meant, that you knew. If you knew about Schedule C you might know about everything. So they're going now. They've got enough breath in the tanks for the crossing. They'll sell less at Corvid. The board will be unhappy and the Factor will explain." He was talking very fast. "Your strike is over, Maren. It was over when they decided. There's nothing you can stop them doing by not diving, because they don't need you to dive any more."

"Then why the terms? Why the farewell?"

"Because they need the heads open for four days to fill the last tanks, and because it's cheaper than killing you all, and because—" He stopped. "Because the Factor wants the union in one place. On High. Where he can see it." He looked at her. "I don't know that. I'm guessing. I'm not in those meetings."

"And the air?"

"They'll turn it back on. They don't need you dead. Not yet." He put a hand over his face, and took it away. "That's not why I came. Listen. Listen to me. I found out something else."

"What?"

"The key," said Lucan. "The Gallows key. I told you the Factor holds it until the account is closed. I didn't know what that meant. Nobody in the office did; it's a phrase from the Clause, it's in the sealed annex. I got into the annex last night." He took a breath. "The *Grace* isn't going straight through. She stops at Gallows. The station crew come aboard — there's six of them, they're on the list for Schedule A, staff. And then the Factor goes to the master console, and puts in the key, and keys the pins to invert."

"Invert," said Maren.

"Run backward. The pins hold the Throat open by pushing on it. Invert them and they pull. All four together, on a timer — he sets it, and the *Grace* goes through, and two hours after she's clear, the pins invert, and the Throat collapses." His voice had gone very quiet. "Not narrows. Not eighteen months. Collapses. To a thread. In about ten minutes. Nothing could ever get through it again — not a ship, not a signal, not anything. Not for centuries. Maybe not ever."

"Why?"

"Because the Clause says so. *Upon withdrawal, the passage shall be closed to prevent fixed assets from following.*" He laughed, horribly. "That's you. Fixed assets. They're afraid you'll follow them. They're afraid somebody in the Sump will build a ship out of scrap and bring thirty thousand angry bonds through the Throat in five years' time and turn up at Corvid asking for their children back. So they're going to shut the door. And then they're going to put Vigil on the books as abandoned, and file the lien, and leave a receiver at the far mouth in case it ever opens, and forget you." He was trembling. "Eighteen months was a lie. It was for the board, it was for us, it was so the staff wouldn't ask questions. There's no eighteen months. There's no *maybe*. There's two hours after the *Grace* clears the mouth, and then you're shut in. Forever. With the twelve of us."

The latrine was very cold. Maren could hear water dripping somewhere in the ice, and the faint far-off voices of the bonds in the head.

"The key does that," she said.

"The key does that."

"Only the key."

"Only the key, at the master console, at Gallows." He looked at her. "Why?"

She did not answer for a long moment. She was thinking about the *Gannet* in a downdraft, falling, and the yellow lever under its hinged guard. She was thinking that every lever in the world worked both ways.

"If the pins can shut it behind them," said Maren slowly, "they can shut it in front of them."

Lucan Vey went white. Not pale; white, as though every drop of blood in his narrow face had gone somewhere else all at once. He stared at her. His mouth opened, and closed. And in the silence between them she heard the boots of a marine in the back corridor, coming to see what was keeping the clerk.

"I have to go," he whispered.

He went. He did not say anything else. He did not need to. She had seen his face.

* * *

Hatch put the terms to the Sump at the sixth bell, standing on her crate, and the Sump took them. Not happily. Not all of them. But the fans on Nine had been off for thirty hours and there were nine dead now, and the farewell — *without the glass, for one hour* — went through the four hundred bonds in the head like warm water, and Maren saw it go, saw it soften faces that had been fists. Lin Okoro was weeping again. Even Farrant got down off the platform.

They met on the water that night for the last time, the fourteen of them, on the iron platform in the middle of the Drowned Decks, and Hatch told them what she meant to do.

"The farewell's on the docks at High," she said. "Four hundred parents, four hundred children, in one place, with no glass between them. For an hour." She looked round at them in the lamplight. "Farrant's right. We can't take the *Grace*. But we don't need to take the *Grace*. We need to take the children."

They stared at her.

"The ward shuttles bring us up," said Hatch. "The ward shuttles take us down. Six of them, at the dock locks, waiting. The marines will be on the docks, but they'll be watching the parents, not the children; they won't think we'd dare, with the kids right there in the crossfire. So at the end of the hour, when they call for the embarkation—" she held up a hand and closed it "—every parent takes their child by the hand and walks into the nearest shuttle. Not the *Grace*. The shuttle. And Ruth's people and Ade's people, who'll be on the shuttle crews because we'll make sure of it, seal the locks and drop. And we bring them home."

"And then?" said Ruth Calloway. "They'll come down after us."

"Then they'll come down after us," said Hatch, "and we'll have four hundred children in the ice, and they'll have four days. They can come down and try to dig them out of the Sump one at a time, with the heads shut again and the whole Sump between them and the kids. Or they can leave on schedule, without their liquid assets." She smiled her thin smile. "I think they'll leave. I think they'll do the sum."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"It's mad," said Farrant at last, almost reverently.

"Yes."

"It might work."

"It might," said Hatch. "Or they'll shoot us on the docks. I'll be honest with you; I don't know which. But I know that if we let them put those children on the *Grace*, we'll never see them again." She looked round at them. "Hands."

The hands went up. Maren's went up.

At the edge of the platform, Dov's went up too, slowly, last.

* * *

She found him afterward on the *Old Patience*, because she knew he would be there. The tender was docked in her cradle at the Number Four head's upper lock, cold and dark, her engines banked since the strike began; and Dov was in her cockpit with a hand-lamp on the console and a tally slip on his knee and a pencil, and a bottle of Ostrander's beside him that he had not opened.

"You weren't at the bar," she said, climbing in.

"I'm on strike." He did not look up. "Sit down. You're letting the cold in."

She sat in the co-seat. The cockpit of the *Patience* smelled of thirty years of Dov: oil, sweat, Ostrander's, the struck-match tang of breath. Above the console, taped to the glass, was a faded picture of a woman with black coarse hair and Maren's own jaw, in a breather's suit, laughing at whoever was holding the camera.

"Four days," said Dov.

"Four days."

"I heard." He wrote something on the slip. "And Hatch's plan. The shuttles."

"Yes."

"It's mad."

"Everybody keeps saying that."

"It's mad and it might work." He wrote again. "And if it doesn't."

She looked at him.

"I've known you thirty-eight years, girl," said Dov. "I held the light. You've had a look on your face since you came back up from that latrine this afternoon that I've only ever seen on one other person, and she's on my window." He nodded at the picture. "Your mother used to get it before a deep seam. When she'd already decided and was just waiting to tell me. So tell me."

Maren was quiet for a long time.

"How long to Gallows?" she said.

He put the pencil down.

"From here?"

"From here."

He didn't answer at once. He picked the pencil up again and looked at the slip on his knee, and she saw, upside down, that there were already figures on it — that he had been working something out before she came in, and that the something was this.

"The *Grace* at convoy speed, with escort," he said, "four days. She's heavy, she's got the stockpile, she'll be careful. The *Patience*, light, at a steady burn — six. She's not fast. She was never fast." He tapped the slip. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless you take her down the limb." He looked at her. "You've dived Tarrow your whole life. You know what it does when you go deep — how the giant grabs you. Take the *Patience* down past the cloud tops, on the right line, at the right speed, and light everything at the bottom of the fall, and the giant throws you. Like a stone from a sling. Your mother and I did it once, on a dare, half-loaded. Took a day and a half off a run to the Number Three relay." He shrugged. "Nearly killed us. The hull's never been right since."

"How fast?"

"Get it right, three and a half days. Get it wrong, you're a smear on the red." He turned the slip over. "Three and a half days, against the *Grace*'s four. And then you're at Gallows with half a day in hand, and an escort frigate sitting on the mouth that'll want to know what you're doing there, and pickets all round the station that'll burn anything that hasn't got a Combine code." He looked at her. "And then what? You haven't got the key."

"I know."

"Without the key, the console's just a box."

"I know."

"You'd shut thirty thousand people in a room with no door," said Dov quietly. "Forever. For one girl."

"They're already shut in. They just don't know it yet." She held his eye. "And it's not one. It's four hundred and twelve."

He looked at her for a very long time in the lamplight, with the picture of her mother laughing on the glass above his head. Then he reached for the bottle of Ostrander's, and turned it in his hands, and put it down again unopened.

"You're mad," he said.

"Will you work it out? The limb. The line and the speed. In case."

"In case." He picked up the pencil. "In case." He was writing again, fast, figures she could not follow. Without looking up, he said, "When's the farewell? Exactly. What bell?"

"First bell on the twenty-second. Why?"

"Need to know when I'll have to have her fuelled," said Dov Arkwright, and went on writing.
