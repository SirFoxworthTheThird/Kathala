# Chapter 20: The Seal

*The Ledger · 2794*

The corridor ran both ways from the docking ring, along the spine, into the dark. Ysolde stood in the frost with her lamp up and looked left and then right, and then, because she had to choose and there was nothing to choose by, she went right, toward the station's centre, because that was where Corin had said the docking ring was nearest to, and a station's heart, she supposed, would be in its middle.

It was very cold. Corin's suit was too big for her and the cold came in at the joints, at the wrists and the knees, and her breath fogged the inside of the visor at the edges in a ring of white. The frost on the walls was thick and soft and feathered, like the frost on the inside of the novice dormitory's window on her first night in the Nave, and where her lamp touched it it glittered. There was nothing in the corridor. Doors stood open on bare rooms. There were no bodies, no wreckage, nothing to say what had been done here — only the frost, and the dark, and the cold, and far down the spine behind her, in the direction she had not taken, something she glimpsed once when she turned her lamp that way: a ragged place where the corridor ended in torn black metal and beyond it, nothing. Stars. An old wound, open to the dark.

She turned away from it and went on.

The corridor ended in a round room.

She knew it was the place before she had properly seen it. The door stood open. The lamp found a curved wall, and in the curved wall a window — huge, floor to ceiling, so thick with frost that it was white, a great pale screen of feathered ice — and through the frost, faint and grey, a light that was not light: the Door. Its bent arcs of stars. Its nothing at the centre. And under the window, a long desk of grey metal with dark screens set into it. And at the desk, in a chair, with its back to her and its face to the window, someone sitting.

She stopped in the doorway.

The figure did not move. Of course it did not move. It was in a suit — a heavy, old-fashioned suit, much patched, of a kind she had seen only in the oldest diving families' cells in the Lungs, hung on walls as relics — and a helmet, and it sat in the plain metal chair quite straight, with one gloved hand flat on the console in front of it and the other in its lap. Frost had grown over it. Over the shoulders and the helmet and the arm on the console, the same feathered white as the walls, so that it seemed at first to be made of frost — a figure of ice someone had carved and set at the desk and left.

She went to it. She did not know how. Her feet took her across the round room the way they had taken her to Piet's door on Spoke Twelve.

She stood beside the chair and held up her lamp and looked through the visor.

It was a woman. The cold and the dark had kept her. Her face was thin and dark and drawn, the skin gone tight over the bones, but it was a face — a woman of perhaps forty, with coarse black hair cut short and badly, and a long jaw, and eyes that were closed. Her mouth was a little open, as though she had been about to say something. There were lines on her face that Ysolde had seen on the faces of every diver in the Lungs: round the eyes, from squinting into the red; at the corners of the mouth, from clenching the teeth against the weight. On the breast of the suit, half under the frost, stencilled in faded white, letters and a number. Ysolde brushed the frost away with her glove.

TULL — H-11873

She was a diver.

Ysolde knew it the way she would have known her own brother's hands. A diver from the Lungs, three hundred and forty years ago, in her diving suit, sitting in this chair at the edge of the world in front of a frosted window. Her right hand, the one on the console, was strapped under the glove — Ysolde could see the bulk of a binding, a splint, as if it had been broken. It lay flat on the grey metal beside a small round lock, and in the lock, set flush, was something white and steel: a spindle, the length of a finger, with a spiral groove.

Her other hand lay in her lap. Something was in it. A cord, and on the cord, half under the frost of the glove that held it, a piece of dull steel.

Ysolde knelt. She brushed the frost away. She did not need to lift it to see what it was; she had seen it every day of her childhood at her father's throat and every day since at Piet's, and three nights ago in the dark of the Reliquary hanging from the arm of a plain metal chair. A half-disc of steel, the size of a palm, with a broken toothed edge.

But this one had not been worn smooth. Its stampings were still there. Sharp and clean, as clean as the day they were cut.

H-11873. SURETY.

* * *

The console was dead. She had thought it would be. Three hundred and forty years in the cold and the dark; there was nothing in it to wake. She went back down the frosted corridor to the pup and found the cable Corin had shown her, the umbilical that the jolly-boat used to feed power to the far relays when their own plants failed, and dragged it up the spine behind her, metre after metre, in the too-big suit, with her breath roaring in the helmet. It reached. Just. She found a socket in the console's flank by touch, the old square Combine shape, and fitted the cable's end into it, and went back to the pup and opened its plant to the line.

When she came back the screens were alight.

Not all of them. Three, of nine. Green and amber, flickering, with lines of the old square hand scrolling across them faster than she could read. And then they steadied, and on the central screen a single plain panel came up, and held.

She stood in front of it. She read the words she knew and guessed the ones she did not. *Pin.* That was in the oldest volumes of the Ninth, she remembered now — a charge, once, at the very beginning, before the Starving, for something called *pin operations*. *Collapsed.* *Inverted.* And *passage*, which was on the first page of the First Book: *Consolidated Statement at Closure of Passage.*

The Door. This was the Door. This desk, this frosted window, this lock with its white-and-steel spindle. Whatever had been done to the Door, it had been done from here, by someone sitting in this chair.

She looked at the woman in the chair.

Then she put her finger on the last line of the panel. LOG.

* * *

The voice was very close. That was the first thing. It came from a small grille in the console to the left of the lock, and the grille was so near the woman's helmet that for a moment Ysolde, kneeling there with her lamp, had the terrible conviction that it was coming *from* her — that the dead mouth behind the frosted visor had opened and spoken.

"*Pell,*" said the voice. "*It's Mum.*"

It was a woman's voice, low and rough. It spoke the old tongue — the tongue of the First Book, of the oldest volumes of the Ninth, of the Compact — but spoken, not written, and Ysolde had only ever heard it read aloud by Chancery elders who had learned it from books and pronounced it as they thought it should be pronounced. This was not that. This was someone for whom it was simply speech. The vowels were wrong, flattened; the words ran together; it was like hearing the catechism sung in an accent from a spoke she had never visited. But she understood it. She had read every page of the First Book's tongue she could find for twenty-two years. She understood every word.

She knelt on the frosted floor beside the dead woman's chair, and listened.

She heard about the Scholarship. She heard about a place called Brannock, and galleries in a heavy world, and children whose bones were soft enough to take the weight. She heard the voice say: *When the* Grace *went through, they were going to stop here, at this station, and turn this key, and shut the Throat behind them so the rest of us couldn't ever follow. Thirty thousand people.*

Thirty thousand. *There were thirty thousand people in the ice of Hesper and twelve Stewards in the Nave.*

She heard: *So I've shut it first.*

Ysolde put her hand over her visor, as if to cover her mouth, and it was not there.

*I've shut it in front of them instead of behind. In about eight minutes it'll be shut and the* Grace *will be on our side of it. On your side. With you on it, and the other children, and the Factor and all his officers. They're not going to Corvid. Nobody's going anywhere. You're staying with us.*

She heard the voice say *I'm sorry about the sky,* and say that it was blue, and stop, and say it again.

She heard it say: *They'll tell you things about me. After. The Factor will, and his people.*

She heard it say: *Count what's owed, love. Count it right.*

She heard it talk about a tag. *I've got my half. You hold yours. One day somebody'll put them together.*

She heard a man's voice, very faint, through some other channel — singing, flat and loud and terrible, a song she knew. The divers' hymn. *Down in the red where the weight comes on.* She had sung it in the Lungs as a child. She had not known it was so old. She heard him get the third line wrong. And she heard a great deep boom, and a scream of air, and then the singing stop.

She heard the voice say: *Whoever hears this. If it isn't her.*

And then, a long time later — the recorder had kept running; she heard the silence, the faint hiss of it, minute after minute — she heard the voice again, quieter, closer, as though the woman had leaned down to the grille with her helmet on: *It's turning, Pell. The* Grace. *I can see it. It's turning round. You're going home.*

And then nothing for a long time. And then, very small and clear, so close to the grille that the sound of it filled the round room, three knocks. A gloved hand, on metal.

Then silence, for as long as the recorder had run.

* * *

Ysolde did not know how long she knelt there.

She was thinking about the Reliquary. She could not stop. She was thinking about the rows of seats with their rotted padding and their straps, all facing one way. The dead consoles. The round corridors with their hand-grips on the ceiling, for walking on the walls. The hatches in the floor with wheel locks. *What the Nave had been before it was the Nave: the Stewards' station, the old Combine hub.*

Not a station. A ship.

The *Grace*. *It's turning round. You're going home.* A ship a voice had called the *Grace*, turning back from a shut Door with every officer of the Combine aboard it, and the Factor, and children — *you, and the other children* — and coming home to Hesper, to the ice, where thirty thousand people were waiting. And then the twelve people on that ship, the Factor and his officers, who had been on their way out through the Door with the children to sell and had found it shut in their faces by a diver from the Sump: what had they done? They had brought the ship home. They had grafted it into the station above the ice. They had sat down together in its hold, in the first year, with no hope of relief, and written: *The Door is closed, and shall be taught as closed against them.* Against *them*. For the debt.

*No soul shall go to Gallows. What was done at Gallows is accounted and closed.*

What was done at Gallows was this. A woman in a diving suit had sat in this chair and shut the Door on the Combine to keep her daughter, and four hundred others, and the Stewards had never once in three hundred and forty years let anyone know that they had not chosen to stay. That they had been caught. That the Door they taught the Lungs to bow to, the Door the Count would open when it was zero, had been shut not by any creditor's judgment but by one of the owed, against them, in the act of their running away.

And the children. *One of a number of children who came into the Stewards' care in the first years.* Quist's voice, over the beans, with the giant at her back. *She learned her numbers at the Stewards' knees.*

Ysolde looked at the steel half-disc in the dead woman's lap. H-11873. SURETY. Its broken toothed edge.

She thought of the plain metal chair at the far end of the Reliquary, on its platform where something larger had once been bolted, and the half-disc hanging from its arm on a cord, kissed smooth.

She did not know. She could not know. There had been four hundred children. There were tags at every diver's throat in the Lungs, every one of them broken along a jagged line, every one of them half of something, and no one had ever known what. But she knelt there on the frosted floor of the round room at Gallows and looked at the dead diver's tag, and at the dead diver's face, the long jaw and the coarse black hair cut short and badly; and she thought of the words cut into the vault above the Count Board, which the First Auditor had written at the head of the first page of the first Count, in the Starving, when she was about Ysolde's age.

COUNT WHAT IS OWED AND NOT A MARK MORE.

*Count what's owed, love. Count it right.*

She found that she was weeping, inside the helmet, where she could not wipe it away. She had not wept since her seventh night in the novice dormitory. She knelt beside the dead woman's chair in the too-big suit and wept, because it did not balance, and because there was no one in the whole of the dark to see her do it.

* * *

It was the console that brought her back. A change in the light. She looked up, and the central panel had dimmed — the pup's plant feeding it was not strong — and in the dimness the three words stood out more clearly than before.

HOLD · INVERT · RESTORE

She got up. She stood in front of the console with the lamp hooked over her wrist and looked at them.

*The console says it can be opened again. RESTORE. It says it needs breath and the key and three or four hundred years.*

She put her finger on RESTORE.

Four hundred years.

She stood and looked at it. She thought, because she could not help it, of the Prelate's sum. A hundred and ninety thousand people. The breath that kept them warm. The Count that held them together. *We'd be back in the Starving inside a year.* She thought of Corin's answer: *Your Prelate's sum has a wrong figure in it. He's counted us as cattle that only walk while you whip them.* She thought of Bett and Hollis and Grandad in the back room, passing the Compact from hand to hand as though it were hot.

And she thought of the dead woman's voice. *If there's nobody left on either side of it who'd do that — then open it. And if there is, don't. Not till there isn't.*

There was. Of course there was. There was the Nave, which took children from the Lungs at nine; there were the divers, sent down at fourteen to pay a debt that did not exist. There was a Prelate who had had a rail raised. She could not open the Door tonight. She could not open it for Piet, or for Bett, or for any child now alive in the Lungs. Nobody could. *Four hundred years.*

But she could begin it.

She could set it going here, tonight, in the dark, where no one would know, and leave it to breathe. And in four hundred years, when it opened — when whatever was on the far side of it came through, as the Compact had feared it might, *if Recovery should ever come* — then whoever was alive in the Lungs then would have the choice that the dead woman in this chair had made for them. Whether to go through. Whether to shut it again. Whether there was anyone left who would sell a child. It would be theirs. Not the Stewards'. Not the Count's.

And if Recovery came, and came to collect—

She looked at her satchel, where it lay on the frosted floor beside the dead woman's chair, with her audit in it.

*So that if Recovery should ever come, it will find the books in order.*

"Let it," said Ysolde Marr, aloud, in the dead air of the round room. "Let it find them."

* * *

The pin feed was on the console's far side, a row of four valves under a hinged plate, each with the old Combine socket for a full-draw bottle. It took her two hours to bring the bottles up from the pup, one at a time, in her arms, along the frosted spine. Six of them. Corin's breath, smuggled up out of the old ice docks under the Drowned Decks for twelve years, to the far relays where a few families had never left. She fitted four of them to the valves. She kept two back; she did not know why.

Then she went to the console and pressed CONFIRM.

> RESTORATION SEQUENCE INITIATED.

> PINS RESUMING FORWARD SHEAR AT MINIMUM.

Through the frosted window, so faint that she would not have seen it if she had not been looking, the four Hanged Men stirred. A glow, deep in each of them. Deep red, like the last coal in a stove. It did not brighten. It held. And she felt, through the deck under her boots, a hum, very low, so low that it was more a pressure than a sound, as though something enormous a very long way off had begun, very slowly, to breathe.

* * *

She found the account at the fourth hour, when she had stopped expecting to.

She had been searching the console's screens, one after another, in the dim light of the pup's failing plant, for anything that looked like a ledger. She had not known if there would be one. But the oldest volumes of the Ninth had charged *pin operations* to the Holy Number, every month, at the very beginning; someone, somewhere, had kept that account. And on the seventh screen, under a heading she could just read — ACCOUNT INTERFACE — there it was.

HV-VIGIL-0001.

The same number. The same forms. She would have known them anywhere; she had kept them for twenty-two years. Assets. Liabilities. A ledger, in the Combine's own machine, at the edge of the world. And at its foot, among the functions she could guess at, one she did not have to guess at at all, because it was the first thing every novice in the Third Chancery was taught to find on a closing sheet.

STATEMENT OF RECONCILIATION — ENTER.

She sat down on the frosted floor beside the dead woman's chair, with her audit on her knees and the lamp hooked over her wrist and the console's little keyboard pulled down to her on its cable, and began to enter it.

It took her the rest of the night. Three hundred and forty-one lines. The Founding, as the First Book had printed it: a hundred and eighteen million, four hundred thousand. The Founding Terms. Every year's tithe, every year's charge, every year's balance carried. *Per Ferrow,* where she could not check it. At the two hundred and eightieth year, the balance: nil. And then the sixty-one years after, credit on credit on credit, to the foot of the column, and the total of them all, and the one word: *Overpaid.*

And in the line for the three hundred and twenty-sixth year, in the field the machine provided for a keeper's note, she typed slowly, letter by letter, in the old square hand: *Tamsin Asch, aged fourteen.*

And at the very end, when the last line was in, the console thought, and then put up a single line of its own at the foot of the statement, in amber.

> AUTHORITY SEAL REQUIRED. PRESENT FACTOR SEAL.

She looked at it. She looked at the key in the lock, white and steel, beside the dead woman's strapped hand. *Factor authority present.* The key opened the Door. It did not, apparently, close an account. For that, the machine wanted a seal.

*Seal of the Prelate-Auditor.*

She laughed, as she had laughed in the *Small Mercy*'s cabin, quite quietly, until it hurt.

* * *

They came at the ninth hour of the next day.

She heard them before she saw them — a jolt through the deck, a long way down the spine, the shudder of a hull meeting a docking ring that had not been built for it. And then, a long time after, lamps. Coming up the frosted corridor toward the round room. Four of them, swinging. Boots on the deck, slow, careful, the way men walk in a dead place.

She did not hide. There was nowhere to hide, and she found she did not want to. She stood up from the floor beside the dead woman's chair and stood in front of the console with her hands at her sides, and waited.

Strand came first. His suit was scorched down one side and his visor was cracked in a long line across its corner, and behind it his pale eyes found her in the lamplight and held. Two Bailiffs behind him, young men, in the black suits of the Prelate's household. He stopped in the doorway of the round room. He looked at Ysolde. He looked at the console, at the lit screens, at the four bottles on the pin feed.

He listened to all of it. She watched him do it. He stood beside the dead woman's chair with both hands on the head of his black cane, and his old broad face behind the visor did not change at all while the voice from the grille spoke of the Scholarship, and Brannock, and thirty thousand people in the ice. It did not change while the voice said *so I've shut it first*. It did not change while the voice said *the Factor and all his officers*. Behind him, Strand stood with his hand on his weapon, and the two young Bailiffs stood in the doorway, and Ysolde saw one of them — the younger, a long-faced boy with the look of the deep spokes about him — put his gloved hand to his throat, where, under the suit, a diver's tag would be.

It was the name that did it.

She saw it happen. She had heard the recording twice now and she knew where it came, and she was watching his face when it did, and so she saw it. The voice from the grille said, gently, at the very beginning, *Pell, it's Mum*, and Quist did not move. But near the end, when the voice said *whoever hears this, if it isn't her*, and paused, and said again, more quietly, *I hope it's you, Pell*, the Prelate-Auditor's face went white.

Not pale. White, as though every drop of blood in it had gone somewhere else at once. His hands on the head of his cane shook. He took a step back from the chair, and another, and his cane slipped on the frost, and the young Bailiff from the deep spokes caught him by the arm before he fell.

"Prelate—"

He was staring at the dead woman. He said it again, in a whisper, as though it were the first word of a prayer.

Nobody in the round room moved.

Quist caught his arm.

"*Let go,*" said Strand, and his weapon was in his other hand, and it went off.

It was very loud in the round room. It threw the Prelate backward against the console, and he slid down it to the frosted floor at the dead woman's feet, and sat there with his back against the grey metal and his legs out in front of him and a dark patch spreading on the white of his suit below the ribs.

Strand stood over him with the weapon in his hand. He looked at it as though he did not know what it was.

And behind him, from the doorway, the young Bailiff from the deep spokes — the long-faced boy who had put his hand to his throat at the sound of the divers' hymn — said in a high clear voice, "Captain. Put it down," and when Strand turned, the boy shot him.

* * *

Strand died where he fell, in the middle of the round room, on his back, with his cracked visor turned up to the frosted ceiling. Ysolde did not go to him. The other young Bailiff knelt beside him for a moment and then got up and went and stood by the door with his back to the room, and did not turn round.

The boy who had shot him lowered his weapon slowly, and stared at it, and then at Ysolde.

"My grandmother was a diver," he said. "Spoke Nineteen." His voice was shaking. "She sang that. The hymn. She got the third line wrong."

"So did mine," said Ysolde.

She went to the Prelate.

He was still alive. He was sitting against the console at the dead woman's feet, with one hand pressed to the dark patch on his suit, and when she knelt beside him he turned his head and looked at her through his visor, and smiled — the open, tired, uncomplicated smile she had seen in the old chapel at the drop dock, as though nothing in the world had pleased him so much as seeing her.

"Sister Marr," he said. His voice was thin. "You've lost weight."

"Don't talk."

"I've a few minutes. I can feel it. I'll talk if I like." He turned his head, slowly, and looked up at the console above him: the lit screens, the three words, the amber line at the foot of the seventh screen. "What's that? Your audit?"

"Yes."

"In the machine." He was quiet a moment. "Of course. The forms. They kept the forms. So that if Recovery should ever come—" He coughed, and something dark showed at the edge of his mouth. "What does it want?"

"A seal," said Ysolde. "A factor's seal."

Quist looked at her.

Then, slowly, with a care that seemed to cost him everything he had left, he lifted his right hand from the frosted floor and held it up in front of his visor and looked at it. At the black seal ring on its third finger, heavy and dull, that every novice in the Nave was taught to kiss and none was permitted to look at closely. He turned it a little, in the light of her lamp. And she saw, for the first time in her life, what was on its face, worn almost to nothing by three hundred and forty years of Prelates' hands: two letters, interlocked. An H. A V.

"Help me up," said the Prelate-Auditor.

* * *

She helped him up. It took both her and the boy from the deep spokes. They held him on his feet in front of the console, one on each side, while the dark patch on his white suit spread, and he stood swaying with his gloved right hand over the seventh screen, where the amber line said PRESENT FACTOR SEAL, and beside the screen, the small round plate of grey steel that it meant.

"Hanne told me," he said, "that it was the Stewards' own. The ring. Passed hand to hand, Prelate to Prelate. He said it was the seal of the one who kept the account." He was breathing in short shallow gasps. "*He kept the account.* That's what it says. On the last niche. You'll have seen it."

"I saw it."

"I always thought it meant he kept it *well*." He laughed, a terrible small sound. "It doesn't, does it? It means he *kept* it. Held on to it. Wouldn't let it go."

He pressed the ring to the plate.

The console thought for a moment. Then the amber line at the foot of the seventh screen went green, and above it, in the same plain square hand, a new line came up.

> FACTOR SEAL ACCEPTED: CASTELLANE, I. — FACTOR, VIGIL.

Quist stared at it.

"*Castellane,*" he said. "*The Factor.*" He looked at the dead woman in the chair beside him, at her strapped hand on the console. "*They'll tell you things about me. The Factor will, and his people.*" He closed his eyes. "Oh, she knew him. She knew him. And he sat in the hold of that ship and wrote that she'd shut it against us." His knees went. They held him. "Read it to me, Ysolde. I can't see it."

She read it to him. The screen had changed again.

> STATEMENT OF RECONCILIATION — SEALED.
> ACCOUNT HV-VIGIL-0001: CLOSED (LOCAL YEAR 280).
> BALANCE: CREDIT TO DEBTORS.

"Credit to debtors," said Aurel Quist. "*Credit.*" And then, so faintly that she had to put her visor against his to hear it: "Not a mark more."

They let him down, gently, to the frosted floor. He sat against the console where he had been, at the dead woman's feet, and the boy from the deep spokes knelt on his other side.

"Is there a name?" said Quist. "For the record. The boy's."

"Teodor," said the boy. "Teodor Brand. Spoke Nineteen."

"Teodor Brand." The Prelate turned his head. "And you heard me, Teodor. And — him." He nodded at the other Bailiff by the door, who had turned round at last. "Witnesses. Good. Hear the Count, then." He took the ring off his finger, slowly, and held it out to Ysolde in his gloved palm. "By order of the Prelate-Auditor. In the three hundred and forty-first year. Ysolde Marr is restored to the Count. And is named—" He coughed, long and wet. "Named Prelate-Auditor after me. Take it."

"I can't."

"You can. You must. Somebody has to keep the account." He closed her fingers over the ring, as Corin had closed them over the pup's yoke, as Piet had closed them over her father's tag. "And somebody has to stop keeping it. Slowly. Not all at once; they'd tear each other apart, I was right about that much. But slowly. Year by year. Take the Interest off. Raise the age back to sixteen. Lower the tithe. Let it get lighter, a little every Reckoning, until they can bear to hear what's in that machine." He was looking at her. "Do the slow thing, Ysolde. It's the hardest. Anselm couldn't. I wouldn't. You're a counter. You can."

"And the Door?"

"Let it breathe," said Aurel Quist. "You've started it. Let it breathe. Four hundred years." His eyes went past her, to the frosted window, to the faint red glow of the Hanged Men. "Let them choose, when it's open. Not us." He smiled. "We've had our turn."

He was quiet after that. She held his hand. After a while she realised that she was holding it for nobody.

* * *

They laid him beside her.

There was nowhere else. They could not take him back; the *Recompense* was crippled and had air for the three of them and barely that. Ysolde would not have put him in the cold of its hold if it had had air for a hundred. So she and Teodor Brand and the other boy, whose name was Lew, laid the Prelate-Auditor of the Nave on the frosted floor of the round room at Gallows, at the feet of the diver in the chair, on his back, with his hands folded on his chest the way the Stewards lay in their niches. Ysolde put his black cane beside him.

She left Strand where he was. The boys took him away down the spine, in the end, and she did not ask where.

Then she sat down on the floor beside the two of them, the old man and the dead woman, and wrote.

She wrote on the back of the last of Corin's reckoning sheets, on her knee, in ink, slowly, because her hands would not stop shaking. She wrote it in the ledger hand, the old square letters, because she did not know who would read it or when, and the ledger hand was the only thing she could think of that might last four hundred years. When it was done she folded it and put it in the tin Corin had given her for her pens — an old breath-seal tin, dented, with ASCH stamped on the lid — and put the tin under the Prelate's folded hands. And with it, folded small, the paper audit she had written in the *Small Mercy*'s cabin: every line, every year, Tamsin in the margin. In case the machine failed. In case whoever came could not read machines.

She left the key in the lock. The console said it must stay there, to hold the restoration. She did not touch it.

She left the dead woman's hand where it lay, flat on the console, beside it.

And then she knelt, and lifted the steel half-disc gently out of the dead woman's lap on its cord, and held it in her palm. H-11873. SURETY. Its broken toothed edge.

"I'll bring it to her," she said, aloud, in the dead air. "I promise. I'll put them together."

She put the cord over her head, beside her father's.

* * *

The Reliquary was cold and dark and smelled of iron, and Ysolde came down into it alone, on the hundred and fortieth rung of the smiths' stair, on Founding Day, with a lamp, in white.

It had been forty days. She had come back on the crippled *Recompense* with Teodor Brand and Lew Okafor and three days of air to spare, and walked into the Nave with the black ring on her finger and two Bailiffs from the deep spokes at her back, and stood at the rail of the Ledger Hall under the Count Board and told the nine Chanceries that Aurel Quist was dead in the Shear and had named her before witnesses. She did not tell them anything else. She did not tell them about Gallows. Not yet. They had not believed her, and then some of them had, and then the Chancery of Law had read the Prelate's last order down the speaker-wire to every corridor in the Lungs, *Ysolde Marr is restored to the Count*, and the Lungs had said *it is just* — and then, a day later, a little uncertainly, *it is just* again, when the dry voice said who she now was.

It would not hold. She knew that. Not without work. There were elders in the Third and the Fifth and the Chancery of Law who looked at her across the Ledger Hall the way Strand had looked at her across Anselm's desk, and there would be more. It would take everything she had, and more, and it would take years, and some of those years she would not survive. She knew that too.

At the first Reckoning, in ninety days, the Interest of Sorrow would be added to the Count as it always was. But it would be a tenth less. And at the Reckoning after that, a tenth less again. That was all. That was the slow thing. She had done the sum.

She crossed the Reliquary in the dark, past the rows of seats with their rotted straps that faced one way, past the dead consoles, past the twelve niches in the curved wall. She stopped at the last one, and held up her lamp, and read the line under the title. *He kept the account.* She looked at it for a while.

Then she went on, to the far end, to the platform where something larger had once been bolted, and the plain metal chair, and the half-disc of steel hanging from its arm on its new cord, kissed smooth by three hundred and forty years of Prelates' mouths.

She knelt in front of it, where every Prelate had knelt.

She took the cord from round her neck — not her father's; the other — and held the dead diver's half-disc in her palm, its stampings sharp and clean. H-11873. SURETY. And she reached out, with her other hand, and lifted the First Auditor's half from the arm of the chair, gently, on its cord, and held it beside the first.

The broken edges were the same steel, the same thickness, the same dull grey.

She brought them together.

They fitted. Tooth to tooth, along the whole jagged line, every point into every notch, the way they had been cut to fit on a day centuries ago when a clerk of the Combine had broken a steel disc in two and given half to a mother and half to a child. There was no gap. There was no glass between them. Ysolde Marr knelt in the dark of the Reliquary with the two halves held together in her hands, a whole disc of steel the size of her palm, sharp on the one side and worn smooth as water on the other.

She tilted it to the lamp. And in the smooth half, so faint that she would never afterward be sure she had not imagined it — a shadow in the steel, the last ghost of a stamping that three centuries of mouths had not quite kissed away — four figures.

0212.

They meant nothing to her. She did not know what they meant. She held the disc there in the lamplight for a long time anyway, and read them over and over, the way she had read Anselm's sheet that first night, until she knew them as well as she knew the Count.

Then she hung it, whole, on the arm of the First Auditor's chair, on both cords, so that the two halves would stay together; and got up from her knees; and said, aloud, in the dark, to nobody, the only response she had ever been able to make come out.

"Paid."
