# Chapter 1: Heavy Breath

*The Bond · 2431*

At nine hundred kilometres down, Tarrow stopped being weather and started being a weight.

Maren Tull felt it arrive the way she always did, first in the teeth. The *Gannet*'s hull took up a low note that had no business in a ship, a note she heard through the bones of her jaw before she heard it in the cabin, and then the straps across her chest began to tighten without anyone tightening them. Outside the forward glass the cloud went from rust to brick to the colour of an old bruise, and the light that came through it — Vigil's light, which was never much to begin with — thinned out until the only reliable thing in the world was the green wash of her own instruments.

"You've gone quiet," said Dov in her ear.

"I'm working."

"You go quiet when you're scared and you go quiet when you're working. Thirty-eight years I've been listening to Tulls go quiet and I still can't tell which."

"Then stop listening and spot."

He laughed, which turned into the cough, which went on long enough that she reached for the comm toggle before he came back. When he did his voice was wet. "Chimney forming at your two o'clock, eighty klicks. Big one. Don't go near it. There's a sink under you that I don't love, and I can't see the bottom of it."

"You never can."

"I can usually see the shape. This one hasn't got a shape."

*Old Patience* was up there somewhere above the decks, eleven hundred kilometres over her head in the thin cold where the cloud tops caught Vigil's light and turned it the pink of a healed scar. Dov flew the tender the way he drank, carefully and continuously, holding station over his diver on a laser thread so fine it could be broken by a squall, and reading the giant's weather off a radar that had been obsolete when Maren's mother first dove. Maren had never seen Tarrow from up there except on the climb. She had only ever seen it from inside.

At nine-forty the pressure alarm began its polite chirping. She thumbed it off. At nine-sixty the hull note climbed half a tone and her ears, sealed in the helmet, felt as though someone were pressing both palms against them and leaning. The scoop indicator on her left knee was still dark. Tarrow kept its heavy breath in seams, thin bright layers where the pressure and the cold and whatever else happened down here — nobody had ever properly explained it to her, and she had stopped asking at nineteen — folded hydrogen over on itself into something that wanted, very badly, to be something else. Find a seam, open the scoops, fill the bottles. Climb. That was the whole job. It was simple the way drowning was simple.

"Talk to me," Dov said.

"Nothing yet."

"You're at nine-seventy."

"I can read."

"Your mother never went past a thousand."

"My mother had a different tally-man." She eased the nose down three degrees. The *Gannet* shuddered and settled, a fat armoured seed sinking through soup. "Siggard's docked me twice this month for light loads. Third time it goes on the bond."

"It goes on the bond either way. Everything goes on the bond. Listen to me, Maren—"

The scoop light came on.

It came on amber and went to green so fast she nearly missed it, and then the whole left side of the board was lit, and beneath the hull, in the dark that was not quite dark, she could see it: a sheet of faint pale light lying in the murk like a seam of frost in black rock, stretching away farther than her lamps could follow. A good seam. A seam you told your grandchildren about, if anybody in the Sump lived long enough to have grandchildren.

"Dov."

"I see it on your telemetry. Christ. Open your mouth, girl."

She opened the scoops. The *Gannet* bucked as the intakes bit, and the sound changed — the low note under the hull broke up into a roar, a long rushing exhale, as if the giant were breathing out through her. The bottle gauges began to climb. Ten percent. Twenty. The cabin smelled faintly, as it always did on a full draw, of struck matches and pennies, which meant the seals were weeping a little of what she was stealing, which meant another week of the cough. She did not care. Thirty percent.

"How long can you hold?"

"Long as you keep me clear of your sink."

"Then you've got four minutes," said Dov, "and I'd like you to take three."

Forty. Fifty. She thought about nothing. You learned that, or you learned nothing else. You did not think about the eleven hundred kilometres of cloud above your head or the thousand-odd kilometres below you where the cloud became a sea and the sea became something without a name, where every skimmer that had ever gone missing was still falling, slow as snow, toward a floor that might not exist. You did not think about the bond. You did not think about the Nursery glass, or the way Pell had started, this last year, to press her palm flat against it before Maren did, as though it were Pell's turn now to be the one who reached. You watched the gauge. Sixty. Sixty-five.

"Maren." Dov's voice had changed. "The sink's moving."

"Moving where?"

"Up. Toward you. It's not a sink. I don't know what it is. Get your nose up."

"I'm at seventy."

"I don't care if you're at a hundred. Nose up, close your mouth, climb."

She closed the scoops. The roar stopped as though a door had shut on it, and in the sudden quiet she felt it before the instruments did: the floor of the world dropping away. The *Gannet* fell. Not sank — fell, nose first, a stone through a hole, and the straps that had been tightening on her chest let go entirely so that for one sick floating second she hung in them with her stomach somewhere up near her helmet ring and her hands already moving. Throttle forward. Pitch. Nothing. The downdraft had her, a whole column of the giant's atmosphere collapsing toward the deep like water down a drain, and the *Gannet* went with it, the altimeter unwinding, nine-ninety, a thousand, a thousand and twenty.

"Climb!"

"I am climbing."

The engines were at full and the needle was still falling. A thousand and forty. The hull note was not a note any more; it was a groan with words in it. Something in the starboard frame let go with a crack like a bone, and a red light came on above her head that she had only ever seen in drills.

She knew the number. Every breather knew the number for their own skimmer, the way you knew your own bond. With seventy percent bottles the *Gannet* weighed what she weighed, and her engines could lift what they could lift, and in a downdraft this strong there was a gap between those figures and Maren Tull was falling through it.

Her hand went to the dump lever.

It was a plain lever, bright yellow under a hinged guard. Pull it, and the bottles vented. Pull it halfway, and half of them did. Everything she had taken in the last four minutes would go back into the giant in a long pale plume, and the *Gannet* would be lighter by however much breath she had dumped, and the tally-man would write the dumped breath down not as a loss to Tarrow but as a loss to the Halloran-Vasquez Combine, product spilled by the negligence of bond H-11873, and charged accordingly.

"Maren."

A thousand and sixty.

She flipped the guard up and pulled the lever to the half mark.

The *Gannet* shrieked. That was what it felt like — a sound the whole ship made, every seam at once, as the bottles opened and the weight went out of her. The altimeter shivered, hung, and turned. A thousand and fifty. A thousand and forty. Climbing. Slowly, and then less slowly, out of the throat of the downdraft and into the shear at its edge, where the *Gannet* rolled ninety degrees and came back and rolled again and Maren sat with her hands on the controls and her teeth together and did not say anything at all.

"There she is," Dov said softly, somewhere at the top of the world. "There's my girl. Come on home."

* * *

The tally house at the Number Four refinery head smelled of hot metal and old sweat and the particular sourness of the Sump's recycled air, which had been breathed by thirty thousand people so many times that it had stopped being air and become a kind of shared memory. It was a long low hall cut into the ice, its walls lined with plastic so old it had gone the colour of tea, and down the middle of it ran the weighing line: a rail of brass-capped cradles where the breathers' bottles were racked and read.

Siggard read hers himself. He was a big man with the soft, careful hands of someone who had never had to make a fist in his life, and he moved down the rack touching each bottle with a little wand, and with each touch a number appeared on the board behind him. Maren stood with her helmet under her arm and her hair plastered to her skull and watched the numbers come up. They were good numbers. They were very good numbers. Thirty-five percent of a full draw from a seam like that was more than some breathers brought up in a fortnight.

"Thirty-four point eight," said Siggard. "Good seam."

"Very good seam."

"Telemetry shows a draw to seventy." He did not look at her. He looked at his slate. "Then a dump."

"There was a downdraft."

"There's always a downdraft." He tapped. "Product loss, thirty-five point two percent of draw, at standard valuation, less equipment damage — you've cracked a frame member, Tull, did you know that? Starboard, number six — less frame repair, less scoop recalibration, less the usual." He turned the slate around so she could see the line at the bottom. It was red. "You're down on the dive."

She looked at it for a long moment.

"I brought up thirty-five percent of a full draw."

"You lost thirty-five percent of a full draw. Combine owns the seam, Tull. Combine owns the breath in it. You took it out of the seam, which makes it Combine product, and then you poured it back into the giant, which makes it Combine product lost." He said it without malice, the way a man might explain the tides. "Count what's owed."

"And not a mark more," Maren said, because it was what you said. Her mother had said it, standing at this same rail. Dov said it. Every breather in the Sump said it, a little prayer, a little curse, the only weapon they had: *I know what you're doing, and I'm watching you do it.* "I want to see the valuation table."

Siggard sighed, and found it, and showed her. She read it line by line while the breathers behind her shifted and muttered and someone said *come on, Tull,* not unkindly. The table was correct. That was the worst of it. It was always correct. The Combine did not cheat you in the arithmetic; it did not need to. It cheated you in the rules, which were the arithmetic's grammar, and which you had never been asked to agree to.

"Fine," she said.

"Sign."

She pressed her thumb to the slate. Somewhere on Hesper High, in a machine she would never see, a number attached to her name got bigger.

* * *

The Sump had been dug into Hesper's crust by the first bonds two hundred years ago, when the Combine still thought of Vigil as a frontier and not a quarry, and it had been dug in the way that frightened people dig — down and inward, away from the sky. Its corridors were tunnels in the ice lined with foam and sheeting, and the ice was always there behind the sheeting, breathing out a cold that got into everything. It ran in seventeen levels down to the Drowned Decks, where the meltwater from the refinery heat had pooled forty years ago and never been pumped out, and above it all, on the outermost level, the bonds lived in cells that they called apartments because it cost nothing to call them that.

Maren's was on Level Three, Spoke Nine. Two rooms. A heater that worked one shift in three. A bunk she had built herself out of a scrapped scoop housing, and on the wall above it, held up with surgical tape, eleven pictures that Pell had drawn and sent down on the visitation shuttle, one a year since she was taken. The first was a crayon house with a yellow sun. Nobody in the Sump had ever seen a yellow sun. The most recent was a precise ink diagram of the *Gannet* in cross-section, with every system labelled in Pell's cramped capitals and a note at the bottom: THIS IS WHERE YOU SIT. IT'S THE SAFEST PART. I CHECKED.

She stood looking at it with her suit half-unpeeled and her body shaking with the slow rattle of exhaustion and what was left of the fear, and then she went back out, because she had been gone for three days and the Spoke would want to know she was alive.

There was a crowd at the junction board. There was always a crowd at the junction board on refinery shift-change, breathers and refinery hands and the children of the Sump who were not wards — whose parents' debts had not yet crossed the line — shoving to read the notices the Combine pasted up: tally rates, air rates, the list of the dead. But this crowd was different. It was quiet. People were reading something and then turning away from it and then turning back to read it again, the way you do with a thing you do not understand or do not trust.

She pushed in. The notice was printed on real paper, which nobody had seen the Combine waste in years, with the H-V seal at the top in silver.

> **THE HALLORAN-VASQUEZ COMBINE IS PLEASED TO ANNOUNCE THE VIGIL SCHOLARSHIP.**
>
> In recognition of the long service of the bonded community of Vigil, the Combine has selected wards of the Hesper Nursery for advancement to Combine academies in the Inner Worlds. Selected wards will receive full education, lodging and placement. The bond obligations of their surety parents will be reviewed for reduction on a schedule to be published.
>
> Selected wards will depart aboard the *Halloran's Grace* with the scheduled rotation convoy in forty days.
>
> The Combine thanks the families of Vigil for their gift.

Below that, in smaller type, ran the list. Ward numbers. Four columns of them, down to the bottom of the sheet and onto a second sheet beneath it. Maren read down the first column without breathing and then the second, and her eyes caught on it before her mind did, the way a hand catches on a nail.

0212.

"That's nearly all of them," a woman beside her said. Lin Okoro, from Spoke Six; her boy was 0188. "Isn't it? That's nearly all of them."

"Four hundred and twelve," said someone further along, an old refinery hand who had counted. "Out of four-thirty."

"What's an academy?"

"It's a school. In the Inner Worlds. Under a real sky." Lin's voice was high and strange. "They're going to go to school under a real sky."

"They're going to go," said the old man, and nothing else, and walked away.

Maren stood with her hand on the board, flat over her daughter's number, as if she could hold it there.

* * *

Visitation was on the fifteenth of every month, Combine reckoning, and the fifteenth was two days later. She rode up in the belly of the ward shuttle with forty other parents, strapped into jump seats in a cargo bay that still smelled of the ice-ore it carried on other days, and nobody spoke much. A few were talking about the Scholarship in low eager voices — what an academy would be like, what the children would learn, whether the bond reductions would be large. Most were silent. The Sump did not, as a rule, trust gifts.

Hesper High hung in orbit over the moon's dark side like a chandelier somebody had dropped and not bothered to sweep up: a central spindle of offices and docks, the long arms of the Residence where the Factor and his officers lived, and, ringed around the hub where the spin gravity was gentlest, the Nursery. Maren had been up here a hundred and thirty-two times. She knew the walk from the shuttle lock to the visitation hall the way she knew the dive line, every turning, every camera, every place where the Combine's guards stood with their hands folded over their weapons and their faces closed.

The visitation hall was a long room divided down its length by glass.

The parents' side had benches. The children's side had nothing; the children stood. The glass was thick, scratched, and cleaned every day by the wards themselves as part of their duties, and there were small voice grilles set into it every two metres, through which, if you leaned close and the child leaned close, you could hear each other. You had fifteen minutes. A light above the door counted them down.

Pell was already there.

She had grown again. Every month she had grown again, and every month it hit Maren like a slap — the length of her, the sharpness coming into her face, the way the grey Nursery smock that had swamped her at six now stopped short of her wrists. She had Maren's mother's hair, black and coarse, cut to the jaw by the Nursery barber, and she had her father's eyes, whoever he had been, and she had a mouth that was entirely her own and had been since she was two: small, straight, held tight over whatever she was thinking until she decided it was worth saying.

She put her palm flat on the glass. Maren put hers on the other side.

"You're thinner," Pell said.

"You're taller."

"That's not a fair trade." Pell leaned to the grille. "Mr. Okoro said you had a downdraft. He saw it on the refinery board. He said you dumped half."

"Mr. Okoro talks too much."

"Did you crack a frame?"

"Starboard six."

"I told you. I told you on the diagram. Starboard six is the weak one, it's a repair from before, you can see the weld line on the—" Pell stopped. Her mouth went straight. "Are you all right?"

"I'm here, aren't I?"

Pell tapped the glass three times with one knuckle: *still here.* Maren tapped back. It was a thing they had done since the first visitation, when Pell was six and too frightened to speak and had knocked on the glass because she did not know what else to do, and Maren, not knowing what it meant, had knocked back. Now it meant whatever they needed it to mean.

"You've seen the list," Pell said.

"I've seen it."

"Matron says it's the greatest honour the Combine has ever given the Sump." Pell said it in Matron Brask's voice, a fair imitation, clipped and pleased with itself. Then in her own: "Matron says we'll learn real maths. Not just tally. Orbital mechanics and statistics and actuarial—" she said the word carefully, like something with edges "—and there are libraries, and there's a sky. A blue one. She showed us pictures."

"That sounds good," Maren said. Her voice did something she did not want it to do and she stopped it.

"It does sound good." Pell looked at her. "Are you coming?"

Maren opened her mouth and nothing came.

"I asked Matron," Pell said. "I asked if the parents were coming on the convoy too, because the notice said bond reviews, and she said that wasn't how it worked, and I asked how it did work, and she said I'd understand when I was older. I said I'm twelve. She said exactly." Pell's hand was still flat on the glass. "I'm not stupid."

"I know you're not."

"So when the notice says *gift*," said Pell, "who's giving what to who?"

The countdown light above the door flicked from green to amber. Five minutes. Somewhere down the glass a man was crying, quietly, his forehead against the grille, while a small girl on the other side patted the glass in front of his face as though it were his cheek.

"I don't know yet," said Maren. "I'm going to find out."

"Will you tell me?"

"I'll tell you."

"Even if it's bad."

"Especially if it's bad."

Pell nodded, once, the way she nodded at a sum that came out right. Then she reached into the neck of her smock and pulled out the cord, and Maren did the same, and they held them up to the glass, one on each side: two halves of a steel disc the size of a palm, broken across the middle along a jagged line. Pell's half was stamped with 0212 and HESPER NURSERY. Maren's was stamped with H-11873 and SURETY. On the day a ward was taken, the Combine broke a disc and gave a half to each, and the rule was that when the bond was paid the halves would be fitted together again and the child returned. Maren had never heard of it happening. Nobody had. But the Combine's rules were the Combine's rules, and it had kept this one in the letter for two centuries — every child, every parent, every jagged line — and so you kept your half on its cord against your skin, and once a month you held it to the glass and watched your daughter hold up hers, and the two edges lined up, tooth to tooth, with fifteen centimetres of glass between them.

"Count what's owed," said Pell.

"And not a mark more."

The light went red.

* * *

The guards herded the parents out by the far door in a shuffle of bodies, and Maren let herself be herded. She was thinking about the word *academy*, and about how Lin Okoro had said *real sky* like a woman describing heaven, and about the old refinery hand who had counted the list and then walked away. She was thinking about the bond review schedule, *to be published*. She was thinking that in two hundred years of bonds and wards and broken discs, nobody had ever been given anything by the Combine that had not been entered somewhere, by someone, as a cost or a gain.

At the corridor junction outside the hall, where the parents' line bent toward the shuttle lock and the Combine's own staff came and went through a side passage to the hub, a young man in a clerk's grey jacket came out of the side passage too fast and walked into her.

It was a hard collision. He was taller than her and badly balanced, and he caught her by both arms to keep from falling, and for a moment his face was close to hers — narrow, pale, with the soft unweathered skin of someone born on a station and fed on station food, and eyes that were very frightened.

"Sorry," he said. "Sorry. I wasn't—sorry."

"Watch yourself."

"Yes." His hands were on her arms, and then one of them was gone, and then he was gone, back into the side passage and walking quickly away with his head down, and a guard was saying *move along, bond,* and she moved along.

She did not find it until she was strapped into the jump seat in the shuttle's belly with the moon swinging up huge and white beneath them. She put her hand in the thigh pocket of her coverall to find her ration chit and felt a fold of something that was not a chit, something thin and stiff. Paper. Real paper, like the notice.

She took it out under the cover of her arm, in the dim red cargo light, and opened it.

It was a strip torn from the margin of something larger. There were five words on it, written in pencil in a fast slanting hand, as though whoever wrote them had been afraid of being seen doing it:

*The Scholarship is a sale.*

And below that, smaller, pressed so hard the pencil had nearly gone through:

*Ask the price of 0212.*
