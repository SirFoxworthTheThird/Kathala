# Chapter 3: Tithe

*The Door · 3190*

The girl I took for the Margrave came back to kill me in the thirty-first year of the Door's breathing, and the first thing I did when I saw her was save her life, which tells you most of what you need to know about how much sense the world makes.

I was out in the skirt that day, picking. The skirt is what we call the junk that orbits loose around Lanthorn — the cast-off, the broken-off, the drifted-in — and picking is what I did instead of living, by then. You take your boat out along the edge of the ring shadow where Tarrow's rings throw their long dark stripe across the habitat, and you sweep for metal, and when you find a piece worth the fuel you grapple it and bring it home and cut it up and sell it in the Kettles for breath or bread or whatever the Kettles are selling that month. There are a hundred pickers on Lanthorn. Most of them are old. Picking is what you do when there is nothing else left that you're any good for, and nobody left who would hire you to do it.

My boat was the *Mendicant*. She had been a reeve's cutter once, in the days when I was a reeve, and I'd stripped the Margrave's colours off her and the gun off her nose and most of the comfort out of her cabin, and what was left was an old fast hull with good engines and a grapple arm that pulled to the left. I liked her. She didn't talk.

"Rook," said the radio. "You're drifting into my eyeline, you old fool."

"Morning, Haddie."

"It's afternoon. I've been at the glass since the ninth hour, and I've got a good clear look at the Door for the first time in a week, and now I've got a picker's arse in the middle of it. Move."

I moved. Haddie Coyle had been watching the Door from the top of Lanthorn's spine since before I was born, out of a glass dome bolted to the highest hulk in the lash with a telescope inside it older than the habitat. She was seventy-four that year. She smoked dried kelp-leaf rolled in ledger paper and drank whatever was going, and she had been, in her time, a diver and a smuggler and a teacher and a wife to two men and one woman, all of whom she had outlived; and she was the only person on Lanthorn who could read the old hand — the ledger hand, the dead square letters that you still find stamped on the oldest metal in the system — and she was the only person on Lanthorn who had ever looked at me, after I quit the reeves, as though I were still a man and not a story people told their children to make them go to sleep.

"How's she breathing?" I said.

"Wider."

"How much wider?"

There was a pause on the line, and the scratch of her lighter. "Two hand-spans on the year," she said. "Two, Rook. In the spring it was one and a bit. I've measured it three times this week and I'll measure it again tonight, but I don't think I'm wrong."

I looked up through the canopy. You can see the Door from anywhere in the Tarrow system if you know where to look, and everyone knows where to look. It hangs off the giant's trailing shoulder, far out, a coin of the wrong sky: a little round place where the stars are smeared, as though somebody had pressed a thumb on the dark and twisted. For most of history it didn't do anything. It was just there, the Door that was shut, the reason for everything, and you were born under it and grew old under it and died under it and it never changed. And then, thirty-one years ago, it had started — so slowly that it took Haddie four years of measuring before she dared say so out loud — to breathe. To open. A hair's width a year, and then a little more than a hair. Now two hand-spans, at the scale Haddie measured in, which I never understood and never asked about.

"What happens when it's open?" I said.

"Nobody knows."

"You must have an idea."

"I've got seventy-four years of ideas, and they're all worth what you paid for them." The lighter scratched again. "Go and pick something. You're making me nervous, sitting there in my eye."

I went and picked something. It was a girl.

* * *

The skiff came in from Kell-ward, low and fast, on a line that didn't make sense for anything that wanted to arrive anywhere. I saw it first as a flicker on the scope, a hot spot crossing the ring shadow with its drive lit, and then I saw the drive go out — not cut, the way a pilot cuts a drive, but stutter and spit and die, the way a drive dies when it's been run three hundred percent over its rating for too long and something inside it has finally melted. After that it was just a tumble. A small blunt shape spinning end over end through the skirt, heading for the edge of Lanthorn's lash at a speed that would have put it through two hulks and out the far side.

I should have let it go. You don't chase hot iron coming in off Kell; nothing good comes off Kell. But it was heading for the lash, and the lash was home, and there were people on the hulks it was going to hit. So I lit the *Mendicant* and went after it.

It took me forty minutes to match its tumble and another twenty to get the grapple on it without the arm tearing off. When I finally had it steady I could see what it was: a refinery skiff, the kind the Margrave's people use for running crews between the vents at Kell Deep, with her colours scorched off her hull and a long black score down one side where someone had shot at her and nearly hit. The canopy was fogged from inside. I couldn't see if there was anyone in it.

I suited up and went across.

The skiff's lock was jammed. I cut it. Inside was air — thin, hot, foul, the smell of a cabin that's been closed with a body breathing in it too long — and the body. Small. Curled up in the pilot's chair with its knees to its chest and its arms wrapped round something big and dark wrapped in a refinery blanket, the kind of grey felt they give the vent-runners at Kell to sleep in. A refinery coverall. Black hair cropped close, the way they crop the tithed so the hair won't catch fire. Hands—

I saw the hands and knew where she'd come from, if I hadn't known already. They were burned. Not freshly; old burns, healed over, the shiny ridged scar you get on the hands of the ones who work the vents with their bare palms because the gloves don't fit children and nobody at Kell Deep has ever bothered to make gloves that would. Every tithed child who comes out of the vents has hands like that. I've seen them on grown women and on dead boys. I've seen them in my sleep.

She was breathing. Barely. I got her out of the chair, and the bundle came with her because she would not let go of it even unconscious, and I got the two of them across to the *Mendicant* and into the lock, and then into the cabin, and then I got her helmet off — she'd had it on, open; she must have been breathing the cabin's dregs through it for hours — and I put her down on my bunk and turned on the heat and sat down on the deck beside her, because my legs had decided I was done standing for a while.

That was when I saw her tag.

The tithed wear them on a wire round the neck. They're not like the old divers' tags, the half-moons of steel with the broken edges that families hand down; they're new-made, stamped in the Margrave's foundry at Kell, a plain rectangle with a hole in one end. They say where you were taken from and which tithe you were taken in and your number in it. When I was a reeve I carried a bag of them blank and a stamp, and when the lots were drawn I stamped the tags and put them on the children myself, because the Margrave liked it done by hand. She said it made it real.

LAN-21-437.

Lanthorn. The tithe of the twenty-first year. Four hundred and thirty-seven.

I sat on the deck of my boat and looked at it, and I'll tell you the truth, because I've decided that in this account I am going to tell the truth even where it costs me: I did not have to look twice. I knew the number before I'd finished reading it. I have taken four hundred and nine children for the Margrave Kask in nine years of tithes, and I don't remember all of them. I'd like to tell you that I do, because it would make me sound like a better man. I don't. Some of them are just a weight. A small hand in mine on the gangway. A smell of soap somebody's mother scrubbed them with that morning, so they'd go clean. But I remember some, and I remember four-three-seven better than any.

She was nine. She had a gap in her front teeth. Her mother was a scrubber-tender on the Number Six hulk and she screamed at me all the way down the gangway, and the girl didn't scream at all. She just looked at me, the whole way, with her chin up. Like she was writing me down.

She was nineteen now, I worked out, sitting there. Ten years in the vents. Most don't last five.

I got up and got the medical kit and a bulb of water, and that was when she woke up and put my own knife to my throat.

* * *

I don't know when she got it. I keep it in my boot. I must have bent over her to check her breathing and she must have come up out of whatever dark she'd been in already moving, because the first thing I knew about it was a hand on my collar and a cold line under my jaw, and her face very close to mine, and her breath on me, foul with the skiff.

"Don't," she said. Her voice was a ruin; she'd breathed too much bad air. "Don't move."

"I'm not moving."

"You're Rook."

"Yes."

"You're the reeve. Absalom Rook. The Margrave's reeve on Lanthorn."

"I was."

The knife pressed. It was sharp; I keep it sharp. I felt the skin go, a little. "You don't remember me."

There's a moment, sometimes, when the right lie is sitting there in front of you like a tool on a bench, and all you have to do is pick it up. *No. I don't.* I could have said it. I don't know that it would have saved me. I don't know that anything would have; I don't know, to this day, how close she came. But it was there.

"You're four-three-seven," I said. "Twenty-first year. Your mother worked the scrubbers on Six. You had a gap in your teeth. You didn't cry."

The knife didn't move. Her eyes did. They went very wide, and then very narrow, and something came into them that I'd seen in a lot of eyes, over the years, and never got used to: the look of somebody who has been carrying a thing for a long time and has just discovered it weighs more than they thought.

"Her name was Lise," she said. "My mother."

"I didn't know that."

"She's dead. She died two years after. Somebody came to Kell on a crew run and told me. Scrubber lung." The knife pressed. "Did you know that?"

"No."

"Did you ever find out? About any of them? After?"

"No," I said. "I made a point of not finding out."

She stared at me. I stayed very still. I could feel blood running warm down into my collar, not much, and the hum of the *Mendicant*'s heater, and under that, from the bundle in the felt blanket lying on the bunk behind her, something else — a sound so low I'd taken it for the engines. A kind of purr.

"My name's Wren," she said. "Nobody's asked. At Kell they call us by the number. But it's Wren."

"Wren."

"I came to kill you."

"I gathered."

"I came to kill you," she said again, as though I hadn't understood. "I've been going to kill you for ten years. I used to lie in the dormitory at Kell and work it out. How I'd get out, how I'd get here, how I'd find you. Whether I'd do it fast. I used to put myself to sleep with it." Her hand on my collar was shaking now; the knife wasn't, quite. "And then I got out, and I came here, and my drive blew, and the first person who came for me was you."

"I didn't know it was you."

"Would you have come?"

I thought about it. I owed her that.

"I don't know," I said.

She laughed. It was a horrible sound, cracked and wet, and it went on too long, and in the middle of it the knife came away from my throat and she sat down hard on the edge of the bunk with it still in her fist and put her face in the crook of her other arm.

I didn't move for a while. Then I put my hand to my neck, and it came away red but not too red, and I took the bulb of water I'd dropped and held it out to her, and after a while she took it.

* * *

She told me about the bundle when she'd drunk the water and a second bulb and eaten half a brick of the kelp cake I keep for myself and don't like, and when I'd taped the cut on my neck and she'd watched me do it without offering to help. She still had the knife. I didn't ask for it back.

"The Margrave's salvage crews caught it," she said. "Three weeks ago. Out by the Door."

"Caught what?"

She pulled the felt back.

I'm not going to be able to describe it properly. I've tried, since, for people who never saw it, and I always end up making it sound like a stone or a fruit, and it wasn't either. It was black. Not painted black, not dark metal: black the way the inside of a hole is black, so that your eye slid off it and couldn't find the edges. It was the shape of a seed, or a teardrop, or the head of a spear with the point blunted, about as long as my forearm and as thick as a child's skull. And it was warm. I could feel it from where I sat, a slow even heat like a body's. And it hummed. That was the purr I'd heard. It hummed so low you felt it in your chest more than your ears, a sound that seemed to come and go, very slowly, like breathing.

"It came out of the Door," said Wren. "That's what they said. One of the Margrave's tugs was out there measuring — she has people watching it too now, did you know? Like your old woman on the spine. Since it started going faster. And this came out. Just came out, like it was spat. They went and got it. They brought it back to Kell and put it in the vault under the Margrave's house and nobody was allowed to go near it except her people and her."

"And you."

"I work the vents under the house. The vents under the house run past the vault. I've been in those vents since I was nine." She looked at her hands, the shiny burn-ridges. "I know every vent at Kell Deep. I know which ones are hot and which ones you can crawl. There isn't a wall there I can't get behind."

"So you stole it."

"I heard her talking. Kask. She was in the vault with it and two of her people, and they didn't know there was anybody in the vent. She said—" Wren stopped, and her mouth went thin. "She said, *This thing is worth more than every brat in the Deep put together, and more than every soul on Lanthorn, and I am going to find out who it belongs to and sell it to them.* She said it like that. *Every brat.* And I thought: if it's worth more than all of us, then whoever has it can buy all of us. And that night I went back in the vent with a cutter and a blanket."

"That's very stupid," I said.

"Yes."

"She'll have every boat she owns looking for it."

"Yes."

"She'll have every boat she owns looking for *you*."

"I know." She looked at me. "That's why I need you."

I'll admit I didn't see it coming. I'd been waiting, I think, for her to pick up the knife again. "Me."

"You know her patrols. You flew them. You know the dark routes and the old picket lines and the places her boats can't follow. You know where she keeps her ships and how fast they are and who flies them. I was going to make you tell me all of it," said Wren, "before I killed you. That was the plan. I was going to make you fly me somewhere safe and then I was going to do it." She shrugged, an awful, tired, nineteen-year-old shrug. "I don't know what the plan is now."

"What's the thing for?" I said. "You've got it. What do you want it to buy?"

She looked at me as though I were simple.

"The Deep," she said. "The children. There's three hundred and twelve in the vents right now. There's sixty in my dormitory. Some of them are nine. I want to trade it for them." She held my eye. "All of them. Every brat."

* * *

I called Haddie because I didn't know what else to do. I'm not proud of that either. I was fifty-two years old and I'd been a reeve and a soldier and a father, and there was a tithed girl with my knife on my bunk and a thing from the Door humming on my blanket, and the only thing I could think of was to call an old woman on the radio and ask her what to do.

She came out in her own boat, a little puttering skimmer she'd flown since before the breathing, and docked at my lock, and came aboard in a cloud of kelp smoke, and stopped dead in my cabin doorway with her mouth open.

"Haddie," I said. "This is Wren."

"I'm sure she is," said Haddie, not looking at Wren at all. She was looking at the thing on the blanket. She was looking at it the way I imagine people once looked at their gods. "Where did you get that?"

"It came out of the Door," said Wren.

"Of course it did," said Haddie softly. "Of course it did. Look at it." She came closer. She put her hand out, not quite touching. "It's warm."

"It's been warm the whole time," said Wren. "It's been humming. It got louder when I came past the ring shadow. When I could see the Door again."

"It's listening," said Haddie. "Or looking. Or—"

The humming stopped.

It just stopped, the way a held breath stops. The silence in my cabin was enormous. And then, from somewhere inside the black seed, so clear and close that all three of us flinched back from it, a voice began to speak.

It was a woman's voice, or the shape of one. Calm. Even. Unhurried. Perfectly courteous, the way the Kettle traders are courteous right before they rob you. It spoke for perhaps a minute without pausing, and I didn't understand a word of it. The sounds were almost our sounds — I could hear things in it that were nearly words, the way you can hear nearly-words in water running — but they came in the wrong order and the wrong shapes, as though our own talk had been taken apart and put back together by someone who had only read about it. Then it stopped. There was a pause exactly as long as a breath. And then it began again from the start, word for word the same.

Wren had the knife up. I hadn't seen her move. "What is it saying?"

I looked at Haddie. Haddie's face had gone grey under its seams, and her lips were moving very slightly along with the voice, and her hand, the one she had put out toward the thing, had closed slowly into a fist.

"Haddie."

"It's ledger-tongue," she said. "The old hand. Spoken." She swallowed. "I've never heard it spoken. I didn't know anyone could. I've only ever—I've only read it. On metal. In the old Count-books." She listened. The voice went on, even and kind. "It's very old. It's older than the books."

"Can you understand it?"

"Some. Not all. Not yet." She closed her eyes. "Be quiet. Both of you. Let me listen."

So we were quiet. The three of us sat in the cabin of my boat in the shadow of Tarrow's rings, with the heater humming and the Door hanging out there off the giant's shoulder like a coin of wrong sky, and listened to the thing that had come out of it say the same words again and again in its patient, pleasant voice. The fourth time through, I saw Haddie's lips stop moving. The fifth time, I saw her open her eyes, and look at Wren, and then at me, and I saw that whatever she had understood, she did not want to say it.

"Well?" I said.

"There's a word it keeps saying," Haddie said. "Near the start. And again at the end." She wet her lips. "It's an old word. It's in all the Count-books, on the first page. Nobody uses it any more. I don't think anybody's said it out loud for a long time."

"What word?"

The voice reached the end, and paused for a breath, and began again.

"*Account,*" said Haddie Coyle.
