# Chapter 14: The Audit of Piet Marr

*The Ledger · 2794*

There were five of them in Corin's back room on the second night, and they read the Compact by the light of one lamp, passing the page from hand to hand as though it were hot.

Ysolde had not known there would be so many. She had thought, in so far as she had thought about it at all in the long cold hours in the tank, that the Nullists of the deep spokes were Corin and perhaps one or two others, old divers with grudges and knives. But on the second night he had brought them down the dead-end corridor one at a time, after the last shift-change, and sat them on the bench and the crates among the hollow tanks: a grey-haired refinery hand named Hollis with burns up both forearms; a diver's widow called Bett, small and brown and fierce, who had lost a husband and two sons to the red; a boy of perhaps seventeen named Aurek, who said nothing at all and watched Ysolde as though she might explode; and an old, old man in a scrubber's coat whom the others called Grandad, who was blind in one eye and had, Corin said, been scratching circles on the walls of Spoke Nineteen for sixty years and never once been caught.

She read it to them first. Then she let them hold it.

Bett read it twice with her lips moving and then put it down on her knee and sat looking at it. "*Taught as closed against them,*" she said. "*Them.*"

"Yes."

"They wrote that. The Stewards. They sat up there in the Nave and wrote that about us." She looked up at Ysolde. "Why would they write it down? If it was a lie. Why write it down where somebody could find it?"

"Because they were keeping an account," said Ysolde. "They kept everything. Even that."

Hollis had the Founding page. He was holding it very close to the lamp, squinting, his scarred forearms trembling. "A hundred and eighteen million," he said. "That's what it says? That's the real one?"

"That's what the Combine left."

"And the primers say four hundred and six."

"Yes."

He put the page down carefully, as if it might break. "My mother," he said, "used to tell us — when we were small, when she put us to bed — she used to say the Count was what it cost to bring us here. Our ships and our air and our food on the way. Four hundred and six million. She said it like a prayer. She said: you're worth that, my loves. You cost all that, and they brought you anyway." He wiped his face with the back of a burned hand. "She died at fifty on the separators."

Nobody said anything.

"We should put it up," said Aurek suddenly. It was the first thing he had said. His voice was high and cracked. "On the boards. In every spoke. We should copy it out and put it up everywhere so they can't grind it off fast enough—"

"No," said Corin.

"Why not? It's *true*. It's in their own—"

"Because they'll grind it off and say it's a forgery," said Corin, "and then they'll find out who put it up and send them to the Breath, and on Sorrow Day the Prelate will read it out in the Hall and say that the Nullists of the Lungs are so wicked they forge the words of the Stewards themselves, and everybody in the Lungs will say *it is just*." He looked at the boy, not unkindly. "I've been doing this twenty years, Aurek. I know what they do with the truth. They count it."

The old man they called Grandad had said nothing at all. He had not asked to hold the pages. He was sitting on a crate near the lamp with his blind eye toward it and his good eye on Ysolde, and when the room had gone quiet he said, in a soft, dry, papery voice:

"Gallows."

They all looked at him.

"It says Gallows," said Grandad. "Doesn't it? On the paper. *No soul shall go to Gallows.*"

"Yes," said Ysolde. "Do you know the word?"

"I know a word like it." He turned his good eye up toward the ceiling, toward the Nave, a hundred kilometres over their heads. "When I was a boy I ran breath to the outer relays. Before they shut them down. There were divers on the long runs who used to say there was something out by the Door, on the far side of the Shear, where nobody went. A dead thing. Hanging there. You could see it on a clear day from the far relays if you had a good glass." He smiled faintly. "The old ones called it the Hanged Men. Because it had four legs, see. Hanging down."

"The Hanged Men," said Ysolde.

"I never saw it myself. I never had a good glass." He folded his old hands. "But I'll tell you this, Sister. I knew a diver once, a woman from Spoke Twenty-one, Nell Asher her name was — no relation — who went out past the Shear on a dare in a stolen skimmer, sixty years ago, to look at it close. And she came back. And she never would say what she'd seen. Only that it was dark, and it was a long way, and there was nothing alive there. And the Bailiffs came for her a month later and she went to the Breath for theft of a skimmer." He looked at Ysolde. "They don't send you to the Breath for a skimmer, Sister. They take your share."

* * *

They came for Piet on the third morning.

She heard it on the speaker-wire. She was in the back room alone, writing — she had begun, the night before, to write the pages out again from memory on reckoning sheets, every word of the Compact and every column of the Founding, in case anything happened to the originals; she could do it, she found, as easily as she could have written out the catechism; she had read them once under a lamp in the Reliquary with Strand coming down the ladder, and they had gone into her like a dye — when the wire crackled at the second bell and the dry voice of the Chancery of Law came down it.

"*Hear the Count. By order of the Prelate-Auditor: Piet Marr, diver, of Spoke Twelve, crew of the* Dutiful*, is taken into the Count's keeping for audit. It is found that forty-one hours of false tithe were entered to his account in the Ninth Ledger, in the hand of the defaulter Ysolde Marr, his sister. He will be audited in the Ledger Hall at the fourth bell tomorrow before the Prelate-Auditor and the nine Chanceries.*"

A pause.

"*If the defaulter Ysolde Marr gives herself into the Count's keeping before the audit, with all that she has taken from the Nave, the audit of Piet Marr will be set aside. It is just.*"

And down every corridor of the Lungs, faint and far, the answer.

She sat at Corin's bench with the pen in her hand and the half-written page in front of her, and did not move until the ink on the nib had dried.

* * *

"No," said Corin.

"I wasn't asking."

"That's twice you've said that to me." He was standing in the doorway of the back room with his arms folded. Behind him the shop was dark and shut. Bett was there, and Hollis, sitting on the bench, not looking at either of them. "And twice I've let you. Not this time."

"He's my brother."

"He's a diver. He's twenty-two. He knew what this was when he sent you that note." Corin's voice was very hard. "*Don't do anything stupid.* That's what he wrote. You told me. He's not a fool, Ysolde. He knew what you'd do for him and he asked you not to."

"They'll send him to the Breath."

"They might. They might not. The Prelate doesn't want your brother. He wants those pages back, and he wants you, and he's using what he's got." He came into the room. "If you go up there, he'll have both. And you'll go to the Breath, and Piet'll go back to the *Dutiful* with forty-one hours struck out and a default on his line that'll follow him the rest of his life, and nobody in the Lungs will ever read a word of what's on those pages. That's the sum. Do it yourself. You're the counter."

She did it. She did it standing there with her hands at her sides and her face, she knew, as white as Hollis's knuckles on the bench. It came out the way he said.

"I'm not going to give myself up," she said.

Corin's shoulders went down a fraction.

"I'm going to give him the pages," she said.

* * *

The audit was at the fourth bell the next day, and the speaker-wire carried it into every corridor of the Lungs. She listened to it in the back room among the tanks, sitting on the bench with her arms around her knees, with Bett on one side of her and Grandad on the other, and Corin standing at the curtain with his back to them, not moving.

It was very short. She had attended audits as a keeper, standing in the ranks of the Third Chancery in her grey, and she knew the form: the charge, the Ledger, the response, the Prelate. The defaulter stood at the rail before the Count Board, under the great plates, and the keeper of the Ledger read the entry aloud, and the defaulter was asked to answer it, and then the Prelate spoke.

The keeper who read the entry was Brother Tomas, who had taken her Seventh. She heard his voice shake.

"*Ninth Ledger, volume two hundred and six, Spoke Eighteen. The* Dutiful*, crew of five. Forty-one hours entered to the crew's account under the date of the last dive, in the hand of Ysolde Marr, keeper. The default mark struck and initialled by the same hand. The hours are false. The dive log of the* Dutiful *shows no such hours.*"

"*Piet Marr,*" said the voice of the Prelate-Auditor. It was very gentle. "*Did you ask your sister to enter these hours?*"

And then her brother's voice, high and clear and ridiculous in the great ribbed space of the Ledger Hall, with the faint ring of the vault behind it.

"*No, Prelate. I asked her not to do anything stupid.*"

There was a sound in the Hall that might have been laughter, quickly stifled. In the back room, Bett put her hand over her mouth.

"*I believe you,*" said Quist. "*I'm sure you did.*" A pause. "*Piet Marr, the Count has been wronged by forty-one hours entered falsely to your account. The Count must be made whole. By the law of the Count, a false entry is answered by the Breath, unless it be set right.*" Another pause, longer. Ysolde could hear the old man breathing. "*I will defer judgment until the fourth bell tomorrow, to allow it to be set right. It is just.*"

*It is just,* said the Hall. *It is just,* said the Lungs, down every corridor.

And then, after the response, so softly that she almost missed it — it was not part of the form; he must have leaned in to the speaking-horn and said it to the wire alone — the Prelate-Auditor said:

"*Ysolde. The drop dock, the old chapel. Tonight, at the middle bell. I'll come alone.*"

* * *

The old chapel at the drop dock was a little room off the shuttle gallery where, in the first century, divers going up to the Nave on audit had been allowed to pray. Nobody had used it in a hundred years. It had four benches and a bare wall where something had once hung, and a single window onto the dock floor, and it smelled of ice.

He was there when she came. He was sitting on the front bench in his white, with his black cane across his knees and his hands folded on it, and he was alone. She had made Corin's people watch the dock for two hours before she came, from every gallery and every grating, and they had seen him come down on the eighth-bell shuttle with two Bailiffs and send the Bailiffs back up on the ninth.

He looked up when she came in, and his broad lined face broke into a smile of such open, tired, uncomplicated pleasure that for a moment she could not move.

"Sister Marr," he said. "You've lost weight. Sit down."

She did not sit. She stood at the end of the bench with her hands in the sleeves of the diver's coat Bett had given her, a long grey thing that smelled of someone else's life, and looked at him.

"Have you brought them?" he said.

"Yes."

"May I see?"

She took them out — the two pages, torn from the First Book, folded small — and held them up where he could see them in the light from the dock window. She did not hand them to him.

He looked at them for a long moment.

"You read them," he said.

"Yes."

"Both."

"Yes."

"And you understood them, I suppose. You're a counter." He sighed. "I read them for the first time eleven years ago. On my first Founding Day as Prelate. Hanne had told me what to expect, before he died, but it's another thing to read it." He turned his cane a little in his hands. "Do you know what I think, Ysolde, every Founding Day, when I go down and kneel by her chair and kiss her tag and then get up and read those words?"

She said nothing.

"I think: *they were right.*" He looked up at her. "Twelve people. Thirty thousand starving in the ice. No hope of relief — they say so, don't they? *Having no hope of relief.* They knew no one was coming. They knew the Door was shut for good. And they had to make thirty thousand frightened, dying, furious people go on diving into the red and bringing up the breath, every day, for the rest of their lives, for no reward at all, or every one of them would die. How would you have done it?" He waited. "Truthfully, Sister. If you were Steward of Law, in the first year. How would you have done it?"

"Not with a lie."

"With what, then?"

She opened her mouth, and found that she had nothing to put in it.

"Quite," said Quist gently. "Nor did they. So they used what they had. They had the account. They had the forms of the Combine and thirty thousand people who'd been raised in them. They knew how to make people work for a number. So they gave them a number." He spread his old hands. "And it worked. It worked for three hundred and forty years. A hundred and ninety thousand people are alive tonight because twelve frightened people in the first year wrote a lie in a book. I think that's the most remarkable thing anyone has ever done in this system. I think about it every day."

"And the Interest?" she said. "The pages cut out of the Ninth? The ink? Was that remarkable?"

He was quiet for a moment.

"That was Prelate Orrin," he said. "In the two hundred and seventy-ninth year. He did the sum the way you did it, the way Anselm did. He saw the Count was going to reach zero. And he did another sum, after it — the one I showed you over dinner. And he took up his pen." Quist looked down at his hands. "I didn't do it. I want you to know that. But I would have. I've done the sum too. Every Prelate since Orrin has done it."

"Did Anselm do it?"

He looked up.

"Did he do the sum?" she said. "Your sum? Before he went to the Breath?"

The Prelate-Auditor looked at her for a long time in the cold of the old chapel, with the dock lights shining through the window on his white robe.

"He did," said Quist quietly. "He came to me with his sheet, and I showed him mine, and he did it. At my table. With my beans going cold." He shook his head slowly. "And then he said to me that the sum was right and that he didn't care. That a hundred and ninety thousand people had the right to know what they owed, even if it killed them. That the First Auditor wrote *not a mark more* and meant it." He smiled, very faintly. "He was a counter."

"And you sent him to the Breath."

"The rail at the Number Two gallery is low," said Aurel Quist. "I've had it raised." He held out his hand. "The pages, please, Ysolde. And your brother goes home tonight, and you go wherever you like in the Lungs, and I'll strike your default at the next Reckoning and say there was an error in the Chancery of Law. I give you my word. I've never broken it."

She looked at his hand. It was old and spotted, and on its third finger the black seal ring sat heavy and dull in the dock light. She had kissed that ring at nine, kneeling in the novice line, without once looking at it.

"What's Gallows?" she said.

He went very still.

"It's in the Compact," she said. "*No soul shall go to Gallows. What was done at Gallows is accounted and closed.* What was done there? What is it?"

"I don't know."

"You're the Prelate."

"I'm the Prelate, and I don't know." He said it plainly, and she believed him — she could not help it; there was something in his face she had not seen before, a kind of blankness, a door shut. "No Prelate does. It's the one thing in the Book that isn't explained. Hanne didn't know. Hanne's Prelate didn't know. The Stewards closed it, and they never wrote down what was in it, and every Prelate since has kept it closed because they did." He was looking at her very hard now. "Don't, Ysolde."

"Don't what?"

"Whatever you're thinking. Don't." His voice had changed. "I've seen that look on a counter's face before. You can't sum it, so you want to go and look. Don't. It's closed for a reason. Twelve people who had the courage to lie to thirty thousand closed it, and wrote that it was closed, and never once in three hundred and forty years has anybody gone to see why. Leave it shut."

She held the pages out.

He took them. His hand closed over them, and for a moment he held them, and her fingers on the other edge, and they were both holding the same two sheets of paper in the dock light, the old man and the young woman, like two people on either side of a glass.

She let go.

"Thank you," said the Prelate-Auditor. He put the pages inside his white robe, against his chest, the way she had carried them. "Your brother will be on the first-bell shuttle down." He got up, slowly, leaning on his cane, and looked down at her. "Go home, Ysolde. Wherever that is now. Be a diver. Be anything." He smiled a little. "Be happy, if you can manage it. Counters seldom do."

He went out. She heard his cane tap away across the dock floor, slow, and the hiss of the shuttle lock, and then nothing.

She stood in the old chapel for a long time, with her empty hands in the sleeves of a dead man's coat.

Then she went back down the ladders to the bottle shop, and sat down at Corin's bench, and took a fresh reckoning sheet and her pen, and began to write the Compact out again from the beginning, every word, in the brown ink's careful hand that she could see as clearly behind her eyes as if it were lying on the bench in front of her.

*We, the Stewards of account HV-VIGIL-0001—*

* * *

Piet came down on the first-bell shuttle, as the Prelate had said. He came straight to the bottle shop. She did not know how he knew where she was; he simply walked down the dead-end corridor on Spoke Twenty and in through the front door of Corin's shop with his hair standing up and a bruise on his cheekbone and his tag on its cord at his throat, and stood looking at her across the counter where she had come out at the sound of the bell.

"You did something stupid," he said.

"Yes."

"I *told* you—"

"I know what you told me."

He came round the counter. He was taller than she was; he had been since he was fourteen. He put his arms round her and lifted her off her feet, the way he had used to when he was a boy, to annoy her, and held her there, and did not say anything, and she felt his chest heave against hers with the small dry catch at the end of every breath that he did not notice and she noticed every time.

"They say you're a Nullist," he said into her hair.

"I'm an auditor."

"That's what I told them." He put her down. He looked at her. "What did you give him?"

"Two pages."

"Were they worth it?"

"I'm going to find out," she said.

* * *

Corin told her about the boat that afternoon, in the back room, with the others gone and the shop shut and the lamp turned down.

"*Small Mercy*," he said. "She's moored in the old ice docks under the Drowned Decks. Has been for twelve years. I run breath in her, to the far relays on the Shear's edge, the ones the Nave closed and a few families never left. Bailiffs know she exists. They don't know where she lives." He was looking at the floor. "She's got the legs. She's carried four tons of full-draw bottles to the Shear and back. She could make the Door."

"How long?"

"Nine days. Maybe ten. The Shear's bad this season." He looked up. "And you'd need breath. A lot of it. For the run, and for whatever you think you're going to do when you get there." His mouth twisted. "Lucky you've come to a bottle shop."

"Corin—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "You're going. I can see it on you. You've been going since you heard the old man say *Hanged Men*. I'd rather you went with somebody who knew the Shear than went on your own in a stolen skimmer like that fool Nell Asher and came back with nothing to say." He was quiet a moment. "And I want to see it. I've been scratching circles for twenty years, Ysolde. I'd like to see what they were so afraid of, just once, before they send me to the Breath."

She looked at him across the back room, among the hollow tanks.

"You're as stupid as I am," she said.

"I've been told," said Corin Asch.

* * *

Piet came to the old ice docks to see them off. She had told him not to. He came anyway, down through the dark under the Drowned Decks where the ice had been cut away three hundred years ago into great caves and then abandoned, following Corin's lamp along a ledge above black water, and stood on the frozen quay beside the *Small Mercy* — a squat, scarred, ugly old hull with oversized engines and a cargo belly fat with bottles — with his hands in his pockets, watching her climb the ramp.

"Wait," he said.

She turned.

He took his tag off. Their father's tag. He pulled the cord over his head and held it out to her: the half-disc of dull grey steel with the broken toothed edge, worn smooth, its old stampings long since gone.

"You don't go out without a tag," he said. "Everybody knows that."

"Piet. That's Dad's."

"I know whose it is." He put it in her hand and closed her fingers over it. "Bring it back. That's all. You don't have to bring anything else back. Just that."

She put it on. The cord was warm from his neck. The steel lay against her breastbone where the torn pages had lain, and the reckoning sheets with her copies of them lay folded inside her coat over it, and she stood on the ramp of the *Small Mercy* and looked at her brother on the quay in the lamplight and could not find a single thing to say.

So she said, "Count what's owed."

And Piet grinned his wide stupid grin, their mother's grin, and said, "And not a mark more," and stepped back from the ramp.

* * *

The *Small Mercy* came up out of the old ice docks through a shaft that Corin said had been a breath-pipe once, three hundred years ago, before the heads were moved, and broke surface on Hesper's night side in a spray of frost. Ysolde was in the second seat, strapped in, with her father's tag in her fist. Through the forward glass she watched the moon fall away below them, white and grey and scarred, and then the dark, and then — swinging up into view as Corin brought the old boat round — the Nave.

She had never seen it from the outside. Not properly. Only from the drop shuttle's tiny port, in glimpses. It hung over Hesper's night side like a great dark chandelier, spokes and rings and towers, lit here and there with the small warm lights of a hundred thousand windows, and at its heart, where the oldest parts were, the huge ribbed bulk of the Ledger Hall and whatever lay beneath it. Somewhere in there, the Count Board was standing in the dark, waiting for the first bell. Somewhere in there, an old man in white had two pages against his chest.

"There," said Corin quietly.

He was looking at the aft scope. She looked too.

Low on the Nave's lower dock, a small hard light had come on. A drive. And as she watched it lifted, and turned, and began, very steadily, to follow.

"Bailiff cutter," said Corin. "Strand's." He did not sound surprised. "Well. He knows where we're going, then." He reached forward and pushed the throttles to their stops, and the *Small Mercy* shuddered all through her old frame and leaned into the dark. "Hold on to your tag, Sister. It's a long way to the Hanged Men."
