# Chapter 12: Receivers

*The Door · 3190*

They came through on the fourth day, a little after the eleventh hour, and Haddie was watching when they did. She'd hardly stopped watching since we got to the Hulk. She'd set her little glass up at the cracked port of the dead dock where I kept the *Mendicant* and she sat beside it on an upturned crate in two coats with a blanket over her knees and a Count-book in her lap, and every hour she'd lean in to the eyepiece and look, and write a number down, and lean back.

The numbers had stopped making sense on the second day. She'd told me so, quite calmly, the way you'd tell somebody the scrubbers were failing. *It's not breathing any more, Absalom. It's opening. Like a mouth.*

I was in the hall with the children when she called me. I'd been trying to get the old scrubber to run on less, because thirty-four people breathing in a room built for none is a sum that doesn't come out. The children were sitting in the curve of the vault under the strung lamps in little knots, the way they'd sat in their bunks at Kell, and Wren was going between them with the water, and Kesse was teaching the small ones a counting game with the white numbers off the fallen plates. Somebody had made a tower out of three of them — a nine, a two, a nought. They'd been at it all morning. It was the first time I'd heard any of them laugh.

"Rook!" Haddie's voice, down the long corridor from the dock. "Rook, come here. Come *now*."

I came. Wren came behind me.

Haddie didn't take her eye from the glass. She just held out her hand behind her, flapping it, and I put mine in it, and she pulled me down and pushed me at the eyepiece.

I don't know what I'd expected. I'd looked at the Door through that glass a dozen times by then, and it had always looked the same: a little round blur of wrong stars, bigger every time, like a coin sinking in dark water. It wasn't that now. The middle of the coin had gone black — not the black of space, but a deeper black, a hole in the black — and round its rim the smeared stars were moving. Turning. Pouring inward, slowly, like water round a drain.

And out of the hole, as I watched, things were coming.

They were small at that distance — specks, sparks — but there were a lot of them. They came out of the black at the Door's heart in a steady stream, like seeds blown off a head of grass, one after another after another, and as each one came clear of the rim it seemed to *open*: to put out vanes or wings or arms, I couldn't tell which, that caught the faint far light of Vigil and glinted. And then they turned. All of them, in the same moment, the way a shoal of fish turns. Toward us. Toward Tarrow. Toward the moons and the rings and the lash and everything that lived.

"How many?" I said. My voice came out strange.

"I stopped counting at two hundred," said Haddie.

* * *

Behind us, on the dock floor in its blanket, the herald stopped humming.

None of us moved. I kept my eye at the glass, watching the sparks pour out of the black. Haddie's hand was still in mine; I could feel it shaking. And then the voice came, the calm courteous voice, but not from the herald alone. From the herald and from the *Mendicant*'s cockpit speaker, twenty metres away through the open lock, and from the hand-radio in Wren's belt, and — faint, far, tinny — from some dead speaker somewhere back in the Hulk's corridors that had not made a sound in two hundred years. All at once. All the same words.

Haddie translated as it went. She didn't need to stop and work at it any more; she'd had four days of the herald saying the same things over and over, and she'd caught the shape of it now, the way you catch the shape of someone's accent. Her voice ran a half-beat behind the machine's, low and steady.

"*This is the receiver for account HV-VIGIL-0001.*

"*The passage is open. Recovery has commenced.*

"*Debtors are asked to remain in their habitations. Debtors will be inventoried in order. Debtors will be held in custody pending reconciliation.*

"*A factor has been appointed from among the debtors to assist the receiver. The factor is—*" Haddie stopped. Swallowed. "*The factor is Teodora Kask, of Kell Deep. The factor speaks for the receiver.*

"*The fixed asset known as Hesper High Station is designated the collection depot. Debtors in custody will be brought to the collection depot for transfer.*

"*Resistance to recovery constitutes a further default.*

"*This notice will repeat.*"

The voice stopped. The herald began to hum.

I took my eye from the glass.

"Hesper High Station," I said. "What's that?"

Haddie didn't answer. She was looking past me, out through the cracked port, not at the Door but down: at the great dark curve of the moon below us, white and grey and scarred. And then at the dock around us. The dead consoles. The old hand painted on the walls.

"It's here," she said. "Isn't it."

Wren had gone very still in the dock doorway.

"It's here," said Haddie again. "This. The Hulk. It was something before it was the Hulk; it had to be. Somebody built it. Somebody owned it." She looked at me, and her old face was grey. "They're going to bring everybody here, Absalom. Everybody they take. They're bringing them *here*."

Behind Wren, down the long corridor, faint and sweet, I could hear the children in the hall still laughing at their counting game.

* * *

We went to Lanthorn. I didn't decide to. I just found myself in the *Mendicant*'s cockpit with the lock sealing behind me, and Wren in the co-seat with her helmet on her knees, having followed me without asking whether she could.

"You should stay," I said. "With them."

"Haddie's with them. Kesse's with them." She strapped in. "You're going for the old woman. The blind one. Yelland."

"And Haddie's sister's lad on Number Four. And the Fenns. And whoever else." I lit the drive. "I left them there. I walked out of that cargo pod and left them."

"They were going to give me to Kask for a tenth more breath," said Wren.

"I know."

"I'm only saying," said Wren. "So you know I know." And she put her helmet on.

* * *

It was six hours from the Hulk to the lash, and they'd beaten us there by two.

I've tried to think how to tell it. I keep wanting to make it sound like something — like a battle, like a fire, like a storm — and it wasn't any of those. It was quiet. That's what I remember. It was so orderly and so quiet.

The tenders — that's what Haddie called them, after; the word was in the notice, she said, she'd just not understood it — were big. Bigger than they'd looked through the glass. Each one was the size of the *Mendicant* three times over, black like the herald was black, so your eye slid off them, and they'd folded their vanes back now and put out legs instead. Six legs, long and jointed. And they'd come down on the hulks of Lanthorn the way a tick comes down on a dog, and *held on*, and where their bodies touched the hulls a faint red light was showing, a seam of it, where they were cutting in.

There were twenty or thirty on the lash already. More coming. As we came in through the ring shadow I watched one settle on the old ore hulk at the lash's edge, slow and careful, legs first, as if it didn't want to wake anything.

And moving among them, in and out of the light, little and quick and red: the Margrave's cutters.

"She's showing them," said Wren. Her voice in my helmet was flat. "Look. Look where they're going. They're going to the big hulls first. Six. Two. The tank hull. The ones with the most people."

She was right. Every tender that came in to land had a red cutter going ahead of it, like a dog in front of a herd, and every one landed on a hull where I knew there were hundreds living. The count. *Name, age, health, trade.* Kask had her lists already. She'd had her reeves out collecting them for four days. And now she was using them to tell the receiver where to start.

"Number Two," I said. "Yelland's hull. Is there one on it?"

"Not yet."

I put the *Mendicant* down hard on Number Two's dock and didn't bother with the lock cycle; I blew it. We went in at a run.

* * *

The corridors of Number Two were full of people, and none of them were running. They were just standing. Hundreds of them, in the passages and the doorways and the common rooms, holding on to each other and looking at the ceiling, where — through the plating, through the old foam, through two centuries of patches — they could hear it coming: a long, slow, scraping sound, metal on metal, like something very large settling itself.

I found Mother Yelland in the cargo pod, at the head of the Council table, alone. She'd sent everyone else away. She was sitting in her chair with her blind face turned toward the door and her two hands flat on the table, the way she'd sat to quiet them all for sixty years.

"Absalom Rook," she said, before I'd said a word. "I'd know that walk anywhere. You've come back."

"I've come for you, Mother. There's a boat on your dock. Come on."

"No."

"Mother—"

"I'm ninety, boy. I'm blind. I've been eldest of Number Two since before you were born." She didn't move her hands. "If they want to inventory me, they can come in here and do it. I'll tell them a thing or two." She smiled, a little. "Take the children. There's a dozen in the crèche on the lower deck. The Fenn girl's with them. Take them."

Over our heads the scraping stopped. And then, very faintly, through the deck, I heard a new sound — high and thin, like a saw.

"Go on," said Mother Yelland. "Go on. You've wasted enough of my time."

I went.

* * *

The crèche was on the lower deck, and the Fenn girl — old Fenn's granddaughter, a big serious girl of sixteen — had them all in the corner furthest from the door with a cutting bar in her hands. Eleven. The youngest was a baby in a sling across her chest. When she saw me in the doorway in my suit she raised the bar, and then she saw Wren behind me and lowered it, and her face crumpled.

"They're in the upper deck," she said. "They came through the roof. I heard them. I heard people—"

"I know. Come on."

We got them out. I'll not pretend it was anything clever. We just ran them, eleven children and a baby, down the corridors to the dock, the long way round, through the back passages behind the tank hull's fuel lines that only pickers and tenders know. And once — only once — at a junction where the back passage crossed one of the main corridors, I looked down it, and saw.

There were things in the corridor. They were smaller than the tenders, a lot smaller — about the size of a man, maybe, if a man were made of black jointed rods and walked on six legs and had no head. They were moving down the corridor in a line, not fast, not slow, and the people standing in the corridor weren't running from them. I don't know why. I think they were too frightened. Or I think that, after a whole life on Lanthorn under the Margrave, they'd learned the same thing the children at Kell Deep had learned: that you don't run, because nobody's listening.

The things reached a woman standing in a doorway. One of them put out two of its rods and touched her, very gently, on the shoulders. And something came out of it — out of its middle, where a body would be — something pale and soft and glistening, like a bubble of oil, that unfolded over her the way a hand closes, from her shoulders down, until she was inside it. Standing up. Her mouth open. I could see her through it, the way you see something through water. And then it went opaque, and hard, and grey, and the thing lifted it — lifted her — and passed it back down the line to the next one, which passed it to the next, and the first one moved on to the next doorway.

Like a chain of people passing buckets.

"Rook," said Wren behind me.

Further down the corridor, in among the black things, there was a woman walking toward them. She was old and she was wearing her best coat and she had her hands pressed together under her chin, and she was smiling. I knew her. It was Dorrit, from the Council meeting. The believer. *For the Stewards, for the Combine.* She walked right up to the nearest of the black things and stood in front of it, smiling up at where its face would be, with tears running down her cheeks, and said something I couldn't hear.

It touched her shoulders. Very gently.

"*Rook,*" said Wren.

I turned away and ran the children to the boat.

* * *

The *Mendicant* got off Number Two's dock with eleven children, a baby, a sixteen-year-old girl with a cutting bar, Wren and me, and we'd been gone from the dock perhaps four minutes when the tender that had settled on the roof of Number Two put a leg through the cargo pod. I saw it on the aft scope. One long black jointed leg, coming down through the roof of the old pod where the Lash Council had sat round its table for a century, slow and careful, like someone feeling for a step in the dark.

I didn't say anything. Neither did Wren.

We came out of the lash on the dark side, low, through the wreck-field, the way I'd come out of it running from Benno Strick, and nothing followed us. Nothing even looked. The tenders were busy, and the Margrave's cutters were busy showing them where to be busy. And as we cleared the ring shadow every speaker on my boat came on at once, the calm voice speaking its notice again, and under it — on the general band, cutting across — another voice.

"*People of the system,*" said the Margrave Kask. "*You've heard the receiver. I've spoken with it. It has made me its factor.*" A pause. "*I know you're frightened. I know it seems hard. But the receiver tells me that every debtor in custody will be held safely at the collection depot until the account is reconciled. Nobody will be harmed. And I am working, every hour, to get us the best terms.*"

Another pause. That voice. That hand on the back of your neck.

"*Don't resist,*" said the Margrave. "*It counts as a further default. I've asked the receiver what that means and it's been very clear. Stay in your hulls. Let them count you. And I'll get you home.*"

"*Home,*" said Wren, very softly, beside me.

In the cabin behind us, one of the small ones had started to cry, and the Fenn girl was hushing it, and the baby in her sling had begun to wail, and the calm voice of the receiver went on and on over all of it, word for word the same, as patient as the cold.

* * *

Haddie had found the word while we were gone.

She met us at the dead dock with a Count-book in her arms and a lamp in her fist and such a look on her face that I thought, for a moment, one of the children had died. Then I saw she was holding the book open against her chest, the way you'd hold a baby, and that her eyes were shining.

"Where have you been? No — I know where you've been. How many?" She looked past me at the children coming out of the lock, the Fenn girl and her baby. "Oh. Oh, good. Good. Get them warm." Then back to me. "Absalom. I've found it. Come and see. I've found where it was held."

"Where what was held?"

"*The Door,*" said Haddie Coyle.

* * *

She showed me in the hall, at a table she'd made out of one of the fallen plates laid across two crates — a big black seven, upside down, with the Count-book open on it under the lamp. It was the oldest of her nine. She'd always said so: the pages were a different stuff from the others, thin and yellow and brittle, the old Combine plastic, and the hand on them was different too, smaller and straighter, all in a brown ink gone the colour of dried blood.

"I never read this one properly," she said. "I never could. It's the oldest of them, and the hand's the hardest, and most of it's just numbers — columns and columns, like all the others. I always thought it was the same as the others. Labour, hours. That kind of thing." She turned the pages carefully. "But it's not. It's older than that. It's from before. It's from when the Door was still *open*."

"How do you know?"

"Because of what it's counting." She put her bent finger on a line. "Look. That word. It's on every page, in the second column. I didn't know it. I'd never seen it anywhere else. I thought it was a name. But I've been listening to that thing—" she jerked her head at the herald, humming on its blanket at the end of the table "—for four days now, saying *passage*, *passage*, *passage*, and I heard it, last night, and I thought, I know that shape. I've seen that shape." She tapped the page. "It's *passage*. It's the same word. And the word beside it, every time, is this."

I leaned in. Beside the word she'd shown me was a short run of the old figures, the same shapes as the white marks on the fallen plates.

"That's a number," said Haddie. "A charge. What it cost. Every page. And the column it's in is headed—" she turned back, to the top of the page, and put her finger under a word written larger than the others, in the same brown ink "—this."

"What is it?"

"I think," said Haddie slowly, "it says *pins*." She looked up at me. "And the heading over the whole page, the place where all of it's being charged — where the cost of the passage is being kept — is here."

There was a word at the head of the page, underlined. Seven letters. I couldn't read them. I'd never been able to read them.

"Gallows," said Haddie.

The herald hummed. Somewhere down the hall a child coughed.

"I don't know what it means," I said.

"Neither do I. Not the word." She took her spectacles off and rubbed her eyes; her hands were shaking again. "But I know what it is. Think, Absalom. *Pins.* *Passage.* *Gallows.* The cost of keeping the Door open, charged every month, in the oldest book there is. Somebody was *holding it*. Back then, back before, when the Door was open and ships went through it. Something was holding it open. And whoever wrote this book was paying the bill." She put her finger on the word again. "And the bill was charged to here. To a place. To something called Gallows."

"The Spider," said Wren.

We both looked at her. She'd come up behind us without either of us hearing, the way she did, and was standing at the end of the table with the water bulb in her hand, looking down at the page.

"The Spider," she said again. "By the Door. The old dead thing. You can see it from Kell, if you go up on the chasm lip on a clear shift with a glass — the vent-runners used to, on dares, for luck. It's tiny. It's right by the Door, just off the edge of it, and it's got legs." She held up her hand, the fingers hanging down. "Four of them. Long ones. Hanging. Like a spider on a thread. Like—" She stopped.

"Like a gallows," said Haddie softly. "With four hanged men on it."

None of us said anything for a while.

"If something at Gallows held the Door open," I said at last, "back then—"

"Then something at Gallows could close it," said Haddie. "Or did close it. Or *can*." She put her spectacles back on. "I don't know, Absalom. I'm guessing. It's a word in a book. But that thing—" the herald "—said *receiver*. It said *the passage is open*. And there are two hundred of those black things already through that Door and taking people out of their beds on Lanthorn, and there's more coming, and I looked through my glass this morning before they came out and I saw something behind them. In the black. Behind the Door." She was very quiet. "Something big. Too big to come through. Not yet. Waiting for the Door to open wider."

"The receiver," I said.

"The receiver. The thing that sent the herald and the tenders. The thing that's *coming*. And when the Door's wide enough, it'll come through, and every one of those pods they're filling on Lanthorn will be carried here, to this station, to the collection depot, and loaded onto it. And then it'll go back the way it came." She looked at me. "And we'll never see any of them again."

The hall was very quiet. The children had stopped their game. Somewhere among them, the baby from Number Two had gone to sleep.

"So we shut it," I said.

Haddie didn't answer.

"We go to the Spider," I said, "to Gallows, whatever it is, and we find whatever it was that held the Door, and we shut it. Before the big one comes through. We shut it on them."

"With what? We don't know what's there. We don't know if anything's there. It's been dead for longer than there's been a Lanthorn."

"So we go and look."

"In what? The *Mendicant*'s got two days of breath in her tanks, Absalom. I checked. The Door's five days out at a steady burn. And everything that burns breath in this system belongs to Kask, and Kask belongs to *that*." She jabbed a finger at the herald. "Where are you going to get breath?"

"The Kettles."

"The Kettles are Kask's too."

"The Kettles are anybody's who'll pay." I was already standing. "There's a breath-seller on the Number Three Kettle who used to sell to the reeves under the counter, when Kask's rations ran short. He owes me. I'll get enough for the run there and back."

"And the children?" said Wren.

I looked at her. She was standing at the end of the table with the water bulb in her burned hand, looking at me — the gangway look, the chin-up look — and behind her, under the strung lamps, in the curve of the great ribbed vault, forty-odd children sat among the fallen numbers of something none of us understood, and looked at me too.

"*The fixed asset known as Hesper High Station,*" said Haddie quietly, "*is designated the collection depot.*"

"I know."

"They're bringing everybody here, Absalom. Kask knows this place better than you do. She'll be here inside two days to get it ready. Her reeves. Those things."

"I know," I said. "So you'll take them deeper."

"Deeper where?"

"Into the core. Below this. There's a gate at the end of this hall, behind the plates, and a stair, and below it there's rooms nobody's ever picked because the stair's half fallen and the air's gone. I've been down there once. There's a place with seats." I looked at Haddie. "Round corridors. Old. You can seal them. I'll leave you the suit patches and the spare scrubber. It'll be cold. But they won't look there first. They'll fill the docks and the halls and the rings first. It'll take them days to get down that far."

"And you'll be back."

"In two days," I said. "With breath. And then we'll take everybody we can carry, and go to Gallows, and see."

Haddie looked at me over her spectacles. Then she closed the oldest Count-book, very carefully, and held it against her chest.

"Two days," she said.

"Two days."

Wren didn't say anything. She just turned away and went back down the hall to the children, and began, in a low voice, to tell them to get their boots.

* * *

I took the *Mendicant* out of the dead dock an hour later, alone, with the drive banked down and her lights dark. I looked back once, as I cleared the Hulk's outer ring. I don't know why. Habit, maybe. I've always looked back.

The station hung there against the moon the way it always had, black and dead and enormous, spokes and rings and towers, a great dark chandelier somebody had dropped and never swept up. And beyond it, out past Hesper's limb, low against the stars — so far off it was hardly more than a speck, so small that if I hadn't known to look I'd never have seen it — something was moving. Several somethings. Tiny glints, catching the faint far light of Vigil.

Coming this way.
