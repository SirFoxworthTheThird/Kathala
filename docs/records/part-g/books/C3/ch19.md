# Chapter 19: Gallows

*The Bond · 2431*

They hit the cloud tops at nine hours and forty minutes, and Tarrow took them in its hand.

Maren had been inside the giant a thousand times. She had never come into it like this — not sinking, slow and careful, a fat armoured seed in the *Gannet*'s cocoon, but falling, at a speed she did not have a word for, in a tender that had never been built to touch anything thicker than vacuum. The *Old Patience* went into the upper decks nose-first in a sheet of white fire that wrapped the forward glass and turned it into a window onto the inside of a furnace. The whole ship screamed. Not the hull note she knew from the *Gannet*; something higher and worse, a shriek that came from everywhere at once, every plate and rivet and weld in the old tender's frame crying out together. The straps across Maren's chest went tight and then tighter, and the world went red at the edges and then grey.

"Talk to me," said Dov.

He was flying with his teeth bared. She could see them in the furnace-light, his lips pulled back, his bearded jaw clenched so hard that the cords stood out in his neck. His hands on the yoke were moving in tiny precise motions, never more than a finger's width, and his eyes were on the one instrument on the board that was still telling the truth, a little dial that showed the angle of their fall against the giant's curve.

"Six hundred," she said. "Down."

"Lower."

"We're at six hundred, Dov, the hull—"

"The hull'll do what it does. I need eight." His voice was quite calm. "Your mother and I went to seven-eighty. Half-loaded. That's why we only got a day and a half." He moved the yoke. "We're full-loaded with a skimmer in the cradle. I need eight. Read me the decks."

She read him the decks. She had read the giant all her life; she knew what the cloud did, how it thickened, where the chimneys stood and where the sinks fell. She called them out to him as they came up on the scope through the fire — *chimney at your ten, big one, stay off it; shear at seven-twenty, it'll grab your tail; sink under you, it's soft, it's fine, ride it* — and he flew what she said without a word, the way he had flown over her for eighteen years, a thread of laser-light from the top of the world. Seven hundred. Seven-forty. The shriek of the hull dropped into a groan she could feel in her spine. Seven-eighty. Something let go somewhere aft with a bang like a gun, and a red light came on above Dov's head, and he did not look at it.

"Eight," he said. "That's eight. Light the *Gannet*."

"What?"

"Light her! In the cradle! Everything she's got! I need her engines on top of mine, girl, *now*—"

She lit the *Gannet*. She had warmed the skimmer's systems on the run in from Hesper, as he'd told her, and her board was on the co-seat console, and Maren put her hand on it and opened the throttles of her own ship from the cockpit of his, and felt the kick of it through the cradle clamps, through the frame, through her own body — two ships' drives together, at the bottom of the giant's well, pushing.

The *Old Patience* went up out of Tarrow like a stone out of a sling.

* * *

When she could see again, they were in the dark.

The forward glass was crazed in a long star-shaped crack across its lower half. There was a smell of burning in the cockpit, and a smell under it of struck matches, which meant a breath line somewhere had gone. The red light above Dov's head was still on, and three more had joined it. And behind them, on the aft scope, filling it, was the giant — not a sky now but a planet, a vast banded ball of bruise and umber falling away behind them, and on its face, where they had gone in, a long pale scar in the cloud tops like a scratch on skin, already beginning to heal.

Dov was coughing. He was bent over the yoke with his arm across his mouth, and the cough went on and on, a deep wet tearing sound that she had heard in the clinic on Level Nine and in her own lungs on bad nights. When it stopped he sat back slowly in the pilot's seat and took his arm away from his mouth and looked at the sleeve, and then wiped it on his knee.

"Well," he said thickly. "That's done."

"How fast?"

He looked at the board. He did a sum in his head; she watched his lips move. Then he laughed, and it turned into the cough again, and when that was done he said: "Three days and nine hours. Give or take." He looked at her. "Your mother'd have been sick with envy."

"The hull?"

"The hull," said Dov, "has docked its last docking but one. She'll get us there. She'll put us on a collar, if I'm gentle. And after that I'd not take her anywhere I couldn't walk home from." He reached up and turned off the red lights above his head, one after another, with a fingertip. "We'll not need her after that, will we?"

Maren didn't answer.

* * *

The run took three days and nine hours, and it was the longest time she had ever spent with Dov Arkwright without a bottle between them.

He did not drink. There was Ostrander's in the locker; she had seen it. He did not touch it. He flew, and when the flying did not need him he slept in the pilot's seat with his chin on his chest and his breath rattling, and when he woke he flew again. He ate a little. He coughed a great deal. On the second day she saw that what he was coughing into his sleeve was red, and he saw her see it, and neither of them said anything.

They did not talk about the docks. They did not talk about Hatch or Lucan or the chit he had dropped out of the side vent into the dark. She found, somewhere on the first day, that the thing in her chest that had made her hit him three times in the face had not gone away but had gone very quiet and very still, like water at the bottom of a sump, and that she could look at him across the cockpit without it moving. She did not know what that meant. She did not try to find out.

On the second night he talked about her mother.

Not much. Not the story of her birth; she had heard that. Small things. The way Ada Tull had sung the breathers' hymn on the dive line, flat and loud and terrible, and always got the third line wrong. The way she had once brought up a seam so rich the tally-man had refused to believe it and sent the bottles back to be weighed again, three times, and Ada had stood at the rail and watched him do it with her arms folded and said, every time the number came up the same, *count what's owed*. The way she had cut Maren's hair herself with a scoop knife, badly, every month, and said it was because barbers cost marks and was secretly proud of the result.

"She was a terror," said Dov. "You're worse."

"I'm not."

"You're worse." He looked at the forward glass, at the long crack across it, and at the dark beyond. "She'd never have done this. She'd have wanted to. She'd have sat here and done every sum and known it came out. And then she'd have turned round and gone home and dived the next morning, because that's what you did." He was quiet a moment. "She never once in her life did a thing she couldn't take back."

"And I am?"

"You're going to shut a door on thirty thousand people, girl," said Dov gently, "that nobody will be able to open for a very long time. I'd call that a thing you can't take back." He turned his head and looked at her. "I'm not saying don't. I'm saying I know what it costs. I watched your face when you said it to me, that night in this seat. I know you've done the sum."

"It's not a sum."

"No?"

"No." She looked at her hands. Her right was strapped, the two broken knuckles swollen and black under the binding. In her left, where it had been for three days, was the key. "A sum's when you weigh one thing against another and see which is heavier. This isn't that." She closed her hand on it. "This is just what's owed."

Dov looked at her.

"Your mother said that," he said at last. "To me. The night before she died, in the clinic, when I came to see her. She couldn't talk much by then. But she held my hand and she said, *Dov. Count what's owed.* And I said what you say. *And not a mark more.* And she shook her head at me, and said it again. *Count what's owed.* And then she went to sleep." He looked away. "I never knew what she meant. Not till that night you said it to me in this seat, with your face like hers. *Her. Not the bond. Her.*" He wiped his eyes with his sleeve, the clean one. "She meant you, girl. She was asking me to look after you. And I sold you for a seat."

The *Patience* hummed round them.

"You're flying me now," said Maren.

"I'm flying you to die." He said it without self-pity. "I can count too."

She didn't answer that. There wasn't anything to say. After a while, Dov went to sleep, with his chin on his chest and his breath rattling, and she sat in the co-seat and watched the dark ahead, where the Throat was, and held the key.

* * *

They saw it on the third day.

It came up out of the dark ahead like a moon rising: a great round place in the sky where the stars were wrong. Not smeared — not yet, not at this distance — but different. A disc of other stars, brighter and closer together and a different colour, a faint blue-white, set into the dull red-lit black of Vigil's sky like a coin in a wall. And as they came closer, over the hours, it grew; and the stars round its rim began to bend, to curve inward toward it in long arcs, like the grain round a knot in wood.

"There it is," said Dov softly. "The Throat."

She had seen it all her life on feeds — a small bright circle, the colony's one door, the place where the convoys came from and went to. She had never seen it like this, with her own eyes, growing in a cracked window. She had never seen what was through it.

Light. That was what was through it. Not much — a glow, faint and blue, the light of a star that was not Vigil, coming through the Throat from wherever the other mouth opened, so that the disc of other stars was faintly lit from behind, like a window at night. It was the colour, she thought, of the sky in the pictures Matron Brask had shown the wards. The colour Pell had been promised.

And at its rim, small and dark against that blue — so small she could cover it with her thumb on the glass — hung Gallows.

It was a spine. A long dark bar of station laid along the Throat's edge, lit with a few small working lights. And from it, at four points round the Throat's rim, on tethers so long and so fine she could only see them where they caught the blue, hung the pins: four great cylinders, each the size of a refinery head, quite still, holding the door of the world open.

"The Hanged Men," said Dov. "That's what the tender crews used to call them. When I was a lad. Before the Combine stopped letting us fly out this far." He looked at the scope. "And there's the hangman."

She saw it. A sharp bright point near Gallows, off its far end, where the spine's docking ring was. A ship. Grey, lean, with the H-V crest on its flank and its gun ports open.

"*Indemnity,*" said Dov. "She's been here a day, by the look of her. She'll have seen us since breakfast." He reached for the band switch. "Time I did some talking."

* * *

He talked for an hour. Maren sat beside him and listened and did not say a word, and she understood, listening, for the first time in her life, what Dov Arkwright had been before the bottle.

"*Indemnity, Indemnity, this is the contract tender* Old Patience *out of Number Four head, Hesper. I've got your thief.*" His voice was rough and cheerful and slightly drunk. "*The bond who did the Factor in his own study. Tull, H-one-one-eight-seven-three. Breather. I've got her trussed in my cradle like a Feast Day bird and I've got the thing she took off him. I want my reward and I want my seat. Over.*"

There was a long pause on the band. Then a clipped voice, a man's, cold.

"*Old Patience, this is Captain Rennick, Indemnity. Identify your cargo.*"

"*I just did, Captain. Bond Tull and the Factor's key. She came to me in the ice two nights back wanting a boat — wanted me to take her out here to the Throat. God knows why. Mad as a sink.*" A laugh. "*I took her money and her boat and her word and then I put her in my cradle with a scoop line round her and turned for home. And then I thought, Arkwright, the* Grace*'s going out past Gallows. The Factor'll want his key back before he goes, won't he? And he'll want to say thank you to the man who brings it him.*" His voice dropped, sly. "*I had a seat promised me once, Captain. Forty-one, Deck Two. Somebody on your dock said I couldn't have it. I'd like to talk to the Factor about that.*"

Another pause. Longer.

"*Hold your position, Old Patience,*" said Rennick. "*I'm referring this to the* Grace."

"*You do that, Captain. I'll put the kettle on.*"

He clicked off the band and sat back. His hands, Maren saw, were shaking very slightly on the arms of the pilot's seat. His face was grey.

"He'll ask Castellane," she said.

"He'll ask Castellane." Dov wiped his mouth. "And the *Grace* is — what — eighteen hours behind us now? So the message goes out, and the Factor reads it, and the answer comes back. Light lag's a few minutes at this range. Say a quarter of an hour, with the thinking."

"And Castellane will say what?"

Dov looked at her.

"He'll say yes," he said. "Won't he? You've seen him. He wants that key more than he wants anything in the world. He can't close the door behind him without it. He's going to come past Gallows in eighteen hours with four hundred kids in his hold and every officer in the colony, and he's going to want to stop and put that key in the lock and set the timer and go through and *shut it*. Close the account. That's the whole of him." He laughed weakly. "He'll say *yes, Captain, let the drunk dock at Gallows and hold them there for me, and I'll collect it myself when I pass*. Because he doesn't think we can add."

The band crackled, eighteen minutes later.

"*Old Patience, Indemnity.*" Rennick's voice was even colder. "*You're cleared to dock at Gallows, ring two. Hold your prisoner and the item aboard your vessel. Do not enter the station. A detail from* Indemnity *will take custody. The Factor will collect the item on his passage. Acknowledge.*"

"*Acknowledged, Captain,*" said Dov happily. "*Very kind. Ring two. I'll be gentle.*"

He clicked off the band. He looked at Maren.

"How long," she said, "for his detail to cross?"

"From where she's sitting? Suited, on a line, with a squad?" He looked at the scope. "Forty minutes. If they're quick. Thirty if they're frightened of him."

"And the inversion?"

"I don't know," said Dov. "Your clerk said ten minutes, once it starts. I don't know how long it takes to start." He reached forward and took hold of the yoke. "Best not to dawdle."

* * *

He put the *Old Patience* on Gallows' ring two so gently that she did not feel the collar take. He had said he would be gentle, and he was. It was, she thought afterward, the most beautiful piece of flying she had ever seen, and nobody but her would ever know it had been done: an old drunk with blood on his sleeve and a cracked hull and three red lights burning over his head, kissing a dying tender onto a docking ring at the edge of the world with the Combine's guns watching him do it.

When the seals took he let go of the yoke and sat back.

"Go on," he said.

She was already out of the seat. She had her suit on, her breather's suit, with the helmet clipped to her belt and the key in the sealed thigh pocket where she had always kept her ration chit, and her half of the steel disc on its cord against her skin under all of it. She stopped in the cockpit door and looked back at him.

"Come with me."

"Can't." He was coughing again, quietly. "Somebody's got to talk to the captain. Tell him you're still trussed in the cradle. Tell him I'm making tea. Buy you a bit of time." He looked up at her. "Go on, girl. You've got forty minutes and a station to find your way round."

She did not move.

"*Go,*" said Dov Arkwright, the way Lucan had said it, and she went.

* * *

Gallows was empty.

She had not known what to expect. A crew, maybe, who would have to be fought; marines; Combine officers at their consoles. There was no one. The station had been stripped. She came through the docking ring into a long corridor that ran down the spine, lit only by the working lights, and it was bare — no crates, no gear, no signs of life — and she understood after a moment that the station's crew had already gone. They would have gone through ahead with the *Surety*, the pathfinder escort that had left Hesper two days before the *Grace*. Lucan had told her: *six of them, on the list for Schedule A.* Gallows had been left running on its own, holding the Throat open for the convoy's passage, with nobody aboard at all.

She ran. Down the spine, past doors standing open on empty rooms, toward the centre of the station where the master console would be. Lucan had drawn it for her, once, on the back of a tally slip in the caretakers' storeroom, from a plan he had seen in the Factor's office: *centre of the spine, a round room, a window onto the mouth.*

The round room was there. Its door stood open. She went in, and stopped.

The window filled one whole wall of it, floor to ceiling, curved, and through it, filling it, was the Throat.

She had seen it from the *Patience*. That was nothing. From here, at its rim, it was not a disc in the sky; it was a hole in the world, and it was full of light. Blue-white, soft, the light of another star — so close, it seemed, that she could have put her hand out through the glass and into it. She could see, through the Throat's mouth, a sky. Not stars: a sky. A great curved glowing reach of pale blue haze, the edge of some world on the far side lit by its sun, so bright after a whole life under Vigil's dull red that it made her eyes water. And round its rim the stars of Vigil's sky bent and curved inward in long arcs, and the four Hanged Men hung motionless on their threads, holding it open.

A sky. A blue one. For a moment she could not move at all.

Then she made herself turn from it, to the console.

It was a long desk of grey metal under the window, with dark screens set in it and banks of switches, and in the middle of it, under a hinged guard like the guard on the *Gannet*'s dump lever, a lock: a small round socket with a spiral groove cut into its inner wall. She opened the guard. She took the key out of her thigh pocket with her good hand and fitted its point into the socket, and felt the spiral catch, and turned it.

It went in like a breath going into a lung. All the way, smooth, until the ceramic sat flush with the steel of the console. And the console woke.

The screens lit, one after another, green and amber. Lettering came up on them in the Combine's square hand, lines of it, status and readings she did not understand. And then, on the central screen, a single panel, very plain.

> PASSAGE CONTROL — FACTOR AUTHORITY ACCEPTED
>
> PIN STATE: OPEN (HOLD)
>
> SELECT:
> HOLD · INVERT · RESTORE

She put her finger on INVERT.

The screen changed.

> INVERSION SEQUENCE REQUESTED.
> ALL PINS WILL REVERSE SHEAR. PASSAGE WILL COLLAPSE.
> ESTIMATED TIME TO COLLAPSE: 9 MIN 40 SEC FROM CONFIRMATION.
> COLLAPSE IS NOT REVERSIBLE BY HOLD.
> RESTORATION FROM COLLAPSED STATE REQUIRES PRIMING (HEAVY BREATH, MIN. 4 UNITS) AND FACTOR AUTHORITY.
> ESTIMATED RESTORATION TO PASSAGE: 380–420 STANDARD YEARS AT MINIMUM SHEAR.
>
> CONFIRM?

She read it twice. Three hundred and eighty years. Four hundred. She read it a third time, and then she stopped reading it, because she knew what it said, and because she had known since the latrine at the tally house what it would say.

She looked out through the window at the blue sky beyond the Throat.

Somewhere behind her — eighteen hours behind her now, sixteen, fifteen — a grey ship a kilometre and a quarter long was coming across the system with four hundred and twelve children in its hold. One of them was in the ward deck. Deck Nine, forward. One of them could count.

She pressed CONFIRM.

* * *

Nothing happened, at first. She had thought there would be a sound. There was only a change in the light on the screens — green going to amber, a number appearing in the corner of the central panel and beginning to fall. *9:39. 9:38.* And through the window, very faintly, the four Hanged Men stirring on their threads, the way a sleeper stirs.

She looked at the falling number. Then she looked along the console, and found what Lucan had said would be there — *every station keeps a log, every console has a recorder* — a small panel to the left of the lock, with a grille and a single switch. She did not know if anyone would ever hear it. She did not know if it would last. She thumbed the switch, and a little light came on beside the grille, red, and she leaned down to it with her helmet in her hand and her broken hand against the console's edge, and talked to her daughter.

"Pell. It's Mum."

*9:12.*

"I don't know if this'll ever reach you. I don't know if anyone will ever come here again. I hope they do. I hope it's you." She stopped. Started again. "I said I'd tell you. You remember? At the glass. I said I'd find out who was giving what to who, and I'd tell you, even if it was bad. So this is me telling you.

"You know about Brannock. You know about the Scholarship. You worked it out before I did, you clever thing. All four hundred and twelve of you, and the price of every one. What you don't know is what they were going to do after. When the *Grace* went through, they were going to stop here, at this station, and turn this key, and shut the Throat behind them so the rest of us couldn't ever follow. Thirty thousand people. They were going to shut us in the ice and forget us."

*8:21.*

"So I've shut it first. That's what I've done. I've shut it in front of them instead of behind. I'm sitting here at the console and the number's going down and in about eight minutes it'll be shut and the *Grace* will be on our side of it. On *your* side. With you on it, and the other children, and the Factor and all his officers. They're not going to Corvid. Nobody's going to Brannock. Nobody's going anywhere." She swallowed. "You're staying with us.

"I'm sorry about the sky. I can see it, Pell. Through the window. It's blue. It's — it's blue, love. Like the pictures. I'm sorry. I'd have given you that if I could. I couldn't give you that and keep you, and I chose keep."

*7:05.*

"They'll tell you things about me. After. The Factor will, and his people. They'll say I was mad, or wicked, or that I did it out of spite, or for the union. Some of it'll be true. I hit the Factor with a knife handle and I'd do it again. But the rest, you'll know. You can count. Count what's owed, love. Count it right. Not their way — not the bond, not the marks. Count *you*." Her voice did something and she let it. "My mum said that, the day I was born. Dov told me. *Look at her. Count what's owed.* I didn't know what she meant until I had you.

"I've got my half of the tag. I'm holding it now. You hold yours. One day somebody'll put them together."

*5:40.*

Through the window, the Hanged Men had begun to move. Not stirring now — swinging, slowly, on their long threads, out from the Throat's rim and back, out and back, like pendulums. And they were glowing. A faint deep red at first, like the cracks in a cooling forge, and then brighter.

And on the band, faint, from the *Patience* at ring two, she heard Dov's voice.

"*—no, Captain, she's still in the cradle, I've just checked, I don't know what your instruments are telling you but I'm sitting here with a cup of tea and—*"

And Rennick's, cutting across it, very cold and very loud. "*Arkwright, the pins are inverting. The pins are inverting. What has she done? Arkwright—*"

Maren leaned back to the grille.

"Whoever hears this," she said. "If it isn't her. If it's somebody else, a long time after. The console says it can be opened again. RESTORE. It says it needs breath and the key and three or four hundred years." She took a breath. "So if you've come here, maybe you've got three or four hundred years. Maybe everybody's dead who'd sell a child. I don't know. I don't know what you are. But if you are — if there's nobody left on either side of it who'd do that — then open it. Let them go and see the sky." She closed her eyes. "And if there is, don't. Not till there isn't. Don't let them take the children."

*3:10.*

"*Arkwright, you lying bastard—*"

"*Ah, Captain,*" said Dov's voice, mild and rough, through the band, "*you've found me out. Well. I always was a poor liar. Ask anybody.*" A cough, long and wet. "*Tell you what, while we've a minute. Do you know the breathers' hymn? You'll not have heard it, on a frigate. Goes like this.*"

And he began to sing.

*Down in the red where the weight comes on—*

He sang it flat and loud and terrible, the way her mother had, the way Hatch had on the docks, and he got the third line wrong, the way Ada always had, on purpose, she thought, and she stood at the console with her hand on its edge and listened.

*—down where the light's a bruise—*

She felt it before she heard it. A jolt through the deck, through her boots, through the console under her hand: a deep hard shudder that ran down the whole length of the spine. And then the sound, coming down the corridor from the docking ring, a long rolling boom like the head's intakes starting, and then a scream of air.

The band went silent.

Not crackle. Not static. Silent, the way a held breath stops.

*2:00.*

She did not go to look. She knew. She stood at the console in her suit with her helmet in her hand and the alarms beginning to sound all through the station, and the air in the round room beginning to move — a wind, a pull, toward the door, toward the corridor, toward whatever the *Indemnity*'s guns had opened in the spine where ring two had been — and she put her helmet on and sealed it, one-handed, the way she had sealed it ten thousand times in the *Gannet*'s cradle, and turned back to the window.

The Hanged Men were burning.

All four. White now, white-hot, swinging on their threads in great slow arcs, and the Throat between them was changing. The blue sky through its mouth was still there, still glowing, but the mouth was getting smaller. Closing. Not like a door. Like an eye. The bent stars round its rim were pouring inward now, faster and faster, the long arcs tightening into spirals, and the blue at the centre shrank, and shrank, a disc and then a coin and then a bright blue bead.

*0:40.*

On the scope beside the window, far off down the system, a light. The *Grace*. Too far to see. Fifteen hours out. Coming on, braking already for Gallows, for the key she thought was waiting for her. On this side.

*0:10.*

The blue bead flickered.

And went out.

* * *

It was very quiet, afterward.

The Hanged Men stopped burning. She watched them through the window over a long time — she did not know how long — go from white to red to a dull deep glow, and then to dark, and hang still on their threads again, round the rim of a place in the sky where there was nothing at all. Not black. Not stars. Nothing. Her eye would not stay on it.

The station's air was gone. She knew that from her suit's board, and from the silence, and from the frost that had begun to form on the inside of the window in long feathered lines. The breach at ring two had taken it all, down the spine, out into the dark. She had her suit's air. A breather's suit carried eight hours for a dive and an hour's reserve. She had come aboard with — she looked — six hours and twenty minutes.

The marines did not come. She had thought they would. She stood at the console for a long time waiting for them, with her helmet sealed and her good hand on the key, ready to do — she did not know what. They did not come. After a while the band crackled, faintly, through her helmet speaker, on the open channel, and she heard Rennick's voice, flat and dead.

"*Indemnity to* Grace. *The passage is closed. I say again, the passage is closed. Collapse confirmed at—*" a time "*—by inversion from Gallows master console. The* Surety *had cleared. The* Grace *is—*" A pause. "*The* Grace *is on this side, sir.*"

Silence. Then, faint, much fainter, with the few minutes' lag of distance, another voice. Old and grave and very quiet. She knew it.

"*Understood, Captain.*" A long pause. "*Recall your detail. Rejoin the convoy. Leave the station.*"

"*Sir, the bond—*"

"*Leave her,*" said Imre Castellane. "*Leave her to it. No one is to go to Gallows. Do you understand me, Captain? No one. Not now. Not ever.*" Another pause, longer. When he spoke again his voice had changed; it was the voice of a man doing a sum, very carefully, that had just come out in a way he had never once imagined it could. "*It's closed.*"

The band clicked off.

* * *

She sat down, after that, because her legs would not hold her any more. There was a chair at the console, a plain metal chair with a straight back, and she sat in it with her back to the room and her face to the window and the dead dark place in the sky where the blue had been.

On the scope, very small, very far, the light of the *Grace* had stopped braking. As she watched — it took a long time; it took, she thought, an hour, two — it began to turn. Slowly, the way a great ship turns, a kilometre and a quarter of grey hull swinging its nose round in the dark. Away from Gallows. Away from the closed Throat. Back across the system, toward Tarrow, toward Hesper, toward the ice.

Home.

She watched it all the way round.

Then she reached over and thumbed the log switch again, and the little red light came on beside the grille.

"It's turning, Pell," she said. "The *Grace*. I can see it. It's turning round. You're going home."

She did not say anything else for a while. She sat and watched the light on the scope. Her suit's board said four hours. Three and a half. Her broken hand had stopped hurting, or she had stopped noticing. The frost on the window had grown into a forest of white ferns, and through it the dark place where the Throat had been hung in the sky like a closed eye, and round it the Hanged Men hung on their threads, quite still.

She reached into the neck of her suit, awkwardly, with her good hand, and found the cord, and drew it out: her half of the steel disc, H-11873, SURETY, with its broken toothed edge. She held it up in front of the window, against the dark, the way she had held it up to the Nursery glass once a month for six years, and looked at the jagged line where the other half should fit.

"Count what's owed," she said, to nobody.

Her daughter's voice answered her, at the glass, a long way off and six years ago. *And not a mark more.*

She let the tag fall into her lap, still in her good hand, on its cord. And she lifted the other — the broken one, strapped and swollen in its glove, that had stopped hurting — and laid it flat on the console, beside the key, beside the little red light of the log, on the cold grey metal under the frosted window.

And knocked three times.
