# Chapter 16: What Dov Sold

*The Bond · 2431*

"He doesn't sleep," said Lucan. "That's the first thing."

It was the middle of the night on the twenty-second day, in the caretakers' storeroom, with the feed screen dark and a single hand-lamp on the floor between them. Lucan had a slate on his knees with a plan of the Residence on it, drawn by his own hand from memory, and he was talking very fast and very low, the way he had talked in the latrine at the tally house, as though the words had been piling up behind his teeth for days.

"Four hours a night, maybe. Never more. He reads. He sits at the window in his study and looks at the *Grace* and reads, or does sums, or just looks. His staff say he's done it for nineteen years. Some of the officers think it's a sign of genius." He laughed, a short dry sound. "I think he just can't stop counting."

"Where?"

"Top level. The Residence spire. The study's on the outer wall, the bedroom behind it, a dressing room behind that." He touched the slate. "Two marines on the corridor door, all night. Nobody gets past them. But the dressing room backs onto the service way."

"What service way?"

"The cleaning drones. There's a crawlway behind every suite in the Residence, for the drones that do the linen and the floors. It runs up the spire from the laundry. The hatches open on a staff palm." He held up his hand, pale and narrow in the lamplight. "The caretaker detail was given staff access to every service way in the Residence yesterday. Somebody has to clean it after they've gone." He looked at his own palm as though it belonged to someone else. "It never occurred to anyone to take it off me. I'm staying. Why would I want to go anywhere?"

Maren looked at the plan. At the little square he had drawn for the dressing room, and the line of the crawlway behind it, and the study beyond with its window.

"He wears it," she said. "The key. All the time."

"All the time."

"Then I'll take it off him."

Lucan looked at her for a long moment in the lamplight.

"And then?" he said.

"Then I need a boat."

"There's nothing on High that can get you to Gallows. Every launch on the station's tethered into the convoy's departure plan; they'd be flagged the second they undocked." He hesitated. "There's only one boat I know of that could make it. That you could trust to fly it."

She didn't answer.

"Maren—"

"I know," she said. "Call him."

* * *

He called from the caretakers' office down the corridor, on a line the Combine used for tally-house queries, and she stood in the doorway and listened. He did not say much. He said that there was a breather on High who needed collecting. He said service lock nine, on the Residence's lower ring, at the fourth bell. He said: *She says, bring the* Patience *fuelled for a long run.*

There was a pause on the line. Then Dov's voice, thin and far and cracked, said, "*All right,*" and nothing else, and the line clicked off.

Lucan put the handset down very carefully.

"He didn't ask why," he said.

"No."

"He didn't ask anything."

"No," said Maren. "He didn't."

* * *

The crawlway was a square metal tube half a metre across, lit every few metres by a tiny green guide-light for the drones, and it went up the Residence spire in a long slanting climb with rungs set into its floor. It smelled of hot dust and laundry soap. Maren went up it on her hands and knees, behind Lucan's narrow back and the soles of his staff shoes, for what felt like an hour. At every junction he stopped, and checked the slate, and put his palm to a plate, and a hatch slid open in front of them with a sigh, and they went on.

At the top he stopped, and turned, awkwardly, in the narrow space, and looked back at her with the green guide-light on his face.

"This is it," he whispered. "The dressing room. I'll wait here. If anyone comes I'll—" He stopped. "I don't know what I'll do."

"Wait," she said.

He put his palm to the plate.

* * *

The dressing room was dark, and full of grey coats. They hung in a row along one wall, a dozen of them, all cut exactly the same, and she pushed between them in the dark like a diver pushing through weed and came out at a door that stood a hand's width ajar. Beyond it, a bedroom, also dark, with a bed that had not been slept in. And beyond that, through an open archway, light.

She had the knife in her hand. It was the breather's knife from her suit belt, a short heavy blade for cutting scoop lines, and she had never in her life held it like this, point forward, low. She went through the bedroom on the balls of her feet, and stopped at the archway, and looked.

The study was long and quiet and lined with shelves. At its far end was a window as tall as the room, and through it, filling it, lit along her flank by the docking lights of High, lay the *Grace*. Pell was in there. Somewhere behind that grey cliff of hull, in a ward deck, asleep or not asleep.

And in a chair by the window, with a slate on his knee and a cup on the table beside him, sat Imre Castellane, in his shirtsleeves, looking out at her.

He did not turn round.

"Mrs. Tull," he said. "I thought it might be you."

She did not move.

"Come in," said the Factor of Vigil. "I won't call anyone. I'd rather talk to you than have you shot in my bedroom. It's been a long day, and I've been sitting here wondering whether you'd come, and I find I'm rather glad you have." He turned, then, in the chair, and looked at her: at the knife, at her face, at the blood dried in her hair. His long grave face did not change. "You're hurt."

"Give me the key."

"Ah." He did not touch it. It lay at his throat on its fine steel chain, against the open collar of his shirt, white ceramic and grooved steel. "Yes. I wondered if Mr. Vey had told you about that. He's in the crawlway, I suppose. He's a decent young man. I should have sent him to Corvid; I'd have liked to see what he'd have made of himself." He tilted his head. "Do you understand what it does?"

"Yes."

"Then you understand that it's no use to you. Let me explain, so that we're clear." He set the slate aside, gently, on the table by the cup. "When the *Grace* passes Gallows tomorrow, I was going to set the pins to invert behind her. You know that. Two hours after we cleared, the passage would close. Without the key, I can't do that. Very well. Then the pins will go on doing what they've been doing, which is failing — slowly, expensively, without fresh breath — and in eighteen months, perhaps two years, the passage will narrow and close on its own." He spread his clean hands. "So you see. All you will have done, by taking it from me, is give the Sump eighteen months of something to hope for. Eighteen months of thinking someone might build a ship. Eighteen months of looking at the Throat." He looked at her with what seemed like real pity. "That's not a kindness, Mrs. Tull. I promise you. I've seen colonies hope."

She crossed the study in four steps. He did not move. She put the point of the breather's knife under the fine steel chain, against the soft old skin of his throat, and he looked up at her past it, perfectly calm.

"Pell's in the ward deck," he said. "If you were wondering. Deck Nine, forward. She asked to see you once more, this evening. I said no. I'm sorry. It would only have made it worse." His voice was very gentle. "She'll be all right, you know. She'll count beautifully. With an aptitude like hers she'll be a gang-tally inside a year. She'll never go into the galleries at all."

Maren cut the chain.

It parted with a tiny sound, a click, like a tally wand on a bottle. The key slid down the front of his shirt and she caught it in her left hand, and it was heavier than she had thought it would be, and warm from his skin.

Castellane looked down at the place where it had been, and then up at her.

"There's a thing about debts, Mrs. Tull," he said, "that your people have never understood. You think they die with the debtor. They don't. They outlive everyone. They outlive the debtor and the creditor and the clerk who wrote them down. They're the most durable thing human beings have ever made." He smiled, very slightly. "You can take that key to the bottom of Tarrow if you like. It won't change what's owed."

"Count what's owed," said Maren.

He looked at her. For the first time, something moved in his face — puzzlement, perhaps, or something like it, a man hearing a word in a language he thought he knew and finding it means something else.

"And not a mark more," said the Factor of Vigil politely, and she hit him across the side of the head with the hilt of the breather's knife, as hard as she could, and he went sideways out of the chair onto the floor of his study and lay there.

She did not check whether he was breathing. She tied his wrists with the sash of a grey coat from his own dressing room and his ankles with another and pushed a third into his mouth, and then she stood over him for a moment in the light from the long window, with the *Grace* lying beyond it like a cliff, and the key in her fist.

Then she went back through the coats to the crawlway.

* * *

They were three junctions down when the alarm went.

It was not loud. The Residence was not built for loud. It was a soft insistent chime that came from everywhere at once, through the walls of the crawlway, and under it a voice, calm and female, saying something Maren could not make out. And the green guide-lights in the crawlway, every one of them, at the same moment, turned red.

"He's awake," said Lucan, ahead of her. His voice was perfectly steady. "Or someone's found him. Keep moving."

They kept moving. Down the long slant of the crawlway, hand over hand, rung over rung, with the red lights pulsing round them and the chime going on and on. At the next junction Lucan put his palm to the plate, and it slid open, and they went through. At the next, the same. At the next he put his palm to the plate and nothing happened.

He stared at it.

"They've locked the staff access," he said. "They've locked the—" He put his palm to it again. Nothing. "No. No, no. They can't have locked all of it, it's a whole-system change, it'd take—" Again. And on the third try the plate went green, and the hatch sighed open, and he let out a breath like a sob. "Partial. They're locking it section by section. From the top down." He looked back at her over his shoulder. "We have to be faster."

They were faster. She did not know afterward how long it took; it felt like the whole night and it felt like no time at all. Rungs and red light and the chime, and Lucan's narrow back ahead of her, and his palm on plate after plate, and twice a plate that took three tries, and once, far above them, a sound of boots in the crawlway — someone coming in after them from the top, slowly, carefully, the way you go down a ladder in the dark.

The last hatch let out onto a service corridor on the Residence's lower ring. Lucan went through first. She heard him say *clear*, and came after him into a grey corridor lit red, and saw at the end of it, twenty metres off, the round armoured door of service lock nine with its little window, and beyond the window, against the black, the scarred grey nose of the *Old Patience*.

"Go," said Lucan.

They ran.

They were ten metres from the lock when the marines came round the corner behind them.

* * *

There were two of them. She heard them before she saw them — the clatter of boots, a voice shouting — and Lucan, beside her, did a thing she would not understand for a long time afterward. He did not run faster. He stopped. He turned round. He put himself in the corridor between her and the marines, with his arms a little out from his sides, the way you might stand to stop a child running into the road, and he said, quite loudly, in his flat clerk's voice:

"I'm staff. Caretaker detail. Lucan Vey, actuarial. Don't shoot."

They shot him.

She saw him jerk, and stagger, and not fall. She was at the lock. She had her hand on the plate and it would not open — red, red, the lock's plate pulsing red under her palm, because it was not her palm, because she was a bond and had no access to anything — and behind her she heard a second shot, and turned, and Lucan was coming down the corridor toward her. Not running. Walking, very carefully, with one hand pressed flat against his stomach and the other held out in front of him. His face was the colour of paper.

He reached the lock. He put his palm on the plate beside hers.

It went green. The door rolled open.

"Go," said Lucan Vey.

"Come on—"

"No." He was leaning against the frame. His palm was still on the plate. She saw that he was holding it there; that if he took it off, the door would close. "No. I'm staying. I was always staying." He smiled at her, a terrible, sweet, frightened smile, with blood on his teeth. "Somebody has to keep the account."

Behind him the marines were shouting. Something hit the wall beside the door and threw sparks.

"*Go,*" said Lucan, and she went.

She went through the lock and into the docking collar and the door rolled shut behind her, and through its little window, in the instant before the *Patience*'s outer hatch opened at her back and Dov's hands caught her and pulled her through, she saw him slide down the frame of the lock with his palm leaving a long dark smear on the plate, and sit, and lean his head back against the wall, and close his eyes.

* * *

Dov did not say anything. He pulled her through the hatch and slammed it and threw the collar release before she was on her feet, and the *Patience* fell away from High with a jolt that knocked her down again, and by the time she had got up and into the co-seat he had the old tender's nose pointed down into Hesper's shadow and the throttles open and the station falling away behind them on the aft scope, all its lights pulsing red.

She sat in the co-seat with the key in her fist and did not look at him.

He did not look at her either. He flew. He took the *Patience* down into the moon's shadow and round its limb, low, fast, through the clutter of ore tugs and relay buoys and old dead satellites that the Combine had been leaving in Hesper orbit for two hundred years, and nothing followed them. Nothing, she thought, would think it worth following. A bond and a drunk in an old tender, running for the dark. Where could they go?

On the console, the Sump's feed was running. Dov had it on low, the way he always did; she had never known him fly without it. At the first bell, as they came round Hesper's far side and the great banded curve of Tarrow came up over the moon's edge to fill the forward glass, the feed changed.

It showed a lock.

It was a plain lock, a cargo lock, somewhere on High; Maren did not know which. Its inner door was open, and inside it, small and grey in the bare white light, stood Agathe Hatch. She was in her cut-down habit. Her hands were tied in front of her. Her face was bruised down one side and her lip was split, and she stood very straight in the middle of the lock and looked at the camera with her pale steady nun's eyes, as though she could see through it — as though she could see every one of them, in every cell in the Sump, sitting in front of their feeds in the cold.

A voice off-screen read the charge. Inciting violence against officers of the Combine. Eleven dead. It was a clerk's voice, flat and quick, and Hatch did not seem to hear it.

When it was finished there was a pause, and the voice said, "Do you wish to speak?"

"Yes," said Hatch.

She looked into the camera.

"They've never once in two hundred years thought we could add," she said. Her voice was perfectly calm. "That's all. Add it up."

The inner door closed.

Maren did not watch the rest. She heard it — the long falling hiss of the lock cycling, on the feed's thin speaker — and she looked out through the forward glass at Tarrow, at the giant's banded face filling the whole of the sky, the colour of an old bruise, and did not look at the screen. When the hiss stopped she reached over and turned the feed off.

In the pilot's seat beside her, Dov Arkwright was weeping. Without a sound. His hands on the yoke were perfectly steady and his eyes were on the glass and the tears were running down his face into his collar, and he did not wipe them away.

"Thirteen," said Maren.

He did not answer.

"I counted," she said. "On the water. Every night. Fourteen of us. And on the docks this morning, I counted again. Who they'd taken. Who they'd shot. Who they'd known to look for." She did not look at him. "I came to thirteen. Every time. I came to thirteen and I stopped."

"Maren—"

"Tell me."

He told her.

* * *

He had gone to them after the first meeting on the water. After the vote. He had walked out of the Drowned Decks before anyone else — before the lamps had started going out along the catwalks — and gone up through the Sump to Level One, to the bond office, which was shut, and banged on its door until a marine came; and he had said that he had information for the Factor about the union, and that he wanted to trade it.

"I told them the strike date," he said. "The eighteenth. I told them about Hatch, which they knew. I told them about you." His voice was thick. "They said it didn't matter. They said they'd let you have the strike. That it suited them. They gave me a chit." He took it out of his breast pocket — a little square of grey plastic with the H-V crest on it, and a number — and dropped it on the console between them. "Passage on the *Grace*. Seat forty-one, Deck Two. For services. They said there'd be more if I brought them more."

Maren looked at the chit and did not touch it.

"And then Hatch told us about the docks," said Dov. "The shuttles. The signal. Who'd be on the crews." He was looking at the glass. "And I went back up. That night. After I'd done your sums for you on the back of a tally slip. I went back up and I told them all of it. Every name. Every berth. The hymn." He made a sound that was not a laugh. "I asked you what bell, and you told me, and I went and told them."

"Why?"

"Because I'm dying." He said it flatly. "Because I've got the rattle and I've had it for four years and Hatch says I've got another one, maybe, if I stop drinking, which I won't. Because I've spent fifty-five years in a hole in the ice watching Tulls go down into the red and come back up again and go down again, and I've never once seen a sky. A real one. A blue one." His voice cracked. "Castellane said the Inner Worlds had skies. He said it on the platform. I thought — just once. Just once before I go. I thought, I'll sit in seat forty-one on Deck Two and go through the Throat and I'll get off at Corvid and I'll find somewhere with a sky and I'll lie down under it and that'll be that." He wiped his face at last, with the back of one hand. "That's all. That's all it was."

"And this morning?"

"This morning I took the chit down to the convoy dock at the third bell," said Dov, "before the farewell, before any of you came up, with my bag packed. Thirty years of my life in one bag. And I showed it to the clerk at the desk." He looked at the chit on the console. "And the clerk looked at it, and looked it up, and called her supervisor. And the supervisor came, and looked at me, and laughed. Not cruelly. She just laughed, the way you'd laugh at a dog that's learned to open a door. And she said: *I'm sorry, Mr. Arkwright. The Factor regrets. You're a fixed asset.*"

The *Patience* hummed round them. Tarrow filled the glass.

"Fixed," said Dov softly. "That was all. I went back down to the head with my bag and sat in this seat until your clerk called." He turned his head and looked at her at last. "I came. I want you to know. I didn't have to come. I could have stayed in the ice and let you get shot on that station. I came."

She hit him.

She did not decide to. Her fist was moving before she knew it, across the space between the seats, and it took him on the side of the jaw and his head went sideways into the cockpit frame with a crack she felt in her own hand. She hit him again. He did not lift his arms. He sat in the pilot's seat with his hands still on the yoke and let her, and she hit him a third time and felt something in her knuckles give, and stopped, and sat back in the co-seat with her broken hand in her lap and her breath coming in great tearing sobs, like the rattle, like the giant breathing out.

"Farrant," she said. "Hatch. Lucan. Eleven on the docks. Ruth and Ade and Pym, tomorrow, the day after. *Pell*." She could hardly speak. "You held the light. You held the light when I was born."

"I know," said Dov.

"She said *count what's owed*. My mother. She held me up and said it, to you, and you never asked what she meant."

"I know."

"She meant *look at her*. She meant: this is what's owed. This. Not the bond. Not the Combine. *Her.*" She was shaking. "And you sold it for a seat."

"I know," said Dov Arkwright, with blood running from the corner of his mouth into his collar where the tears had gone. "I know, girl. I know."

They sat in the cockpit of the *Old Patience* with the giant filling the glass, and neither of them said anything for a long time.

* * *

It was the scope that broke it. A chime, from the console — a new contact, far behind them, at Hesper. Dov looked at it, out of long habit, and Maren saw his face change.

"There she goes," he said.

She looked. On the aft scope, very small, at the edge of Hesper's disc, a light had come on that was brighter than anything else in the moon's orbit — a long blue-white flare, steady, enormous, growing. The *Grace*'s drives. The convoy was leaving.

And ahead of it — well ahead of it, already far out from Hesper and moving very fast, on a line that ran straight across the system toward the Throat — a second light. Smaller. Hotter.

"*Indemnity*," said Dov. "The escort frigate. She's running ahead. She's running hard." He turned and looked at Maren. "Your Factor's not stupid. He's guessed where you're going, girl. He's sending her on to sit on Gallows and wait for you."

Maren opened her left hand. Her right was swelling, two knuckles broken, useless. In her left lay the key, white ceramic and grooved steel, still faintly warm. She looked at it.

Then she looked at Dov. At his bruised face, his wet beard, his steady hands on the yoke.

"How long?" she said. "To the limb."

He did not answer at once. He looked at her the way he had looked at her in this same cockpit three nights before, when she had asked him the same thing and he had already had the figures on his knee.

"Nine hours to the dive line," he said. "If I start now."

"Then start now."

"You're going to let me fly you?"

"I'm going to make you," said Maren. "You're the only pilot in the Sump who's ever taken a tender down the limb and lived. You and my mother. And she's dead." She closed her hand on the key. "So you'll fly. And you'll get me there. And then we'll see what's owed."

Dov Arkwright looked at her for a moment longer. Then he reached down and picked the grey chit up off the console — seat forty-one, Deck Two — and turned it over once in his fingers, and opened the side vent beside his seat, and dropped it out into the dark.

"Strap in," he said. "And put the *Gannet*'s systems on warm. You'll want her ready." He pushed the throttles forward, and the *Old Patience* shuddered all through her old frame and turned her nose down toward the giant. "Your mother never went past a thousand. She'd have hated this."

"She'd have loved it," said Maren, and strapped in.
