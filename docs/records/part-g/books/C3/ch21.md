# Chapter 21: Receipt

*The Door · 3190*

The receiver went past us on the second day out, and I'll tell you what it looked like, because I'm the only one who saw it go by close and is still alive to say.

It looked like nothing. That's the truth. It looked like a piece of the dark had come loose from the sky and was sliding across it. I'd seen the edge of it from the Hulk, coming through the Door — that long black curve catching the light — and I'd thought, *that's big*. I hadn't known. It passed us at a distance I'd guess at forty thousand kilometres, going the other way, toward Hesper and the depot, and it took two hours to go by. Two hours, at the speed it was moving. I sat in the *Mendicant*'s cockpit with the drive banked and every light off and watched the stars go out behind it and come back, go out and come back, for two hours, and in all that time I never once saw its shape. Only where the stars weren't.

Wren didn't watch. She sat in the co-seat with Haddie's book on her knees and Haddie's cracked spectacles on her nose, which were much too big for her and which she had to keep pushing up with one burned finger, and read. She'd been reading since the Hulk. She read the way Haddie used to, with her lips moving, one word at a time, going back when she got one wrong. Sometimes she'd say a word out loud — an old one, a square-lettered one — and look at me as though I might know what it meant. I never did.

"*Debtor,*" she said, when the receiver had gone by. "*Debtors.* It's on every page."

"I know that one."

"*Credit.*" She frowned at the page. "That's the other side. Haddie said. Debit's what you owe. Credit's what's owed to you." She pushed the spectacles up. "There isn't much of that."

"There never is," I said.

* * *

Kask's yacht stayed on our tail the whole way. She was faster than us — the *Open Hand* had been built for the Margrave's comfort and the Margrave's speed, and her drive was three times the size of mine — but we'd had half a day on her out of the Hulk, and I'd spent thirty years flying the dark between the habitats in a boat that didn't care if she got scratched, and I didn't give it back. By the fourth day she was eight hours behind us. Then six. When we came into the reaches round the Door, she was five.

The reaches had changed. Haddie used to say that, back before the breathing, the space round the Door had been wrong — that the old divers who'd run breath out to the far relays had called it the Shear, and talked about stars that swam and boats that got pushed sideways by nothing. I'd never been out that far. Nobody had, in my lifetime. And now, with the Door open, the Shear wasn't swimming any more. It was pouring. Every star round the Door's rim was sliding inward in a long bright arc, like water round a drain, and the *Mendicant* felt it: a pull, steady and deep, that I had to fly against every minute, the way you fly against a current. Twice we passed dead things in the dark — old mines, Wren said, reading the shapes on the scope; the oldest Count-books had pictures of them — and once a long drifting field of wreckage, old and cold, that might have been a boat once, a long time ago.

And ahead of us, getting bigger, the Door.

I'll not try to describe it. I've tried, since, and I can't. I'll tell you it was open, and that what was through it was light. Not starlight. Not the dull red of Vigil that I'd lived under my whole life. A pale blue-white glow, soft, enormous, coming through the Door from wherever the other side was, so bright after fifty-two years of red that I couldn't look at it straight and couldn't stop looking. And I'll tell you that in the middle of the glow, if you let your eye go soft, you could see the edge of something curved and hazy and pale. A world, maybe. Lit from the side by its sun.

"Is that a sky?" said Wren. Very quietly.

"I don't know."

"It's blue."

"I know."

She didn't say anything else.

And at the Door's rim, small and dark against all that light, with four long threads hanging down from it round the edge of the glow: the Spider. Gallows. A long thin spine of station, frosted white along its length. And the four things hanging from it on their threads — enormous now, close to, each the size of a hulk on Lanthorn — were glowing. Not brightly. A deep, steady, coal-red light, from inside, like the cracks in Kell's rock. The way a stove glows when it's been banked for the night and is still, very slowly, burning.

"They're alive," said Wren. "The legs. Look. They're doing something."

"They're holding it," I said. I don't know how I knew. "They're holding it open."

* * *

There were three docking rings on the spine. One was gone — torn away, a long time ago, the metal round it ragged and black and frosted over, an old wound. One was empty. And one, near the middle of the station, had a boat on it.

It was a little thing. A squat cargo skiff, not much bigger than the *Mendicant*'s cockpit, clamped to the ring with its own docking arm, dead and dark and furred with frost so thick it looked carved out of snow. I brought us in beside it. As we came past I put the lamp on its flank, and under the frost there was lettering, the old square hand, and Wren leaned to the glass and read it.

"*Mercy,*" she said. "It says *Mercy.* And a two." She looked at me. "Whose is it?"

"I don't know," I said. "Somebody's."

I put the *Mendicant* on the empty ring. The collar took. And I sat there for a moment with my hands on the yoke and my ribs aching where Benno had taught me manners, and the station humming faintly through the hull — a low deep hum, more pressure than sound, as if something very large were breathing — and I thought about Haddie, and the word in her book, and how she'd said *I don't know if there's anything there at all.*

"Come on," said Wren.

* * *

The station had no air, so we went in suited. It had light, which I hadn't expected: faint working lamps along the spine, dim and amber, as though something somewhere had been keeping them lit for a very long time with nobody to see by them. The corridor was frosted from floor to ceiling, white and feathered, glittering where our lamps touched it. Doors stood open on empty rooms. Nothing moved.

We went toward the middle of the spine, because that was where the hum was loudest.

The corridor ended at a round room with its door standing open, and I stopped in the doorway, and Wren stopped behind me, and for a while neither of us went in.

There was a window. It filled the whole far wall of the room, curved, floor to ceiling, and it was clear — the frost had grown over it once, I could see the ghost of it at the edges, but something had cleared the middle, the light or the heat or the pins' slow burning, and through it, filling it, was the Door. That blue-white glow. So close you could have put your hand out into it.

Under the window was a long desk of grey metal with screens set in it. They were lit. Green and amber, steady.

And in front of the desk, in a plain metal chair with its back to us and its face to the window, someone was sitting.

* * *

I went in first. I'd like to say I did it to spare her. I don't know that I did.

It was a woman. In a suit — an old suit, patched all over, heavier and cruder than anything I'd ever seen on a picker or a diver, with a round helmet. She was sitting quite straight in the chair with one hand flat on the desk, a gloved hand with something bound under the glove, like a splint. Beside the hand, set into the desk in a small round socket, was a thing like a spindle — white and steel, about as long as my finger. And the frost had grown over her the way it had grown over the walls, over her shoulders and her helmet and her arm, so that she looked like a thing made of ice and set there.

I held my lamp up to her visor.

She'd been dead a long time. Longer than anything I knew. Longer than the Hulk had been dead, longer than Lanthorn had been lashed together, longer than the Margraves. Her face had gone tight and dark over the bones, but it was a face. Short black hair. A long jaw. Lines round the eyes I'd seen on every diver I ever knew, from squinting into the red. Her mouth a little open. On the breast of her suit, under the frost, somebody had once brushed a patch clear, and there were letters there that I couldn't read and a number I could.

"Rook," said Wren behind me.

She wasn't looking at the woman. She was looking at the floor.

There was a man on the floor at the woman's feet. He was lying on his back with his hands folded on his chest, the way we fold the dead on Lanthorn before we put them out of the lock. He was in a suit too — a different kind, white, finer, newer than hers but still older than anything I'd ever seen — and there was a long black stick lying beside him, like a walking cane, and under his folded hands, held there, a flat dented metal box.

Wren knelt beside him. She looked at his face through the visor for a long moment. I didn't look. I'd seen enough dead faces that week.

Then she put her burned hands on his, very gently, and slid the box out from under them.

* * *

It was a tin. Old and dented, the size of her two palms, with something stamped on its lid. She turned it to the light.

"*Asch,*" she read. "Is that a name?"

"I don't know."

She opened it. Inside were papers. Two lots: a thick fold of sheets covered in columns of tiny figures, close-written, page after page; and a single sheet, by itself, folded in four, with writing on it. Not figures. Words. In the old square hand.

She took out the single sheet and unfolded it, and pushed Haddie's spectacles up her nose with one finger, and looked at it.

"Can you read it?" I said.

"Some." Her voice was very small inside her helmet. "Haddie taught me most of the letters. Not all the words." She looked at it. "It's long."

"Try."

She sat down on the frosted floor beside the dead man, cross-legged, with the sheet on her knee and her lamp held up, and began.

I'll set down what she read. Not the way she read it — she was slow, she went back, she spelled out the words she didn't know, letter by letter, and some she got wrong and I only learned afterward what they'd been — but the way it was. I've had it read to me since, by people who know the old hand better than Wren did then. I've had it read to me a good many times.

> *To whoever comes.*
>
> *My name is Ysolde Marr. I was an auditor of the Third Chancery of the Nave, which you may know by another name, or none. I am writing this in the three hundred and forty-first year of the Count, by the light of a lamp, on the floor of this room.*
>
> *The woman in the chair is Maren Tull. She was a diver of the Sump. Long before I was born she sat in that chair and shut the Door from this room, with that key, to stop the Combine carrying four hundred and twelve children through it to be sold. One of them was her daughter. She died here. Her voice is in the machine, if you can wake it, and if you can understand it. I hope you can.*
>
> *The Combine's officers were caught on our side when she shut it. They told us the Door was shut against us for a debt. They made us pay it. We paid it. By my count — and by another man's before me, Anselm Ferrow, keeper of the Ninth, who died for it — the debt was paid in full in the two hundred and eightieth year of the Count, and we went on paying for sixty-one years after, and for as long after that as it has taken you to come here and read this.*
>
> *The man at her feet is Aurel Quist, Prelate-Auditor of the Nave. He was my teacher, and he was wrong for most of his life, and right at the end of it. Before he died he sealed the account closed with the Factor's own ring. It is in the machine, under the Number. On the papers beside this one is the same, in my hand, in case the machine has failed. There is a name in the margin of the three hundred and twenty-sixth year. Please read it.*
>
> *I have set the Door to open. Slowly. It will take four hundred years. I did not open it for us; I could not. I am going back to the Nave to do the slow thing, if I can, and make the Count lighter year by year until the people can bear to hear what is in this machine. I do not know if I will manage it. If you are reading this, someone managed something.*
>
> *If you have come here because the Door has opened, and someone has come through it to collect what we owe — give them this.*
>
> *It is a receipt.*
>
> *And if you have come here to shut it again: she says, in the machine, that you should, if there is still anyone on either side of it who would sell a child. Is there? I can't count that for you. Count it yourself.*
>
> *Count what is owed, and not a mark more.*
>
> *— Y. M.*

Wren stopped. She sat on the floor with the sheet on her knee and the lamp in her hand, and didn't look up.

"*Receipt,*" she said. "I don't know that word."

"It's what you get," I said, "when you've paid."

* * *

I'd come to shut it.

I want to be plain about that, because I've been plain about everything else. I'd come four days across the system with Teague's breath in my tanks and a dead woman's spectacles on my console to find whatever held the Door and shut it. On the receiver. On the pods. On eleven thousand people in grey stacks along the arms of the Hulk, and Benno in the Deep with forty children, and everybody else. To trap the receiver on our side and leave it there, with everything it had taken, and let whatever happened next happen. It was the sum. It was the only sum that came out. I'd done it every hour of the four days.

I stood in front of the grey desk and looked at the screens. I couldn't read them. But I didn't need to read the middle one. It had three words on it, three short words in a row, the old square hand, and Haddie had taught Wren all three on the second night at the Hulk, before we went down to the seats, and Wren had taught them to me on the run out.

The one in the middle was *shut*.

I put my hand up over it. Not touching. Just over it, my glove a finger's width from the glass.

"Rook," said Wren.

"I know."

"She said count it yourself."

"I know what she said."

I stood there with my hand over the word. I could feel the sum in me, the way you feel a current. The receiver on one side and eleven thousand pods on the other. The Door open and the Door shut. Kask five hours behind. Everything I knew about the thing that had come through, and everything I didn't. I could feel it coming out. I could feel it coming out the way it had come out every night for seven years on the edge of my son's bunk, the way it had come out in the Deep when my hand closed on air beside Mattie's shoulder, the way it had come out in the cargo pod at the third hour of the morning with the roll spread out in front of me and a pen in my hand.

I took my hand away.

"No," I said.

Wren looked up at me from the floor.

"I'm not doing it," I said. "The sum. I've done it all my life and it's come out every time and every time it came out wrong. I'm not doing it again." I looked down at her. "You do it."

"*Me?*"

"You can read. I can't. You can read what she wrote and what's in that machine and I can't read any of it." I sat down on the frosted floor, suddenly, because my legs had decided I was done standing, beside the dead man in white. "And it's not my turn. It was my turn in the cargo pod. I had my turn. You've had none." I looked at her. "So you count it."

She looked at me across the dead man, with Haddie's spectacles crooked on her nose.

Then she got up, and went to the desk, and started to read the screens.

* * *

She found it on the seventh screen. It took her a long while. She went from screen to screen with her finger on the glass, spelling the words out under her breath, and twice she came back to the middle one with the three words on it and stood looking at it, and twice she went away again. And on the seventh screen she stopped, and stood very still, and then said, without turning round:

"Rook. Come and look."

I came and looked. I couldn't read it. But I could see the shape of it. It was the same shape as the pages in Haddie's Count-books — columns of figures, ruled — and at the top, in larger letters, the mark I'd seen on every page of every one of them. The *H* and the *V*. And the number.

And at the bottom, in green, two lines.

"What does it say?"

"*Account.*" She put her finger on the first line. "*Account H-V-Vigil-nought-nought-nought-one. Closed.*" She moved her finger. "*Balance. Credit.*" She stopped. She pushed the spectacles up. "*Credit to debtors.*"

"Credit," I said.

"What's owed to you." She was staring at it. "That's what Haddie said. Credit's what's owed to you." She turned round and looked at me. "Rook. It says *they* owe *us*."

There was another word under the two lines. A single word, by itself, in a little box. Even I knew that one; it was on the receiver's notice, Haddie had taught it me in the dome the first night. I put my finger on the glass beside it.

"And that?"

"*Transmit,*" said Wren.

* * *

That was when Kask came.

She'd made up an hour on us in the reaches. Of course she had; she'd had nothing to do for four days but push her yacht, and she'd never in her life had to fly against a current. I felt the jolt of her docking through the deck — a hard one, a careless one, on the old torn ring at the spine's far end that I'd have said nobody could dock on — and I heard, very faint through the frost and the vacuum, nothing at all, because there was no air to carry it. But I knew. I'd stood behind her chair for nine years. I knew what it felt like when she came into a room.

"How long?" said Wren.

"Ten minutes. She'll have four reeves. Maybe five."

"Then press it."

"Is that your count?"

"*Press it,*" said Wren. "I've counted. There's somebody on both sides of that Door who'd sell a child. There's somebody coming down this corridor right now who'd sell a hundred. I'm not opening it for them and I'm not shutting it for them." She put her burned finger on the glass beside the word. "I'm giving them the receipt."

I pressed it.

The word went from green to amber. Under it, a line came up, and Wren read it.

"*Transmitting to receiver in attendance.*" She swallowed. "That's all. It doesn't say anything else."

"It's at the Hulk," I said. "Or near it. That's—" I tried to work it out. Haddie would have known. "Minutes. Four, five. For it to get there. And then the same again for any answer to come back."

"Ten minutes."

"About that."

We looked at each other.

"Then we'll have to keep her off it for ten minutes," said Wren, and took my knife out of her boot.

* * *

There were five of them, and Kask.

They came up the frosted corridor in a line with their lamps swinging, the way men walk in a dead place, and stopped in the doorway of the round room, and the light from the Door came through the window behind us and lit them all up: five reeves in red suits with the white hand on the breast, with weapons, and in the middle of them, in a red suit of her own that had been made to measure, with her grey braid coiled inside her helmet and the herald in its felt blanket held in the crook of one arm like a baby, the Margrave of Kell Deep.

She looked at the woman in the chair. She looked at the man on the floor. She looked at the screens. And then she looked at me, through her visor, with her small clever light-brown eyes, and smiled.

"Absalom," she said, on the open band. "You found it."

"I found it."

"And the girl." She looked at Wren. "Still with you. After everything. How sweet." She came a step into the room. "Well, I'm afraid I'm going to need you both to step away from that desk. I've some business with the receiver, and this is where it's done."

"The receiver's business is finished," I said. "It's closed."

She laughed. "Is it?"

"The account. It's closed. It's been closed for hundreds of years." I nodded at the seventh screen, where the amber word was. "It's in the machine. Somebody sealed it. A long time ago. We've just sent it on."

Kask stopped smiling.

She looked at the screen for a long moment. I don't think she could read it. I don't think, when I think about it now, that Kask ever had to read anything; she'd always had people to do it for her. But she knew what the H and the V were. She knew what an amber word meant.

"Sent it," she said.

"To the receiver. To your friend." I watched her face. "It'll be getting it about now."

"And what," said the Margrave Kask, very softly, "do you think it'll do?"

"I don't know," I said. "Neither do you. That's the first time I've ever seen you not know something, Teodora."

She looked at me. And then, quite calmly, she turned her head to the reeve on her left and said, "Kill them both, and then bring me whoever can read that desk."

* * *

I'll not tell you all of it. I've told you enough fights in this account. I'll tell you that there were five of them and two of us, and they had weapons and we had my knife, and that it should have been over in a breath.

It wasn't, because of the frost. I'd lived my whole life in the lash and the Kettles and the Hulk, in the dark and the cold, and I'd learned to walk on frosted metal before I'd learned to read a scope; and the Margrave's reeves had been trained in the Deep, where it was hot. The first one came at me across the round room and his boots went out from under him on the white floor and I was on him before he'd stopped sliding. The second one fired and missed and the shot went into the frosted wall and blew a great white cloud of ice crystals into the airless room that hung there glittering in the light from the Door, so that for a moment none of us could see anything at all.

In the white I heard Wren say something. I didn't hear what.

When it cleared I'd taken a shot high in the side, under the cracked ribs, and I was on one knee by the desk with my glove pressed to my suit where the seal was going, and two reeves were down, and one was backing away toward the door with his weapon up and his eyes wide, looking at the woman in the chair. I don't know what he thought he'd seen. And Wren was by the desk too, a little way from me, with my knife in her hand and her back to the screens.

And Kask was at the desk.

She'd come round the far side of it in the white, while we were fighting. She'd never been fast, Teodora Kask. She'd never had to be. She was standing in front of the middle screen with the herald still in the crook of her arm, and she'd put her other hand up — her still, still hand — flat on the glass. On the middle word.

On *shut*.

The screen changed. A long line of the old square hand came up, and under it, a single word in a box, like the one we'd pressed.

"*Confirm,*" said Wren. She read it out. Her voice was very flat.

"If I can't have it," said the Margrave Kask on the band, quite pleasantly, not looking at either of us, "then nobody leaves. That's fair, isn't it? That's just. That's what my people will say. *It is just.*" She lifted her hand from the glass, slowly, toward the little box with the word in it. "Nobody goes through. Nobody comes through. Everybody stays where they are, in the dark, with me." She smiled. "I think the receiver will find, Absalom, that it's very much in my debt."

I couldn't get up. I tried. I put my hand on the edge of the desk and pulled and my side opened and the world went grey.

Wren didn't try to get up. She was already up.

I'll not tell you about this either. I've decided. It was hers, not mine, and she can tell it or not. I'll tell you only that she was nineteen and had spent ten years in the vents under the Margrave's house, where she'd learned every wall at Kell Deep there was to get behind; and that she went across the two metres between them on the frosted floor without slipping once, as though she'd been born on it; and that Kask's still hand never reached the box.

The Margrave of Kell Deep went down beside the chair, at the dead woman's feet, next to the man in white. The herald rolled out of the crook of her arm in its felt blanket and lay on the frosted floor, warm and black and humming.

The last reeve dropped his weapon. I heard it clatter, faintly, through the deck. Then I heard him go away down the corridor, running, and slipping, and running.

Wren stood over Kask with my knife in her hand for a long time. Then she put it down on the desk, carefully, beside the dead woman's bound hand, and came and knelt beside me, and put her burned hand over mine on the seal of my suit, and pressed.

"You're leaking," she said.

"I know."

"Stop it."

"I'm trying."

Behind her, on the desk, the middle screen was still waiting. *Confirm.* Nobody had touched it.

And on the floor, in its felt blanket, the herald stopped humming.

* * *

It spoke from everywhere. The way it had in the Hulk the day the tenders came through: from the herald on the floor, and from the speakers in our helmets, and from the desk, and from some dead grille in the station's walls that hadn't spoken in centuries. The calm voice. Courteous. Even. A woman's voice, or the shape of one, that had been waiting at a closed door for longer than anybody could count.

Wren listened with her head on one side and Haddie's spectacles crooked on her nose, and her lips moving. When it paused for breath, as it always did, she turned and looked at me, and her face was very strange.

"I don't know all of it," she said. "Haddie would've. I don't."

"Tell me what you've got."

She told me. The voice went round again, word for word the same, and she said it after it, slowly, a beat behind, the way Haddie had done in the Hulk — getting some wrong, going back.

"*Statement of — of reconciliation received,*" she said. "*Seal — seal verified. A name. Castellane. Factor.*" She frowned. "*Account H-V-Vigil-nought-nought-nought-one, reconciled. Closed. In the year—*" a number she didn't know "*—local.*" She stopped. Her voice shook. "*Recovery is — terminated.*"

I couldn't speak.

"*Debtors in custody,*" said Wren, "*are released.*"

The voice went on. She went on after it.

"*Balance — credit to debtors. The receiver — acknowledges.* Something. Something about *overpayment.*" She shook her head. "And then *the receiver holds no*—something—*assets other than*—" She stopped. She listened to it come round again. "*Other than itself.*" She looked at me. "*Title to the receiver vessel transfers to the debtors. In settlement of balance.*"

"What does that mean?"

"I don't know," said Wren. "I think it means it's ours."

* * *

The band came on an hour later, while Wren was sealing my suit with the patches from the *Mendicant*'s locker. Not the receiver. A man's voice, young and cracked, faint with distance and the Shear, on the reeves' channel.

"*Rook. Rook, can you hear me? It's Benno. Benno Strick.*" A long burst of noise. "*I'm at the Deep. I don't know if you can — Rook, the pods. The pods are opening. I've got it on the scope from here. The ones on the Hulk, all down the arms — they're splitting. People are coming out. They're just — standing there, on the arms, in the vacuum, inside these sort of — bubbles—*" He was laughing, or crying. "*And the drones are carrying them. Carrying them back. Into the station. Into the air. Gently, Rook, like — like you'd carry a child. I don't know what you did. I don't know what you did. Rook—*"

The band broke up into noise and didn't come back.

Wren had stopped patching. She was kneeling over me with a seal in one hand and her face behind her visor wet.

"Eleven thousand," she said.

"And the kids at the Deep."

"And Kesse, and Odo, and the Fenn girl's baby." She wiped her visor with the back of her glove, which did nothing. "And Mother Yelland, if she's in one. And Teague. And Dorrit." She laughed, wetly. "Dorrit'll think it's the Return."

"Maybe it is," I said.

She looked at me.

"Not theirs," I said. "Ours."

* * *

I've thought a great deal, since, about what I did next, and I'm still not sure I can tell you why I did it. I'll tell you what I did.

When Wren had finished patching me I got up. It took a while. She helped. I went to the grey desk, past the Margrave on the floor and the man in white, and stood beside the woman in the chair, and looked at the middle screen. It was still waiting where Kask had left it, with the long line of square letters and the word in its box. *Confirm.*

I didn't touch it. There was another word on the screen, at the bottom, smaller, that Wren told me meant *cancel*. I touched that. The long line went away. The three words came back.

"Which one's it on?" I said. "Now."

Wren looked. "*Restore.* It's been on *restore*. That's what she did. The auditor. That's what she says in her letter. *I have set the Door to open. Slowly.*" She looked at the window, at the blue-white glow pouring through it. "It's not slow any more."

"And *hold*?"

"*Hold*'s — keep. Keep it how it is." She looked at me. "Open."

I put my finger on it.

The screen asked me something. Wren read it. I said yes. And through the window, round the rim of the light, the four great things hanging on their threads changed their colour, very slightly — the deep coal-red going a shade brighter, a shade steadier, like a stove that's been banked for the night having its door opened and the draught let in.

The hum through the deck changed too. It didn't get louder. It got steady. Like a breath held.

"There," I said.

"What did you do?"

"I don't know," I said. "I think I'm holding it."

* * *

There's a voice in the machine. The auditor said so in her letter — *her voice is in the machine, if you can wake it* — and on the third day, when I'd stopped bleeding, Wren found it. A line on the first screen that she'd been reading past for two days because it was only one word and a number. She touched it, and from a little grille in the desk beside the lock, beside the dead woman's hand, a voice came out.

It was a woman's voice, low and rough. It spoke the old tongue — the receiver's tongue, the herald's — but not the way the receiver spoke it. It spoke it the way people speak. It ran its words together and swallowed its vowels and paused to breathe, and once, near the beginning, it stopped altogether for a moment, the way you stop when you can't trust your voice.

I couldn't understand a word of it. Neither could Wren; she'd learned the old hand from Haddie, written, on paper, and this was something else. We sat on the frosted floor of the round room, the two of us, beside the woman in the chair and the man at her feet, and listened to all of it without understanding anything, for as long as it ran. I heard a name in it, said more than once, that might have been a name. I heard a man somewhere far off singing, and knew the tune, and nearly laughed, because I'd heard it all my life on Lanthorn in the mouths of old divers and never known it was so old. I heard a sound like the end of the world.

And at the very end, after a long silence, so close to the grille that it filled the round room, I heard three knocks. A gloved hand, on metal.

You don't need the old tongue for that. I've knocked on enough doors.

* * *

The receiver came to Gallows on the ninth day.

It came slowly, the way the drones had carried the people back into the Hulk, gently, as if it didn't want to wake anything. It filled the whole of the window on our side of the Door — a darkness where the stars weren't, too big to have a shape — and stopped there, and waited. And the herald on the floor, which Wren had wrapped back in its felt blanket and put on the desk beside the dead woman's hand, began to hum, and then spoke. A short thing. Wren didn't need to translate it.

"*Awaiting instruction.*"

They came out from the Hulk on the receiver's tenders — the same black six-legged things that had sat on Lanthorn's hulls like ticks — and docked them along the spine. Benno brought the first lot. He had his arm in a sling and a grin on his face like a split melon, and behind him in the tender's hold were forty children from Kell Deep with burned hands, and Kesse with her burned ear, and Odo with his squint, and the Fenn girl with her baby, who had grown. And behind that tender, others. People from the pods. People from Lanthorn and the Kettles and Sallow who'd come out of the grey and stood in the air of the Hulk blinking, and heard what had happened, and asked where the Door was.

Not all of them. Most went home. Most of them had homes, or what was left of them, and children, and debts of their own that hadn't been in any machine. Mother Yelland went home to Number Two hull, I heard, and had the Lash Council's table mended where the tender's leg had come through it, and sat at the head of it and quieted them all down. Teague went back to the bottom of the Number Three Kettle and started selling breath again.

But some of them came.

* * *

Wren went on the eleventh day.

There were two hundred and six of them in the end, who wanted to go through. Kesse. Odo. Half the children from the Deep, the older ones, who'd seen the stars from the top of the Old Chimney on a dare and wanted to see what came after stars. A few old divers who'd heard the hymn on the band and come to see where it ended. Dorrit, God help us, who'd come out of her pod on the Hulk's Arm Six smiling and still thought it was the Return, and who I didn't have the heart to tell. And Benno, who said somebody had to fly the tenders and he was the only reeve left who wasn't dead or ashamed.

And Wren.

She came to say goodbye in the round room, on the last morning, in her suit. I was sitting where I'd sat most of the eleven days, on the frosted floor with my back against the grey desk, beside the woman in the chair and the man at her feet. We'd put the Margrave out of the lock on the third day, the way we do on Lanthorn, into the light. Wren had said the words. I hadn't.

She stood over me for a while without saying anything. She'd cut her hair, or let Kesse cut it, short and badly. Haddie's spectacles were in her breast pocket; she'd had the receiver make her a pair that fit, out of something, and she said they were better than Haddie's and she'd never wear them.

"You're staying," she said.

"Somebody has to hold it."

"The receiver could hold it. It said so. *Awaiting instruction.*"

"I know what it said," I said. "I'm not giving that thing an instruction. I've seen what it does with them. It'd keep one for a thousand years." I looked up at her. "Somebody has to sit here and hold it open. And mind them." I nodded at the chair. At the floor. "And keep an eye on what comes back through."

"And if something bad comes back through?"

"Then I'll be here," I said. "And I'll count it."

She looked at me.

"You still owe me," said Wren.

"I know."

"You're never going to pay it all."

"I know."

"Then keep paying," she said, the way she'd said it in the cargo lock at the Hulk with the grey hatch behind us and Haddie on the other side of it. "And don't stop."

She bent down, awkwardly in her suit, and put her helmet against mine, visor to visor, so close I could see her face behind it — the bruise gone yellow, the gap in her front teeth that had never closed, the eyes of a nine-year-old girl on a gangway writing me down. She'd finished, now. She'd got all of it.

"I'm still not forgiving you," she said.

"Good," I said.

She laughed. And then she straightened up, and turned, and went out of the round room and down the frosted corridor toward the tender, and didn't look back. She never had, on the gangway, either.

* * *

I watched them go through.

The receiver went first, because it had to; it was the only thing big enough to carry two hundred and six people into whatever was on the far side and bring them back if they wanted bringing. It went slowly. It slid across the window from right to left, a piece of the dark come loose, and where it went the blue-white light went out and then came back, and then it was in the Door, and the light round its edges was enormous, and then it wasn't there any more. Just the light.

It took the better part of an hour. I sat on the floor and watched the whole of it.

When it was gone I got up. My side still hurt. I went to the desk and stood beside the woman in the chair, with her bound hand flat on the grey metal beside the white-and-steel key, and looked out through the window at the Door. At the sky through it, or whatever it was. Blue. It was blue.

I thought about Haddie. I thought about Jory, and the roll in my coat, and the six hours on the edge of his bunk. I thought, for no reason I could have given you, about the plain metal chair at the far end of the place with the seats under the Hulk, where Wren and the children had hidden, and the thing I'd seen hanging from its arm the first time I ever went down there, years ago, and never mentioned to anybody: a disc of old dull steel the size of my palm, on two cords, with a jagged line across the middle of it where it had been broken once, its two halves bound together now so close you could hardly see the join. I thought about a girl of nine on a gangway with her chin up. I thought about the woman in the chair, whose name I'd been told and couldn't say properly, and the man at her feet, and the auditor who'd written *count it yourself* and gone back to do the slow thing, whatever that had been, wherever it had got her.

I didn't do a sum. I've stopped doing those.

I put my glove flat on the desk beside the dead woman's hand, on the cold grey metal under the window, with the light from the Door coming through onto both of them.

And knocked three times.

*Still here.*
