# Chapter 7: The Drowned Union

*The Bond · 2431*

The union met on the water.

Maren had not known that. In nineteen years of living above it she had never been down to the Drowned Decks — nobody went down there, it was said, except the pump crews who had given up forty years ago and the children who dared each other to — and when Hatch led her down the last service ladder on the ninth night, with a hand-lamp swinging from her wrist and her habit hitched up into her belt, she stopped on the bottom rung and simply stared.

It was a lake. The Sump's three lowest levels had been cut into the ice as warehouses, back when the colony was young and the Combine still imagined there would be things to store; and when the refinery heat began to seep down through the crust, the ice had softened and run, and the meltwater had found the warehouses and filled them. Now the three levels were one black still pool under a ceiling of dripping ice, and across it, a metre above the surface, ran the old cargo catwalks — iron grilles on iron legs, rusted orange, linking the stumps of the support pillars like stepping stones across a river. The cold was enormous. Her breath went up white and hung. And every sound — the drips, Hatch's boots on the grille, her own heart — came back off the water doubled, so that it seemed there were two of everything.

"Nobody listens down here," said Hatch. "The Combine put microphones on every level of the Sump the year I arrived. They never bothered with this one. They don't think anybody's mad enough to stand about on a rusty catwalk over forty metres of ice water." She looked back at Maren, and in the lamplight her old face was almost merry. "They're right, of course. But we're mad."

They were waiting in the middle of the lake, where four catwalks met on a square iron platform round the stump of a pillar: a dozen people, maybe a few more, standing close together for warmth, with lamps hung from the pillar's rusted bolts. Maren knew some of them. Ruth Calloway, who ran the scrubber crews on Level Seven and had the loudest voice in the Sump. Ade Okoro, Lin's husband, a refinery hand from Number Two head with arms like hawsers, whose boy was 0188 on the list. Old Pym, who had been a counter in the tally houses for forty years before his eyes went and who still knew every rate by heart. Farrant and Moss and the Ellery sisters, breathers all, who dove for the Number Three head and had always kept their distance from her because she was better than them.

And Dov. She had brought Dov. He stood a little apart from the rest, at the platform's edge, with his hands in his pockets and his shoulders up round his ears against the cold, looking down into the black water as though there were something in it he recognised.

She had argued with herself about bringing him. He was a drunk. He talked. But he was also the best tender pilot in the Sump and the only one who flew for her, and if the breathers walked out the tenders would have to walk out too, and the tenders listened to Dov the way the breathers were beginning to listen to her. And he was Dov. He had held the light when she was born. When she had told him, the night before, that Hatch had asked for him, he had looked at her for a long moment without saying anything and then nodded and said, *All right, girl,* and had come down the ladder behind her that evening sober, which she could not remember him being after dark in twenty years.

"You all know why we're here," said Hatch. "You've all seen the paper."

They had. Hatch had read it to each of them herself, one at a time, behind the curtain in the clinic over the past six days, and then burned the copy and kept the original somewhere Maren did not know and had not asked.

"Then you know what it means," said Hatch. "They're leaving. They're taking our children to sell, and they're taking the breath, and they're taking everything else that isn't nailed down, and they're leaving us here to die in the ice when the Throat closes. That's what *Exodus Clause* means. It means the Combine has decided we're not worth the price of the door." She looked round at them, one by one. "I've been doing this for thirty years. I've organised a dozen walkouts and three strikes and I've buried more of you than I can count. And I have never, in all that time, had anything I could use. Not once. The Combine's always had what it needed, and we've always had nothing, and that's why we've always lost." She smiled, a thin hard smile. "That's changed. For the first time since I came to this miserable moon, they need something from us."

"Breath," said Pym.

"Breath." Hatch nodded at Maren. "Tell them."

Maren hadn't expected that. She cleared her throat; it came back off the water at her, doubled.

"The *Grace* takes breath for the passage," she said. "To run her drives and to feed the pins while she's in the Throat. That's a lot of breath. And then she takes the stockpile on top, to sell at Corvid — that's what's in the tanks at High, eight months of it. But eight months isn't enough for both, not if she wants to make the crossing with full tanks and still have the stockpile to sell." She had got this from Lucan, two nights ago, in a hurried whisper at the back of the tally house. "They need another four weeks of draws. That's why the convoy's in forty days. They need us to keep diving right up until they leave."

"So we stop diving," said Ade Okoro.

"So we stop diving," said Hatch. "Every breather, every tender, every refinery hand. Every head in the Sump shuts down. No breath comes up. Nothing goes into the tanks. And the *Grace* can't leave without what's in the tanks, or if she does, she leaves without her stockpile and the board in Halloran finds out their Factor's lost them a fortune." She looked round. "And then we tell them our price."

"Which is?" said Ruth Calloway.

"The children," said Hatch. "Every ward on that list, returned to their parents before the *Grace* leaves. That's the first thing. That's the thing we don't trade."

"And then?" Calloway pressed. "Say they give us the kids. Then the *Grace* goes, and the Throat closes, and we've got four hundred more mouths to feed in a moon that's going to starve anyway. What then, Sister?"

There was a silence. The water dripped.

"Then we'll starve together," said Hatch. "With our children. I'd take that over the other."

"I wouldn't," said Farrant, the breather. He was a short, hard man of forty with the rattle already in his voice. "I'd take the *Grace*."

Heads turned.

"I mean it," said Farrant. "Why are we bargaining? There's thirty thousand of us. There's what, two hundred marines on High? Three? We go up on the shuttles, we take the docks, we take the *Grace*, and we take ourselves out through the Throat with our kids. Let the Combine sit in the ice and see how they like it."

There was a murmur, and Maren heard in it how much some of them liked the idea. She liked it herself, for a moment. She felt it in her chest like heat.

"With what?" said Hatch quietly.

"With—"

"With what, Farrant? Cutting bars? Scoop wrenches? There are three hundred and forty Combine marines on Hesper High, I've counted them, and every one has a rifle and armour and a station built to be held against exactly what you're describing. You'd get up there with your wrench and they'd cut you to pieces in the shuttle lock before you got your feet on the deck. And then they'd cut the air to the Sump and wait." She shook her head. "They've had two hundred years to make sure we can't take anything from them by force. They're good at it. The one thing they haven't made sure of is that they can make us *work*."

Farrant didn't answer. But he didn't look convinced, and neither did the Ellery sisters behind him.

"How long can we hold out?" said Old Pym. "Once we stop. They'll cut our rations. They'll cut air."

"Then we'll be hungry and cold," said Hatch. "And so will they, if they want that breath. It's a contest. Who can wait longest." She looked at Maren. "That depends on the breathers. If the breathers hold, the heads stay shut. If the breathers break, it's over in a day."

Maren understood, then, why Hatch had wanted her. Not for the paper. For this.

"The breathers will hold," she said.

"Will they? You can promise that?"

"No," said Maren. "But I'll be the last one to break."

Hatch looked at her for a long moment, and nodded.

At the edge of the platform, Dov had turned from the water. He was looking at Maren, and his face, in the lamplight, had an expression on it she did not recognise and could not read.

"When?" he said.

They all looked at him. He did not usually speak in rooms like this; he did not usually come to rooms like this.

"When do we stop?" said Dov. "What day? And who's going to be at the heads when it starts?" He shrugged. "If I'm going to tell the tenders to stand down I'll need to tell them something. They'll want to know who's running it."

"We'll tell you," said Hatch. "When it's time."

"Fair enough," said Dov, and put his hands back in his pockets, and looked down into the water again.

* * *

The Factor came down to the Sump on the tenth day for the Scholarship ceremony.

The Combine had announced it a week before: a celebration, at the Number Four refinery head, the biggest open space in the Sump, to honour the families of the selected wards. Attendance was not compulsory. It was made clear that it would be noted. And so on the tenth day at the sixth shift-bell there were four thousand bonds standing in the great domed cavern of the refinery head, under the hanging intake pipes and the dark bulk of the separators, in their best coveralls, with their children who were not wards held in front of them by the shoulders so they could see.

Maren stood near the back, against the cold wall, with Hatch on one side of her and Dov on the other. She had not wanted to come. Hatch had made her. *You want to know what you're fighting,* Hatch had said, *you go and look at it.*

The Factor's party came in by the shuttle gallery, high up on the far side, and walked out onto the separator platform where everyone could see them. Thirty officers in Combine grey, and a dozen marines in black armour with their rifles held across their chests, and in the middle of them, in a grey coat no different from the others except that it was very well cut, a tall old man with silver hair.

Imre Castellane had been Factor of Vigil for nineteen years. Maren had seen him a handful of times in her life, always from far off — on a feed, on a platform, once crossing the docks at High with his officers around him like a wall. He was taller than she had expected, close up, and thinner, and he stood very straight; he had the kind of face that would have looked well on a coin, long and fine-boned and grave, and he looked out over the crowd of bonds below him the way a man might look over a field he owned and had come to love.

He had a slate in his hand. He did not use it.

"Families of Vigil," he said. His voice was not loud, but the refinery head's speakers carried it, deep and clear, to every corner of the cavern. "Thank you for coming. I know that for many of you it is a long way to come and a hard thing to come for. I know that some of you are frightened. That is natural. I want to speak to you plainly about what is happening, and why."

Behind him, on the platform, a great screen lit. On it — and a sound went through the four thousand bonds like wind through a field — were the wards. Rows of them, in the visitation hall of the Nursery, on the children's side of the glass. They were in new uniforms, dark blue, with the H-V crest on the breast. Their hair had been cut. Their faces had been scrubbed. They stood in neat lines with their hands at their sides, and as the feed came up they began, at some signal Maren could not see, to sing. It was a Combine song, one she half-knew from the feeds, about the Inner Worlds and the light. Their small voices came thin and sweet out of the speakers into the refinery head, and all around her, Maren heard parents begin to weep.

She looked for Pell. She could not find her. There were too many. And then she did — in the third row, at the end, half a head taller than the girl beside her, standing very still with her mouth closed. Not singing.

"Your children," said Castellane softly, over the song, "are going to have what none of you have had. That is the simple truth. They are going to be educated. They are going to live under open skies. They are going to have work that does not kill them, in a part of the Combine where the air is free and the water falls from the sky. I have been Factor here for nineteen years, and I have watched you send your children up to the Nursery as surety, and I have watched you grieve for them, and I have asked myself every year: what can I give back?" He paused. "This is what I can give back."

The song ended. The feed held for a moment on the children's scrubbed, uncertain faces, and then went dark.

"I know what some of you are saying," Castellane went on. "I hear it, you know. The Combine hears a great deal. Some of you are saying that this is a trick. That the Combine never gives anything away. That there must be a price." He smiled, a little sadly. "And you're right. There is always a price. That is the first thing I learned when I joined the Combine at seventeen, and it is the most important thing I know. Everything has a price. Every hour of your labour, every litre of your air, every bed in the Nursery and every meal your children eat — someone, somewhere, has paid for it, and has written it down. That is what the Combine *is*. Not a company. Not a government. A ledger. A great book in which every debt and every payment is recorded, so that nothing is lost and no one is cheated." He spread his hands. "People think the ledger is cruel. It is not. The ledger is the only kindness that lasts. Without the ledger, there is only the knife. There is only whoever is strongest, taking whatever they can reach. I have seen worlds without ledgers. I would not send your children to them."

He looked out over the crowd, and his voice dropped.

"Your children's price has been paid," he said. "By you. By your years in the dark. And now the ledger is going to pay you back." He lowered his hands. "That is all. Thank you. Count what's owed."

And from four thousand throats, ragged, uncertain, an automatic murmur, the way you'd answer a priest:

*And not a mark more.*

Beside her, Hatch had gone white to the lips.

"He's good," said Dov softly, on her other side. He sounded almost admiring. "He's very good."

* * *

Afterward the officers came down off the platform into the crowd, the way the Combine's officers did at ceremonies, to shake hands and be thanked. The marines came with them, a pace behind. Maren would have left. She was moving toward the gallery stair when a grey-coated woman stepped into her path with a polite smile and said, "Mrs. Tull? The Factor would like a word."

She looked at Hatch. Hatch's face was perfectly calm, and her eyes said: *go*.

The Factor was standing in a little clear space near the separator base, with officers around him and marines at the edges, talking to Lin and Ade Okoro. Lin was weeping and clutching his hand. Ade stood beside her like a wall, saying nothing. Castellane was speaking to Lin very gently, his silver head bent to hers, and as Maren came up he straightened and released her hand and turned, and Lin and Ade were moved away by the grey woman, and Maren found herself standing in front of the Factor of Vigil with nothing between them but a metre of cold air.

"Mrs. Tull," he said. "Thank you. I know you didn't want to come."

She did not say anything.

"I make a point of meeting the best breather on Vigil every year or so," said Castellane. "It used to be your mother. Ada Tull. Twenty-two years in the deep seams; I remember her record. She came up to the Residence once, you know, to receive a commendation — the year she brought up the big seam off Number One head. She told me to my face that the commendation would be more use to her as a reduction on her bond, and that if I wanted to honour her I could stop charging her for the air she breathed while she was being honoured." He smiled. "I liked her very much."

"She died of the rattle," said Maren. "At forty-six."

"I know. I'm sorry." He seemed to mean it. "Most of them do. It's the great cruelty of this place. The breath is in the deep seams, and the deep seams kill the people who go down to them, and there is no other way to get it up." He looked at her. "Your daughter is on the list."

"0212."

"Pell," said Castellane. "Yes. I've seen her assessment. Remarkable. Quite remarkable. She'll go a very long way."

Maren looked at him. She looked at his fine, grave, sorrowful face, and his clean hands, and the soft grey of his well-cut coat; and at his throat, where the collar was open, on a fine steel chain against his skin, something that caught the work-lights of the refinery head and threw them back.

It was not jewellery. She had thought for a moment it was. It was a spindle, the length of her finger, half ceramic and half steel, the ceramic white and smooth and the steel worked with a fine spiral groove. At one end it tapered to a blunt point. At the other end was a ring for the chain. It looked like a part of something. It looked like it had been made to fit, very precisely, into a place that was waiting for it.

Castellane saw her looking. He did not touch it, or cover it. He only smiled.

"May I tell you something, Mrs. Tull?" he said. "As one who knew your mother?"

"You're the Factor."

"I am." The smile did not change. "People believe, in places like this, that distance is a kind of forgiveness. That if you are far enough from the centre of things, or long enough, the debts you owe will simply — fade. Be forgotten. I want you to understand that this is not so." His voice was very gentle. "The Combine does not forget. It does not forgive. It does not close an account because the account has become inconvenient to reach. It has receivers, Mrs. Tull, that have been waiting at closed doors on the edges of the map for longer than this colony has existed. Machines. Very patient ones. They'll wait a thousand years if they're told to. And when the door opens—" he spread his clean hands "—they'll come in and count."

"Is that a threat, Factor?"

"No," said Imre Castellane. "It's arithmetic." He inclined his head to her, a small, courteous bow. "Give my regards to Sister Hatch."

* * *

Lucan found her in the shuttle gallery afterward, in the press of bonds going back to the Sump and officers going back to the shuttles. He came alongside her in the crowd and walked with her for ten steps, not looking at her, with a slate held up in front of his face as though he were reading.

"He knows about Hatch," Maren said.

"He's always known about Hatch. He thinks she's harmless." Lucan turned a page on his slate. "He's not stupid. He'll have guessed there'll be something. A walkout. He's planning for it."

"How?"

"I don't know. I'm not in those meetings." He was quiet for three steps. "You saw it. Didn't you. At his throat."

"What is it?"

"The Gallows key," said Lucan. "Factor's authority over the pins. Every Throat station has a master console, and every master console has a lock, and that's what goes in the lock. The station runs itself day to day. But anything that isn't day to day — anything that changes what the pins are doing — has to go through that key." He turned another page. "There's only one. He's worn it since he came to Vigil. He sleeps in it."

"Why does it matter?"

Lucan stopped walking. The crowd flowed round them. For a moment he looked at her over the top of his slate, and his frightened, narrow, decent face was more frightened than she had seen it yet.

"Because under the Clause," he said, "the Factor holds the key until the account is closed."

"What does that mean? Closed how?"

But a grey-coated officer was calling his name from the shuttle lock, and he had already turned away from her, and in a moment he was gone into the crowd of grey.

* * *

They voted that night, on the water.

It took no time at all. Hatch put it to them simply — the strike, every head, every breather and tender and refinery hand, until the wards were returned — and asked for hands, and the hands went up in the lamplight, one after another, until they all were. Even Farrant. Even the Ellery sisters, though they looked at each other first.

Maren's went up. Dov's went up, after a moment, at the edge of the platform.

"The eighteenth day," said Hatch. "At first shift. The heads go dark and stay dark." She looked round at them. "Go home. Tell nobody. Not your wives, not your husbands, not your children. On the seventeenth night you tell your own crews and nobody else. And on the eighteenth day we find out what we're worth."

They went back across the catwalks in ones and twos, lamps swinging, footsteps doubled on the water. Maren went last but one, with Hatch beside her, slowly, because the old woman's knees were bad on the ladders.

At the foot of the service ladder Hatch stopped and put a hand on her arm.

"Your Factor gave you his regards for me," she said.

"Yes."

"Did he say anything else?"

Maren thought about the spindle on its fine chain. She thought about the patient machines waiting at the doors on the edges of the map. *They'll come in and count.*

"He said it was arithmetic," she said.

Hatch laughed — a short dry sound that came back off the black water twice. "It always is, with them," she said. "That's the trouble. They've never once in two hundred years thought we could add."

She started up the ladder. Maren stood at the bottom and looked back across the Drowned Decks, at the lamps going out one by one along the catwalks as the union went home, and counted them, without meaning to. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

There should have been fourteen. She looked for the last lamp, the one at the edge of the platform, where Dov had stood.

It was already gone.
