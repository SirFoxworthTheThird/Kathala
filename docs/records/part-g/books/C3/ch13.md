# Chapter 13: The Scholarship

*The Bond · 2431*

The convoy dock at Hesper High was the biggest room Maren had ever stood in, and it was full of children.

She had seen it from the outside a hundred times, coming up on the visitation shuttle: a great bulge on the station's spindle, ribbed with gantries, its outer face one long port of armoured glass. She had never been inside it. Bonds were not taken through the convoy dock; bonds came up through the ward locks at the other end of the station and went back down the same way. But this morning the six ward shuttles had docked at the convoy dock's lower berths, one after another, and the parents had come out of them in a long uncertain file into a space so high and wide that the far wall was hazy, and had stopped, all of them, just inside, and stared.

Through the long port, filling it from end to end, lay the *Grace*.

Maren had known she was big. Lucan had told her: a kilometre and a quarter, the largest ship in the Combine's Corvid fleet, built to carry a colony's staff and stores in a single run. Knowing it was nothing. She lay alongside the dock in Hesper's shadow like a whale beside a raft, her flank a curved grey cliff studded with ports and hatches and the black mouths of cargo bays, and down her side in letters each taller than a refinery head, white on grey, ran her name.

HALLORAN'S GRACE

A boarding tube ran from the dock's centre to a hatch in her flank, wide enough to drive a shuttle down. At its mouth stood a desk, and at the desk sat three clerks with slates. And between the parents and the desk, across the whole width of the dock floor, a line had been painted in yellow, and behind the yellow line, in rows, in their new blue uniforms with the H-V crest on the breast, stood four hundred and twelve children.

They were so quiet. That was what Maren remembered afterward, more than anything. Four hundred and twelve children and not one of them making a sound.

"*Families of Vigil,*" said a voice from the dock's speakers. It was a woman's voice, warm and pleasant. Deputy Factor Vole. "*Welcome. You may cross the line.*"

* * *

She found Pell by not looking.

She had known she would not be able to see her in the rows, so she did not try. She crossed the yellow line with the others in a rush that was half a stumble, four hundred parents breaking all at once into the rows of children like water into sand, and she stood still in the middle of it with her eyes shut and listened, the way she listened in the dark of a dive for the change in the hull's note; and somewhere off to her left, among all the voices that had suddenly started at once — the sobbing and the names and the laughter — she heard one that said, perfectly clearly, in a tone of mild exasperation:

"You're standing in the wrong *row*."

She opened her eyes and turned, and her daughter walked into her.

There was no glass.

She had thought about it every night for four days, since Vole had read out the terms in Siggard's office. What it would be like. She had imagined it a hundred ways. None of them had been anything like it. Pell was taller than she was ready for, and thinner, all bones under the stiff new uniform, and she was warm. That was the thing. She was *warm*. Maren had not touched her daughter in six years, and she had forgotten, somehow, that she would be warm — that she would have weight, and a smell, soap and something under it that was only Pell, the smell of the top of her head that Maren had breathed in every night for six years in the crèche on Level Four and then never again. Pell's arms went round her neck and Pell's face went into her shoulder, and Maren held her, and held her, and could not get her close enough.

"You're squashing me," said Pell, into her shoulder, muffled.

"I know."

"It's fine. I'm just telling you." Pell did not let go. "You smell like the head."

"I've been on strike."

"I know. Everybody knows. Matron was furious." Pell pulled back a little and looked at her, and her small straight mouth was doing something it did not usually do; it was shaking. "You look terrible."

"So do you."

"I've been *crying,*" said Pell, with disgust, and put her face back into Maren's shoulder.

They stood like that for a long time. Around them, all through the rows, it was the same. Lin Okoro was on her knees on the dock floor with a boy of ten in her lap, rocking him like a baby, and Ade standing over them with his great arms round them both. An old man Maren did not know was holding a girl's face between his two hands and just looking at it. Somebody was laughing in a high strange way that did not stop.

And all round the edge of the dock, up on the gantries and along the walls and in the shadows of the berths, in their black armour, with their rifles held across their chests, the marines stood and watched.

* * *

"I know about Brannock," said Pell.

It was half an hour later. They were sitting on the dock floor, the two of them, against the base of a gantry leg, a little apart from the others, with Pell's back against Maren's chest and Maren's arms round her. Pell had her hands on Maren's wrists, holding them there.

"How?"

"Matron leaves her slate on the desk in the study room. I can read upside down." Pell's voice was very quiet and very level. "I saw the manifest. Four weeks ago. Before the Scholarship was even announced. I didn't know what it meant, at first, it just said *Schedule C* and *consigned* and a lot of numbers. So I looked up the words. And then I looked up Brannock in the library." She was quiet. "There were pictures."

Maren held her tighter.

"I didn't tell the others," said Pell. "I didn't know if I should. The little ones are so happy. They think they're going to have a sky." Her fingers tightened on Maren's wrists. "I worked out my number. Eighty-eight thousand four hundred. I'm the third highest. Because I can count." She laughed, a small wet laugh. "You always said I wasn't worth anything."

"You're not."

"You said I was yours."

"You are."

"That's not how it works," said Pell, but softly, and leaned her head back against Maren's shoulder.

Maren put her mouth against her daughter's hair.

"Listen to me," she said. "Listen. When the hour's up, they'll call for embarkation. And when they do, somebody's going to start singing. The breathers' hymn. You know it?"

"Everybody knows it."

"When you hear it, you hold my hand. Don't let go. Whatever happens. We're going to walk into the shuttle at Berth Four, the one we came up in, and they're going to seal the lock, and we're going home."

Pell was very still.

"Is that the plan?" she said at last.

"That's the plan."

"Whose plan is it?"

"Sister Hatch's. The union's."

"How many marines are there?"

Maren looked up, at the gantries. At the black shapes along the walls.

"I don't know," she said.

"I counted," said Pell. "When they brought us in. There's a hundred and six I can see. There's more in the berths, behind the shuttle locks. I saw them go in this morning, before you came." She turned her head, and looked up at Maren, and her eyes were her father's eyes, whoever he had been, and very clear. "They went into the shuttles. Into the berths. Before you came up."

Maren looked across the dock at Berth Four. At the shuttle she had come up in, nosed into its lock, its hatch closed. At Ruth Calloway's crew, who should have been standing at that hatch, waiting.

There was no one standing at the hatch.

* * *

She would go over it afterward, again and again, in the dark of the caretakers' room and the long dark of the run to the Throat, and she would never be able to make it come out differently. If she had seen it sooner. If she had moved. If she had found Hatch in the crowd and said *the crews are gone, the shuttles are taken, it's a trap* — and what would Hatch have done? There was no other way off the dock. There was no other plan. Maybe they would have stood there and let the hour run out and let the clerks call the names and let the children walk one by one down the boarding tube into the *Grace*, and Maren would have stood on the yellow line and watched Pell go. Maybe that would have been better. She never knew.

What happened was this.

The light over the boarding tube turned from green to amber, and the warm voice of Deputy Factor Vole said from the speakers, "*Families, thank you. The hour is ended. Wards will now form their rows for embarkation,*" and all through the dock a sound went up, a great helpless moan, four hundred parents at once. And across the floor, from somewhere near the centre, Maren heard an old cracked voice begin to sing.

*Down in the red where the weight comes on—*

It was Hatch. Maren saw her: a small grey figure in her cut-down habit, standing in the middle of the rows with her hands at her sides and her chin up, singing at the top of her voice, flat and loud and terrible.

*—down where the light's a bruise—*

And the parents moved. Every one, all at once, the way a shoal turns: they took their children by the hand and turned from the boarding tube and began to walk, not run, walk, toward the shuttle berths. Maren was on her feet with Pell's hand in hers before she knew she had stood. They walked. All round them the hymn was going up, ragged, four hundred voices, the breathers' hymn in a dozen keys, *bring up the breath for the ones who are owed*, and the children were singing too, the ones who knew it, and they were walking toward the berths, and the marines on the gantries had not moved, and for nine or ten steps — Maren would remember every one — it seemed possible.

Then the shuttle hatches opened, all six of them at once, and the marines came out.

* * *

There were perhaps sixty of them in the shuttles. Pell had been right. They had been waiting there since before the parents came up — sitting in the jump seats where the parents would sit, in the dark, with their rifles across their knees. They came out of the hatches at a run, in a black flood, and spread across the front of the berths in a line, and stopped, and raised their rifles.

The hymn faltered. The walking slowed. The four hundred parents and their children came to a ragged halt ten metres from the black line, and stood there, swaying, and the hymn went down to a few voices, and then to one. Hatch's. Still singing, alone, flat and cracked, *and bring yourself up if you choose.*

"*Families,*" said Vole's warm voice from the speakers. "*Please return the wards to their rows. No one will be harmed. Please return the wards to their rows.*"

Nobody moved.

"*Please.*"

And it was Farrant who broke it. Of course it was Farrant. He was at the front, near Berth Two, with a girl of about eight by the hand — his daughter, Maren supposed, or someone's — and he looked at the black line of marines ten metres in front of him, and he looked back at the *Grace* lying at the dock's port like a grey cliff, and he said, quite loudly, in a voice that carried across the whole dock, "*No,*" and pulled the girl behind him and ran at the line.

They shot him. Maren heard it — a flat hard crack that came back off the dock's high ceiling three times — and saw him stop as though he had walked into a wall, and sit down, and then lie down. The girl he had been holding stood beside him for a moment and then began to scream.

And then everyone was running.

* * *

She did not know afterward how much of it she saw and how much she put together later from the things other people told her and from the feed. She knew there was more shooting. She knew there were marines among the parents, wading in, pulling children away by the arms and the collars, and parents fighting them, and going down. She saw Ade Okoro lift a marine off his feet bodily and throw him into a gantry leg, and then disappear under three more. She saw Lin on the floor with her boy under her, covering him with her body. She did not see Hatch.

She had Pell's hand. That was all. She had Pell's hand and she was running, away from the berths and the black line, back across the dock toward the gantries at its far side, through the crowd, through the screaming, with no idea where she was going except *away*.

"*Not that way!*" Pell was pulling at her. "Mum! *Not that way*, there's a lock, they'll—*this* way!"

Maren let herself be pulled. Pell ran like a hare, low and fast, jinking through the crowd, toward the dock's inner wall where it met the station spindle, and there — behind a stack of cargo cradles, in a corner Maren would never have seen — was a hatch. A small square service hatch at knee height, with a grille over it.

"Laundry," gasped Pell. "Goes to the Nursery. We used to hide in it." She was on her knees, pulling at the grille. It came away in her hands. "Get in. Get in, Mum, you'll fit, I've seen Matron's fat cook—"

Maren got in. It was a chute, square, metal, slanting down into the dark. Pell came in after her feet first and pulled the grille closed behind them, and for a moment they lay there together in the chute in the dark, breathing, with the noise of the dock coming through the grille behind them like the roar of the head.

"Where does it go?"

"Down. To the Nursery laundry. Then there's a service way to the ward locks." Pell's voice was shaking. "The ward locks are where the shuttles come in on visitation days. There's always a shuttle on the ward lock. For the Nursery. For emergencies."

"Can you fly a shuttle?"

"No. Can you?"

"I can fly anything," said Maren, and let go of the edge of the chute.

* * *

They slid. It was a long way. They came out at the bottom in a tangle on a pile of grey smocks in a hot steamy room that smelled of soap, and Pell was up before Maren and pulling her by the hand again, through a door, down a white corridor that Maren half recognised — the visitation corridor, the children's side, the side of the glass she had never been on — and round a corner, and down a ramp, and they were nearly there. She could see it ahead, the lock to the ward berth, the little Nursery shuttle sitting beyond its window like a fat white grub.

And the marine came out of the side passage into the corridor ahead of them, and stopped, and raised his rifle.

He was young. That was what Maren saw. Under the black helmet he was perhaps twenty, with a soft station-born face and frightened eyes, and he had been running too; he was breathing hard. He must have been sent down from the dock to cover the ward locks, in case anyone thought of it. He looked at them over the barrel of his rifle — a breather in a coverall with blood on her face from somewhere, and a girl in a blue uniform — and his mouth opened and nothing came out.

"Let us by," said Maren.

"I can't."

"Let us by. Please. She's my daughter."

"I *can't*." His voice cracked. "They'll — I'll go on a bond, they'll — get down. Get down on the floor. Both of you. Please."

Maren let go of Pell's hand.

She did not remember deciding. She remembered the yellow lever on the *Gannet*'s console, under its hinged guard, in the downdraft, and her hand already on it. She went at the boy across four metres of white corridor, low, the way you go into a seam, and she heard the rifle go off over her head and felt the heat of it on her scalp, and then she was inside his reach and her shoulder was in his chest and they went down together, hard, on the white floor, and the rifle went skittering away. He was bigger than her. He was trained. She was a breather who had spent nineteen years hauling bottles. She got her hands on his helmet and slammed it into the floor, once, twice, and he got a knee into her stomach and she lost all her air and rolled away, and he was on his feet, and so was she, and she heard Pell scream.

There were two more marines at the top of the ramp. And between them, in her grey Nursery coat, with her hand already out, Matron Odile Brask.

"Pell," said Matron Brask. "Come here, dear."

Pell stood in the middle of the corridor, between Maren and the ramp, with her hands in fists at her sides.

"No," she said.

"Pell. Don't be silly. Come here."

"*No.*" Pell turned round. She looked at Maren. Her face was white and set and perfectly calm, and she was twelve, and she looked exactly like Maren's mother. "Mum—"

One of the marines behind Brask came down the ramp and took her by the arm.

Maren went for him. She got two steps. The young marine she had fought came off the wall behind her and hit her across the back of the head with something — his helmet, his fist, she never knew — and the white corridor tilted and went grey, and she was on her knees, and she was reaching, and she had Pell's hand. She had it. She had her daughter's hand in hers on the white floor of the Nursery corridor and she held on to it with everything she had, the way she held the yoke of the *Gannet* in the shear, the way she had held the dump lever, and the marine pulled, and Pell was pulled, and Maren held on.

"Let go," said someone above her. Brask. "For heaven's sake, let go, you'll break her arm."

She held on.

"*Mum,*" said Pell. "Mum, let go. Let go. It's all right." Her voice was very steady. "It's all right. I'll count. I'll count it all. Let go."

Somebody kicked her in the side. Somebody else knelt on her wrist. And her fingers opened, because fingers will, and Pell's hand slid out of hers like a rope going down into the red.

She lay on the white floor. She saw, sideways, from very low, the marine carry Pell up the ramp — not dragging her; Pell was walking, with his hand on her arm, very straight — and at the top of the ramp, beside Brask, Pell turned and looked back. Maren saw her lift her free hand. She saw her knock, three times, on the white wall of the corridor beside her: *still here.*

Then they were gone.

* * *

She did not know how long she lay there. She knew that at some point the young marine was gone, and the other one, and that she was alone in the corridor, and that she could not seem to make her legs work. She knew that there was a sound, very far away, that might have been more shooting or might have been the *Grace*'s boarding tube cycling. She knew that she was crying, and that it did not feel like crying; it felt like breathing, like the giant breathing out through her on a full draw, a long rushing exhale that went on and on.

And then there were hands on her. Not a marine's. Thin, soft, frightened hands, under her arms, pulling. And a voice, close to her ear, that she knew.

"Get up. Maren. Maren, get up, they're coming back, they're sweeping the whole Nursery level for anyone from the shuttles, you have to *get up*—"

Lucan Vey. His narrow face above her, whiter than the corridor.

"She's gone," Maren said. "They took her. She's on the *Grace*."

"I know. I know. Get up." He was hauling at her. He was not strong. "Please. I can't carry you. Please."

She got up. She did not know how. He got her arm across his shoulders, and they went — not toward the ward lock but away from it, through a door Maren had not seen, into another corridor, grey, plain, a staff corridor, and down it, and through another door, and down a stair, and through a door with a lock that Lucan opened with his palm, into a small dark room that smelled of dust and machine oil.

He let her down on the floor. He locked the door. And then he sat down on the floor too, with his back against it, and put his face in his hands, and shook.

* * *

The room was a storeroom on the Residence's lowest level, in the block that had been assigned, Lucan told her later, to the caretaker detail: twelve small rooms for twelve officers who would stay behind. Nobody came down here. Nobody had any reason to. The caretakers had not moved in yet; they would not be needed until the *Grace* had gone.

There was a feed screen on the wall. At the eighth bell, Lucan turned it on.

It was the Factor. He was standing in his office in the Residence with the long window behind him, and through it, in Hesper's shadow, Maren could see the grey cliff of the *Grace*'s flank. He was in his grey coat. He looked tired. He spoke to the screen the way he had spoken from the separator platform: gravely, and gently, as though to people he loved.

"*Families of Vigil. I am sorry to speak to you tonight in sorrow.*

"*This morning, at the farewell for the Scholarship wards, a small group of agitators attempted to disrupt the embarkation. They endangered the lives of the very children they claimed to protect. We were warned of their intention in advance, and were able to prevent the worst. But I must tell you that eleven people were killed, among them two marines. I grieve for every one.*

"*The wards are safely embarked. The convoy will depart as scheduled, at first bell tomorrow.*

"*The ringleaders are in custody. They are Agathe Hatch, Ruth Calloway, Adebayo Okoro, Simon Pym, and four others whose names will be posted. The Combine's law is clear on the penalty for inciting violence against its officers. I take no pleasure in it. Agathe Hatch will be liquidated at first bell tomorrow, before the convoy departs, and the others in the days that follow. The liquidation will be shown on the Sump's feed. I wish it were otherwise.*"

He looked into the screen for a moment, silent. Behind him the *Grace* lay in the dark like a promise.

"*Without the ledger,*" said Imre Castellane softly, "*there is only the knife.*"

The screen went dark.

Maren sat on the floor of the caretakers' storeroom with her back against a crate. She did not say anything. Across from her, Lucan was looking at the dead screen.

"We were warned," he said. "He said it. *We were warned of their intention in advance.*"

"I heard."

"They had the shuttle crews before the hour started. Ruth's people. Ade's people. They took them at the berths before you even came up — I saw them bring them in, at the start of shift, I didn't know what it meant. They knew which shuttles. They knew the signal. They had marines in the hulls." He looked at her. "Somebody told them. Somebody told them everything."

Maren did not answer.

She was counting. She could not help it; she had always counted, and Pell had always counted, it was the one thing they had in common that no one had ever given them. She was counting the lamps on the catwalks of the Drowned Decks, going out one by one along the water as the union went home. Fourteen people on an iron platform in the middle of a black lake, where nobody listened.

Hatch. Ruth. Ade. Pym. Farrant, who was dead. Moss. The Ellerys, two. Corley. Brand, Teo's mother. The two from the scrubber crews whose names she had never learned. Herself.

Thirteen.

She counted again. She came to the same place. She stopped there, and did not count the last one, and sat in the dark with her daughter's warmth still on her hands, and the fourteenth lamp going out at the edge of the platform before all the others, and did not let herself think the name.
