# Chapter 4: Liquid Assets

*The Bond · 2431*

He found her, in the end. She had spent two days trying to work out how to find him — a clerk in a grey jacket, in a station she was allowed to enter once a month for fifteen minutes under guard — and on the third day, coming off a short dive with a quarter-draw and no downdraft and nothing to dump, she went to rack her bottles in the Number Four tally house and found Siggard gone and a stranger at the weighing line with an auditor's slate.

"Combine audit," he said, to the room, not to her. "Routine. Racks one through twenty, please. Tally-master Siggard has been asked to wait in the office." He did not look at her once while he read her bottles. He read them slowly and carefully, touching each with his own wand, and when he came to the last one he wrote something on his slate and said, in exactly the same flat audit voice, "Bond H-11873, there's a discrepancy on your rack. Bring your bottle tags to the back store, please."

There was no discrepancy. Every breather on the line knew it, and a few of them looked at her the way you look at someone being called out of a crowd by a Combine man: sorry, and glad it wasn't them. She went through to the back store.

It was a cold cave cut into the ice behind the tally hall, stacked to the ceiling with empty bottles waiting to be cleaned, and it smelled of struck matches. He came in a minute after her and shut the door and stood with his back to it, breathing hard, as though he had run.

"You got it," he said. "The note."

"I got it."

"I didn't know if you'd understand. I didn't know if you'd tell someone. I didn't know—" He put a hand over his face. "Sorry. I haven't slept. My name's Lucan Vey. I'm in the Factor's office. Actuarial."

"I don't know what that is."

"I work out what things are worth," he said. "What people are worth. How long they'll live and how much they'll bring up before they die and what it costs to keep them alive in between. I've been doing it for four years. Your mother's on my tables. She's under *Expired, debt transferred.* So are you, under *Active.* I know your draw rate and your lung function and how many years you've got left at your current rate of exposure, and the answer's about nine." He took his hand down. His eyes were red-rimmed. "I'm sorry. That isn't why I came. I'm telling you so you'll understand that I'm not guessing about any of this. I'm the one who does the sums."

"What sale," said Maren.

He took a sheet of paper from inside his jacket. It was folded small, and when he unfolded it she saw that it had been printed and then cut down with a blade, the edges uneven, as if he had taken only what he dared from something larger. He held it out to her, and she took it, and she read it standing in the cold with the bottles rising round her like the pipes of some enormous dead organ.

> **HALLORAN-VASQUEZ COMBINE — VIGIL ACCOUNT HV-VIGIL-0001**
> **SCHEDULE C: LIQUID ASSETS FOR TRANSFER**
>
> Surety contracts, Hesper Nursery (412). Consigned to Brannock Deepworks Ltd., Corvid System, under master sale agreement BD-2431-07. Delivery on arrival. Transfer price per contract at schedule rate plus aptitude and health premium (Appx. 4).
>
> Total consignment value: 31,880,500 marks.

Underneath, one line had been cut from somewhere else and pasted on — she could see the faint edge of the strip — and it read:

> 0212 | TULL, P. | F | 12y | Health A | Aptitude A+ (numeric) | 88,400

She read it three times. She read the number at the end of Pell's line, and the word *numeric* in its little brackets, and she understood all at once, with the cold of the store going into her through the soles of her boots, exactly why her daughter was worth more than the girl on the line above her and the boy on the line below.

"What's Brannock Deepworks," she said.

"Mines," said Lucan. "In Corvid. Deep-crust mines on a heavy world — two and a half gravities. They use children in the narrow galleries, because children fit, and because their bones are still soft enough to take the weight if you start them young." He was looking at the floor. "Brannock buys indentures from all over the Combine. They pay a premium for aptitude because they train the clever ones as gang-tallies. Overseers. Pell would — she'd be kept out of the galleries, probably. With an A-plus. She'd be put to counting the others."

Maren was silent. She found that she had folded the sheet of paper in half and then in half again, small, and that her hand around it was a fist.

"There's more," said Lucan.

"Tell me."

"There's no rotation convoy," he said. "There's no convoy after this one. The *Grace* isn't coming back. Nothing's coming back." He made himself look at her. "The pins are failing. The Throat's drifting — it's been drifting for ten years — and to hold it open, the pins burn more breath every year, and the breath costs more every year to bring up, and last year the Vigil account ran at a loss for the first time since the colony was founded. The board in Halloran voted in the spring. They've invoked the Exodus Clause."

"I don't know what that is either."

"It's a clause in the colony charter. Every Combine colony has one. It says that when an account can no longer be profitably maintained, the Combine may withdraw its personnel and its liquid assets and place the account in Recovery." He said it like a man reciting. "The *Grace* takes the staff. All of us, from the Factor down. The *Grace* takes the breath stockpile — there's eight months of it in the tanks at High, enough to run the pins for the passage and sell the rest at Corvid. And the *Grace* takes the liquid assets. Schedule C. That's the children. They're the only thing on Vigil that's worth more than it costs to move."

"And us."

"You're not liquid," said Lucan. "You're fixed."

The store was very quiet. Out past the door she could hear the weighing line, the click of wands on bottles, someone laughing at something.

"The engineers give the pins eighteen months without fresh breath," he said. "Maybe two years. After that the Throat narrows. It doesn't close, exactly; it just stops being something a ship can pass. And after that—" He shrugged, a small helpless jerk of the shoulders. "Recovery. The account goes on the books as abandoned, and a lien's filed, and if the Throat ever opens again, the Combine sends a receiver to collect what's owed. They never close an account. Not really. They just stop coming."

"How long have you known."

"Four months." He said it to the floor. "I knew the Clause was being drafted. I didn't know about Schedule C until six weeks ago, when they asked me to price it. I priced it. That's my job. And then I went to my section chief and I asked—" He laughed, a short, terrible sound. "I asked whether the aptitude premium was *appropriate*. That was the word I used. I asked whether, given that the parents had been told it was a scholarship, the premium might be *reputationally sensitive*. And he looked at me for a while and then he said I was clearly very committed to Vigil, and it was good to have people who were committed to Vigil, because the Clause required a caretaker detail."

"A what?"

"Twelve officers," said Lucan. "To stay behind. Under sealed orders. To keep the account in order until Recovery." He looked up. "They put me on it the next morning. I'm staying, Mrs. Tull. When the *Grace* goes, I'm staying here with you. Eleven others and me, in an empty station, with thirty thousand people in the ice below who don't know yet what's been done to them." His voice cracked. "How long do you think we'd last, once they knew?"

So there it was. She had wondered, and now she knew, and it made her trust him a little more rather than less, because it was a reason she understood.

"You want them to know now," she said. "While you've got a chance to get on that ship."

"I want—" He stopped. "Yes. All right. Yes. I want there to be some other way this goes. I don't know what it is. I'm not brave. I'm not a— I don't know anything about—" He gestured, a vague sweep that took in the store, the Sump, the ice, the bonds. "There's a union. Isn't there? Down here. There's a woman who runs it. Everyone in the Factor's office knows her name, they joke about her. Hatch."

"I'm not union."

"I didn't think you were. That's why I came to you." He took a breath. "I asked around the tally lists for a breather with a ward on Schedule C who wasn't on the security watch. Somebody they'd believe. You're the best diver in the Sump, Mrs. Tull. You've never been union, never been disciplined, never been late on a tithe. People listen to you because you've never asked them to. If you take that sheet to Hatch, it's real. If I take it, it's a trap."

"And if they find it on me?"

"Then you'll tell them I gave it to you," said Lucan Vey simply, "and they'll put us both out of an airlock, and you'll have had a worse week than you were expecting." He put his hand on the door. "I have to go back. I've been in here too long for a discrepancy. Siggard will talk."

"Wait."

He waited.

"Why'd you write 0212," she said. "On the note. Why her? Why not just say a sale?"

He looked at her for a moment as though he did not understand the question.

"Because I knew you'd ask the price," he said. "Anybody would. And I thought — if you knew it — you wouldn't be able to stop."

* * *

She tried the honest way first. She owed herself that.

The bond office was on Level One, near the shuttle docks, a long counter behind a wire screen where Combine clerks sat at slates and dealt with the bonds' paper: transfers, inheritances, deaths, the endless small adjustments of the debt. Maren had been there perhaps a dozen times in her life. Once at nineteen, the day after her mother died, to press her thumb and take the debt. Once at thirty-two, the day after the accident on Number Two head that had cracked the *Gannet*'s first hull and put eighteen thousand marks of repairs on her bond in an afternoon, taking it over the forty-thousand line; she had stood at this counter and been told, very kindly, that the bond now required surety, and that her daughter would be collected from the crèche on Level Four that evening. She remembered the clerk's face. She remembered that he had had a cold, and kept apologising for it.

The clerk today was a woman she did not know, with short grey hair and a manner of terrible patience.

"I want to buy out a surety," Maren said.

"Ward number?"

"0212."

The woman tapped. Her face changed very slightly — a flicker, there and gone — and she tapped again. "That contract's committed."

"Committed to what?"

"The Scholarship." The woman looked up. "I'm sorry. All Scholarship contracts are committed as of the announcement. They're not available for buyout."

"I haven't told you what I'm offering."

"It doesn't matter what you're offering."

"I'm offering," Maren said, "everything. I'll take double draws. I'll dive the deep seams. I'll sign over the *Gannet*. I'll sign over my own bond — put it on her contract, put it all on me, I don't care what the number is. Tell me the number."

"There isn't a number," said the clerk. Not unkindly. Almost gently. "That's what committed means. It means it's not for sale."

Maren looked at her through the wire.

"It's for sale," she said. "It's just not for sale to me."

The woman's face did not change. But she looked, for a moment, down at her slate, and then up again, and in that look Maren saw that she knew. Maybe not all of it. Maybe not Brannock and the narrow galleries and the aptitude premium. But enough. Everyone in the Combine knew enough.

"Is there anything else, bond?" the clerk said.

"No," said Maren. "Count what's owed."

The woman's mouth moved, and for a second Maren thought she was going to answer — *and not a mark more* — the way anyone in the Sump would have. But she was not of the Sump. She only nodded, and called the next in line.

* * *

Ostrander's was a bar in the sense that it sold drink and had a counter. It was a cave on Level Six that had been a coolant pump room once, and still had the pumps in it, dead and huge, and the drinkers sat on the pump housings and on crates and on the floor, under a ceiling that dripped. It sold a spirit that Ostrander made himself from hydroponic waste, and the spirit was the colour of weak tea and tasted of solvent and was the only thing in the Sump, apart from children, that nobody had ever found a way to put on a bond.

Dov was where he always was, on the second pump housing from the door, with a cup between his hands and his back against the cold iron. He looked up when she came in and his face did a thing it had done for thirty years — lit up, and then caught itself, and settled into a kind of rumpled pleasure that pretended to be less than it was. He was fifty-five that year and looked seventy. He had flown tender for Maren's mother for twenty years and for Maren for eighteen, and in all that time she had never once seen him sober at Ostrander's or drunk on the dive line. He kept the two worlds as separate as a man could. It was, she sometimes thought, the most disciplined thing about him.

"Starboard six," he said. "I put a request in. They say a week."

"Let it wait."

"It can't wait, it's a frame member, you can't dive on a cracked frame—"

"Dov." She sat down next to him on the housing. "Give me some of that."

He gave her the cup without a word, which was how she knew she must look bad. She drank. It went down like a burning wire.

She told him. She did not show him the paper; she had not shown the paper to anyone, and it was inside her coverall against her skin, folded small, where Pell's tag hung on its cord. But she told him what it said, all of it, in a low voice with her head close to his, while around them the bar went on drinking and dripping and somebody at the back sang the first two lines of the breathers' hymn and gave up.

When she was done he was silent. He took the cup back and looked into it. His hands, she saw, were shaking, which they never did.

"Brannock," he said at last.

"You know it?"

"Everyone knows it. Everyone my age. They used to say it, when I was a boy — the old ones — when you were bad. *You'll go to Brannock.*" He drank. "I thought it was made up. Like a story."

"It's not made up."

"No." He put the cup down on the housing between them, very precisely, the way he put the *Patience* down in a cradle. "How many seats on the *Grace*?"

"What?"

"The *Grace*. How many does she carry? If she's taking the staff and the breath and four hundred kids—" He was frowning, doing the sum. "That's a big ship, but it's not that big. There'll be crew quarters. Escort berths. There'll be room."

"Room for what?"

"I don't know." He laughed, without humour. "I don't know, Maren. I'm just thinking out loud. I'm an old drunk sitting in a hole in the ice thinking about ships." He picked the cup up again. "We could run. You know that? The *Patience* could make the Throat. She's slow but she's got the legs for it. Six days at a steady burn."

"And then what? We've got no transit codes. No papers. The pickets would burn us before we got within a thousand klicks of the mouth."

"I know."

"And Pell would still be on the *Grace*."

"I know. I said I was thinking out loud." He drank. "I flew for your mother twenty years," he said. "Did I ever tell you about the day you were born?"

"About a hundred times."

"She dove that morning. She was eight months gone and she dove anyway, because it was a good seam and she was behind on the bond, and she came up with a full draw and her waters broke in the cradle. Right there in the *Patience*'s cradle. And I had to fly her home with her screaming in my ear and me screaming back at her that it was her own damned fault, and you came out in the shuttle lock at Level Two with me holding the light." He was looking at the dead pumps. "You were the ugliest thing I ever saw. You looked like a boiled rat."

"You've said."

"And she held you up, in the lock, all wet, and she said—" He stopped. His mouth worked. "She said, *Count what's owed, Dov.* That's what she said. First thing. And I said, *And not a mark more.* And she said, *No. Look at her. Count what's owed.*" He shook his head. "I never knew what she meant by it. I never asked."

Maren took the cup out of his hands and finished it.

"I'm going to Hatch," she said.

Dov didn't answer. He sat with his empty hands on his knees and his back against the dead iron and looked, for a moment, like a man trying to work out a sum that kept coming out different.

"Be careful," he said finally. "With Hatch. With all of them. Unions get people killed."

"So do scholarships."

"Yes," he said. "Yes, they do."

* * *

She did not go straight to Hatch. She went home first, to the two rooms on Spoke Nine, and sat on the bunk under Pell's eleven drawings with the heater ticking and not working, and took the folded paper out and smoothed it flat on her knee and read Pell's line again.

*Aptitude A+ (numeric).*

She had known since Pell was three. Pell had counted the drips from the ceiling of the old crèche on Level Four and told Maren, gravely, how many there would be by bedtime, and been right. At five she had done Maren's tally sheets for her, checking Siggard's arithmetic in a child's scrawl while Maren cooked, and found two errors in a month, and Maren had taken them to the tally house and been paid for them — the only time in her life the Combine had ever handed money back. At six, the day before they took her, she had asked Maren how much she was worth. Not as a joke. She had heard the word *surety* and looked it up on the crèche slate and wanted to know the figure. *Because if I know the figure,* she had said, *I can work out how long.*

Maren had not told her. She had not known. She had said: *You're not worth anything, love. You're mine.* And Pell had looked at her with that small, straight, considering mouth and said: *That's not how it works.*

Eighty-eight thousand four hundred.

She thought about the day her mother died. Ada Tull had died of the rattle at forty-six, in the clinic on Level Nine, drowning slowly in her own lungs while Sister Hatch sat with her and held a mask to her face that did nothing. Maren had been nineteen. She had sat on the other side of the bed. At the end, Ada had opened her eyes and looked at her and tried to say something, and couldn't, and had died with her mouth open on whatever it was. And the next morning a tally-man had come to the door of these two rooms with a slate, and Maren had pressed her thumb to it, and the slate had chimed, and she had owned her mother's debt.

She had never been angry about it. That was what struck her now, sitting on the bunk. Not once, in nineteen years, had she been properly angry about it. It was the weather. It was Tarrow. You did not get angry at a downdraft; you dumped your load and climbed. She had dumped and climbed and dumped and climbed, and kept her half of a steel disc on a cord against her skin, and held it up to the glass once a month to watch the other half line up with it, tooth to tooth, and believed — without ever once saying so to herself — that somewhere at the end of all the numbers there was a day when the two halves would be fitted together.

*That's not how it works.*

She folded the paper and put it back inside her coverall, and went out, and down.

* * *

The clinic was on Level Nine, at the bottom of the inhabited Sump, where the air was worst and the cold came through the sheeting in visible breath. It had been a medical bay once, in the colony's first years, and then a storeroom, and then for thirty years it had been Agathe Hatch's: twelve cots, a cabinet of drugs she bought or stole or made, and a curtain at the back behind which, everyone in the Sump knew and nobody said, the union met.

Hatch was sixty-four. She had been a nun once, in some order on some world that Maren had never heard of, and had come to Vigil on a mission contract forty years ago and somehow never gone home; she still wore the remains of a grey habit, cut down into a work smock, and she still had the nun's trick of looking at you as though she already knew what you were going to say and had forgiven you for it in advance. She was at the far cot when Maren came in, sitting with a boy of about fifteen whose chest was making the wet clicking sound of the early rattle, and she did not get up.

"Maren Tull," she said. "Sit down. You look like somebody died."

"Not yet."

"That's something." Hatch looked at her. "You're not sick."

"No."

"And you're not union. You've told me so to my face three times, I believe, over the years." The old woman's eyes were very pale and very steady. "So."

Maren took the paper out. She unfolded it. She did not hand it over; she held it out at arm's length, flat, and let Hatch read it in the cold light of the clinic, while the boy on the cot breathed his small wet breaths and did not wake.

Hatch read it twice. Then she reached out and took it, gently, out of Maren's hand, and read it a third time with her lips moving. When she looked up her face had changed. Not much. A tightening around the eyes; a set to the jaw that made her, for a moment, look less like a nun and more like what Maren supposed she must actually have been, all these years, behind the curtain.

"Where did you get this?"

"I'm not going to tell you that."

"Good." Hatch folded it. She did not give it back. "Is it real?"

"Yes."

"How do you know?"

"Because it's got my daughter's lung function on it," said Maren. "And her number. And her price."

Hatch was quiet a long moment. Down the row of cots, someone coughed and coughed and stopped.

"I've been waiting eleven years," said Sister Agathe Hatch at last, very softly, "for somebody to bring me a piece of paper like this." She got up. Her knees cracked. She went to the curtain at the back of the clinic and drew it aside, and behind it, in the dark, Maren saw a table, and chairs round it, and faces — four, five, six of them — turning toward the light.

"Come in," said Hatch. "Come in, Maren. You're union now."
