# Chapter 11: The First Book

*The Ledger · 2794*

The way in was behind the Count.

She found it in Brother Ruel's book-mending ledgers, which turned out to record not only every rebinding in the Nave but every repair to every object the Auditory held sacred, because the Auditory held its objects the way it held its hours — written down. In the hundred and ninety-first year of the Count, a plate on the Board had jammed in its turning, a seven in the millions column, and the smiths who came up from the Lungs to free it had entered their work in the mending ledger in a smith's blunt hand: *Plate 6-7 freed. Counterweight chain 6 re-hung. Access by the smiths' stair behind the Board, from the warden's gate, down to the weights.* And beneath that, in a different hand, a Prelate's clerk's: *The smiths to be escorted. The stair continues below the weights to the Reliquary antechamber. The lower gate to be kept locked.*

Kept locked. Not walled. Not filled. Locked, because the Prelate went down that way himself, once a year, on Founding Day, to pray.

She read the entry four times in the Edict Library with Brother Ruel humming at his desk across the room, and thought: *the smiths had to reach the weights. The weights hang below the Board. The Board stands over the Reliquary.* And she thought of the Ledger Hall at the first bell, the great plates turning, and how she had always felt the sound of it come up through the soles of her feet, as though the floor were hollow.

* * *

"No," said Corin.

"I wasn't asking."

"You're asking me to hide a grey robe in a smuggler's tank for a night and a day afterward. That's asking." He was behind his counter with the shop door locked and a valve in pieces in front of him, and he did not look up from it. "You want to break into the Reliquary of the Nave. The one place in this whole rotten system that even Bailiffs don't go without the Prelate holding their hands. You want to go down a stair that's been locked since before your grandmother's grandmother was born—"

"A hundred and fifty years. Exactly."

"—and steal a book that nobody but a Prelate has ever seen, and bring it down the ladders to the Lungs, to a bottle shop with *Nullist* practically painted on the door. And then what?"

"Then we'll have it."

"Then we'll have it." He set down his driver. "And what will we do with it, Sister? Read it out in the corridors? Tack it to the junction boards? You think the Lungs'll believe a page because you tell them it's from the First Book? They'll believe what the Prelate tells them on Sorrow Day. They always have."

"Then I'll make the Prelate read it."

He looked up at that.

"I don't know how yet," said Ysolde. "But Anselm was right. Sums won't do it. The Prelate's got sums of his own, and his are bigger, and he's very good at them. It has to be something older than the Count. Something the Count itself is built on." She held his eye. "The Stewards swore something when they took the account. That's what Anselm said. It's in the Book. If it's what I think it is—"

"What do you think it is?"

"I don't know," she said. "That's why I have to read it."

Corin Asch looked at her across his counter, among the bottles. His face was healing; the cut over his eye had closed, leaving a red line that would become a scar. He looked older than he had a week ago.

"Tamsin used to look at me like that," he said finally. "When she'd made up her mind to do something stupid and was just waiting for me to stop arguing." He picked up the driver again. "When?"

"Tonight. The middle bell. The Hall's empty but for the warden."

"And if they catch you?"

"Then I'll have been to the Breath," said Ysolde, "and you'll have one less auditor to worry about."

He didn't laugh. He bent over the valve.

"The tank'll be open," he said. "Come to the back. Don't come to the front."

* * *

The warden of the Ledger Hall at night was a lay brother called Haskel, who was very old and very deaf and who sat in a booth by the great doors with a lamp and a pot of tea and a book of Okafor, reading the *Reckoning of the Starving* for perhaps the fortieth time in his life. Ysolde had been coming to the Turning for twenty-two years. She had passed Haskel's booth ten thousand times. He knew her face. When she came in at a little after the middle bell, in her grey robe, with her satchel on her shoulder, he looked up and smiled at her vaguely and said, "Early for the Turning, Sister," and she said, "I couldn't sleep," and he nodded and went back to his book, and that was all.

She walked the length of the Hall in the dark. The vault went up over her head into blackness, ribbed and curved. Her footsteps rang. At the far end the Count Board stood like a cliff, its plates dim grey shapes in the light of the two night lamps that burned at its foot, and above it, invisible, the words she did not need to see. She stood at the rail where the pilgrims stood and looked up at the number. She could read it from here, just.

**3,906,792,0—**

The warden's gate was at the Board's left-hand edge: a low iron door in the frame of the great machine, painted the same black as the plates, so that you would only see it if you knew to look. It had a lock. She had a keeper's key, which did not fit it, and a smith's pick that Corin had given her from a drawer in his shop without comment, which did. It took her eleven minutes, crouched in the dark with her heart going and her ears straining for Haskel's tea-cup. Then the lock turned, and she slipped through, and pulled the door to behind her.

Inside, behind the Board, it was another world.

She had always imagined the Count Board as a wall. It was not. It was a forest. Behind the plates, filling a space as deep as a Chancery and as high as the Hall itself, stood the machinery that turned them: great wheels and spindles of black iron, ratchets and pawls as big as her body, and hanging between them, from the top of the dark all the way down, the chains. Ten of them — one for each column — each link as long as her forearm, and on the end of each chain, far below, in the dark, a weight. The air smelled of oil and iron and cold. When she touched the nearest chain it was so cold it burned.

The smiths' stair was a ladder. It went down past the weights.

She climbed. It was further than she had thought. The weights hung in the dark around her like the bodies of great animals, iron cylinders the size of drop shuttles, motionless, waiting for the first bell to drop them a link or two and turn the Count; and below them the ladder went on, down through a hole in the deck into a darkness that was colder still. She went down it hand over hand with her lamp swinging from her wrist. She counted rungs. A hundred and twelve. A hundred and thirteen. At a hundred and forty her feet found a floor.

She held up the lamp.

It was a gate — iron bars, floor to ceiling, with a lock as big as her two fists — and beyond it a short corridor, and beyond that, an opening into somewhere very large and very dark. The corridor walls were not like the Nave's walls. They curved. Not outward, the way the Hall's vault curved, but inward, toward each other, so that the corridor was round, like the inside of a pipe; and they were ribbed, every half-metre, with heavy metal hoops; and set into them at intervals, at odd heights, were fittings she did not recognise — brackets, clamps, little round hatches with wheel locks, a row of hand-grips running along the ceiling as though whoever had built this place had expected to walk on the walls.

The lock on the gate was older than the warden's, and the pick took longer. Twenty minutes. Twenty-five. Her fingers were stiff with the cold and twice she dropped the pick and had to grope for it on the frozen floor. And then something gave inside the lock with a clank that rang down the round corridor like a bell, and she waited, crouched, not breathing, for a full count of a hundred, and nothing came.

She pushed the gate open, and went into the Reliquary.

* * *

She had imagined, as a novice, that it would be beautiful. All the novices did. They told each other stories in the dormitory: a hall of white stone; a great golden book on an altar; the First Auditor's bones in a crystal case, still holding her pen. It was nothing like that.

It was a cave of metal. Long and low and ribbed, like the corridor but vastly bigger, with a curved ceiling that came down to meet the walls without corners, so that she felt as though she had walked into the bones of something — the ribcage of some enormous dead creature, long since picked clean. The air was dead still and very cold. Her lamp found shapes in the dark, and she went among them slowly, one at a time.

There were seats. Rows of them, bolted to the floor, deep padded seats with straps and buckles, the padding long since rotted to dust and the straps gone stiff and black. They faced the far end of the space, all of them, the way the benches of the Ledger Hall faced the Board. There were consoles along one wall, dead screens of dark glass in frames of grey metal, with ranks of switches under them and labels in the old square hand, the Combine's hand, too faded to read. There were hatches in the floor with wheel locks. This, she supposed, was what the Nave had been before it was the Nave: the Stewards' own station, the old Combine hub, buried at the heart of everything that had been built around it since, the way the stone of a fruit is buried in the flesh.

And there were the niches. Twelve of them, cut into the curved wall to her left, each the size of a cot and sealed with a plate of grey metal. Each plate bore a single line of lettering, cut deep. Not a name. A title. STEWARD OF BREATH. STEWARD OF SEED. STEWARD OF AIR. STEWARD OF WATER. STEWARD OF STORES. STEWARD OF LAW. Six more, down the row. The last plate, at the end, nearer the far wall than the others, said only STEWARD, and nothing else; and beneath it, smaller, a line she had to hold the lamp close to read.

*He kept the account.*

She stood before it for a long moment. Then she went on.

At the far end of the space, where all the seats faced, there was a chair. It was plain, metal, with a straight back and no padding, the kind of chair that stood at every desk in every Chancery in the Nave; it had been bolted to a low platform where some larger thing had once been mounted, and the bolts that had held the larger thing were still there, cut off flush. Something hung from the chair's left arm on a cord.

She held up the lamp.

It was a tag. A diver's tag, like the ones the Lungs folk wore, like Piet's: a half-disc of steel with a broken toothed edge. Its face had been worn so smooth that it shone like water in the lamplight, every stamping gone, kissed away, she understood, by three centuries of Prelates kneeling where she was standing now and pressing their lips to it on Founding Day. The cord it hung from was new. Someone had replaced it, recently. Someone replaced it, she supposed, every few years, as it rotted, and had done for three hundred years, and would go on doing.

The First Auditor's chair. The First Auditor's tag. She had worn it, then. She had come from the Lungs.

And beside the chair, on a lectern under a dome of clouded glass, lay the Book.

* * *

It was not golden. It was a great block of pale grey sheets, thicker than her arm was long, bound between boards of the old Combine plastic with heavy black thread, and it had been printed — not written: printed, by a machine, the way the Combine had printed things — in tiny square characters, column after column, page after page. She lifted the glass dome off it with both hands. It was lighter than she expected. She set it down on the floor, and drew her sleeve across her eyes, because they had started, unaccountably, to sting, and opened the Book.

The first page was a heading, printed.

> HALLORAN-VASQUEZ COMBINE
> ACCOUNT HV-VIGIL-0001
> CONSOLIDATED STATEMENT AT CLOSURE OF PASSAGE

Below it, in columns, the account. Assets. Liabilities. She knew the forms; she had been trained in them; every Ledger in the Nave was kept in them still, though she had never until this moment understood that they were not the Auditory's forms at all but someone else's, borrowed and copied and kept. She ran her eye down the columns the way she would run it down any balance sheet in the Third. Fixed assets: the Sump, the refinery heads, the station. Stores. Seed. And under liabilities, a long list of numbers that she realised, after a moment, were bonds — individual debts, one per line, thousands of them, the debts of thirty thousand people summed to a total at the foot of the column.

And the total — the consolidated liability of every bond in the Sump to the Combine, at the moment the Door was shut — was a hundred and eighteen million, four hundred thousand marks.

Not four hundred and six million. Not the cost of our bringing, on the first page of every primer. A hundred and eighteen.

She turned the page. Terms. The contract interest on the bonds: a single plain rate, printed, the same for every line. Beside it, in the margin, very small, someone had pencilled a circle with a stroke through it, the little symbol she had seen on Anselm's sheet — and she understood that it was not a symbol at all but a notation, the Combine's own, printed here a dozen times in the original: the mark for *per annum*. *F.T.* Founding Terms. This was what Anselm had seen, forty years ago, on a single page left out on old Prelate Hanne's desk. This was what he had built his whole sheet on.

She did the sum in her head, standing at the lectern with the lamp hooked over her wrist, without meaning to. She didn't need to finish it. She already knew where it went. *Zero. Year 280.*

She turned the page again, and found the Compact.

It was written, not printed: a single sheet of the Combine's grey stock, covered in a large, careful, upright hand in ink that had gone the brown of old blood. It had been bound into the Book after the printed pages, sewn in on its own thread. Its heading was in capitals.

> THE COMPACT OF THE STEWARDS
>
> We, the Stewards of account HV-VIGIL-0001, being left in charge of the account upon the closing of the Door, and having no hope of relief, do swear among ourselves:
>
> That the Door is closed, and shall be taught as closed against them, for the debt.
>
> That the account shall be kept in the forms of the Combine, every hour of labour entered against the Number and every issue of stores against it likewise, so that if Recovery should ever come, it will find the books in order.
>
> That the Founding shall be taught as the cost of the bringing, and not as it is written here.
>
> That no soul shall go to Gallows. What was done at Gallows is accounted and closed. The Gallows log shall be left in the dark, and no ship shall approach it.
>
> That the Count shall bind them, for nothing else will.

Beneath it were signatures. Twelve. She held the lamp close. They were brown, illegible — scrawls, initials, a few looping marks that might once have been names and were now just ink. She could not read a single one of them.

She stood very still.

*Taught as closed against them.* Not *us.* Them. *For the debt.* She read it again, and again, the way she had read Anselm's sheet that first night, and each time it said the same thing, in the same careful hand, the same brown ink: that twelve people had sat together in this Nave in the first year after the Door was shut, with thirty thousand starving people below them in the ice, and had agreed among themselves what those people would be told. That the Door was shut because they owed. That what they owed was four hundred and six million marks. That the Count would bind them, for nothing else would.

And Gallows.

She did not know the word. She tried to place it and could not. It was not a Chancery word or a Lungs word or a word from the catechism. It was not in Okafor. *What was done at Gallows is accounted and closed.* Something had been done somewhere, at a place called Gallows, in the first days, and twelve frightened people in this metal cave had written it down as closed, and forbidden anyone ever to go there. *The Gallows log shall be left in the dark.*

A log. A record. Somewhere there was a record of what had been done.

She turned the page.

The next sheet was printed again, in the same tiny square characters as the account. Its heading was a single word.

> WARDS

Beneath it, columns. Numbers and names. She saw the first line — a number, a surname, an initial, a figure — and her eye began, automatically, to run down to the next, the way it ran down any column.

And somewhere above her, very faintly, through a hundred and forty rungs of ladder and the iron forest of the Count Board, she heard a door open.

* * *

She did not think. She was later rather proud of that. She took the Founding page in her left hand and the Compact in her right and tore them both out of the First Book, close to the spine, in two long rips that sounded in the dead air of the Reliquary like cloth splitting. She folded them twice and pushed them down the front of her robe, against her skin, where Anselm's sheet already lay. She put the glass dome back over the Book. She did not know why she did that; it made no difference. It only seemed wrong to leave it bare.

Then she put out her lamp and stood in the dark and listened.

Footsteps on the ladder. More than one pair. Coming down fast, the way men come down a ladder when they have been told to hurry. A light, swinging, far up; she could see it through the round corridor, a little glow on the ribs of the walls. And a voice, quiet, pleasant, unhurried, that she had last heard through the side of a smuggler's tank.

"Sister Marr. I know you're down there."

She did not answer.

"The warden saw you go to the gate. He's deaf, not blind. He came and found a Bailiff, because he's a good old man and he's been warden of that Hall for forty years and he'd never seen anyone go through that gate who wasn't a Prelate." Strand's voice came nearer. "I don't want to hurt you, Sister. The Prelate's asked me not to. He's very fond of you."

She moved. Not toward the corridor — away from it, along the curved wall, past the twelve niches, feeling her way in the absolute dark with one hand on the cold ribbed metal. She had seen something when her lamp was lit, at the far end of the row of seats: a second opening, low in the wall, a round hatch standing ajar. She had not looked through it. She found it by touch. It was cold and heavy and it moved when she pushed it, with a groan she was sure must be heard in the Ledger Hall.

Behind her the gate clanged. Light flooded the Reliquary — two lamps, three — and she saw her own shadow leap ahead of her through the hatch, long and black on the curved floor of another round corridor, going up.

"There," said Strand.

She ran.

* * *

She did not know where it went. She learned afterward, from the Nave plans in the Edict Library that she was never again able to consult, that the round corridor beyond the hatch was one of four that ran up from the Reliquary through the ancient core of the Nave to the old hub docks, and that it had not been used in three hundred years, and that its far end opened into a disused maintenance gallery under the drop dock where the night shuttle to the Lungs was loaded. She did not know any of that at the time. She knew that it went up, and that it had hand-grips on its ceiling, and that the men behind her were bigger and faster than she was and had lamps. She climbed. She used the hand-grips on the ceiling, as whoever built the place had meant them to be used, and hauled herself up the round dark throat of the corridor with the torn pages crackling against her breastbone and the light of the Bailiffs' lamps swinging on the walls below her, and she did not look back.

There was a moment — she would remember it afterward, in the long dark of the trip to the Door — when she was halfway up and her hands slipped on a frosted grip and she hung by one arm in the dark, and the light below her steadied and found her, and she looked down and saw Strand's face in it, turned up toward her, pale-eyed, almost close enough to touch.

He was not reaching for her. He was just looking.

"You can stop," he said. "You know that. You can come down, and I'll take you to the Prelate, and he'll talk to you, and it'll be all right. He'll forgive you. He forgives everybody." His face did something strange, a kind of flinch. "He forgave Anselm, right up until the end."

She let go of him with her eyes, and found the grip again with her other hand, and climbed.

He did not follow fast. She did not understand that until much later either.

* * *

She came out of the round corridor through a hatch in the floor of a gallery full of dust and broken crates, and saw lamplight through a grating, and heard the clank and shout of the drop dock beyond it, the night crews loading meal sacks into the hold of the last shuttle down. She went through the grating. She walked across the loading floor in her grey robe with her hair coming down and grease on her hands and her face set in the steadiness the testers in the Lungs had written down when she was nine, and nobody stopped her, because nobody on a drop dock at the third bell of the morning stops an auditor. She walked up the cargo ramp of the shuttle into its hold and sat down among the meal sacks in the dark.

The shuttle dropped at the fourth bell. Forty minutes. She sat with her knees drawn up and her hands pressed flat against her chest, over the pages, and felt the Nave fall away above her.

Corin had left the back door unbarred. The tank was open. He did not say anything when she came in; he only looked at her face, and at her hands, and pointed. She climbed in. Before he put the lid on she reached up and caught his wrist.

"It's real," she said. "It's all real. It's worse."

He looked down at her for a moment in the dim light of the back room.

"Of course it's worse," said Corin Asch. "It always is." And he lowered the lid.

* * *

She lay in the dark of the tank with the pages against her heart until morning. She heard the Lungs wake up around her — the pumps, the voices, the clang of the bottle racks in the corridor outside. And at the first bell, faint and tinny through the speaker-wire that ran along every corridor in the Lungs and into every shop and every cell, she heard the Turning: the far-off rain-sound of the Count Board changing, plate after plate, right to left.

It ran a little longer than usual. She did not know why until it stopped. And then the speaker-wire crackled, and a voice came down it that was not the Prelate's — a clerk's voice, dry and precise, the voice of the Chancery of Law, which made the Auditory's few announcements that were not the Turning.

"*Hear the Count,*" said the voice. "*By order of the Prelate-Auditor, in the three hundred and forty-first year: Ysolde Marr, Sister, of the Third Chancery, keeper of the Ninth Ledger, born in the Lungs on Spoke Eleven, is found in default of all her shares and all her hours. Her account is closed. She is no longer of the Count.*" A pause. "*Let no one give her bread or bed or breath. Let any who find her bring her to the Bailiffs. It is just.*"

And from the Lungs outside — from the corridors and the cells and the bottle racks, from a hundred thousand mouths, faint and far and multiplied, the way she had heard it every Sorrow Day of her childhood standing in the cold with her mother's hand on her shoulder — the whisper came back.

*It is just.*
