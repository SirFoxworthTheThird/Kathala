# Chapter 6: Notice of Recovery

*The Door · 3190*

Haddie worked on it all night, up in her dome at the top of the spine, and Wren and I sat on the floor under the telescope and watched her do it, because neither of us could think of anywhere else to be.

The dome was the size of my cabin and twice as cold, and it was full of books. Not real books, mostly — not the kind you see in pictures, with paper and covers — but what we call books on Lanthorn, which is anything with the old hand on it that somebody has thought worth keeping. Hull plates. Lengths of bulkhead. Sheets of the brittle old plastic you sometimes find in the deep wrecks, crazed and yellow, covered in rows of square dead letters. And among them, wrapped in oilcloth on a shelf of their own, nine real ones: thick blocks of paper sewn between boards, swollen and stained, which Haddie had traded for over fifty years from pickers who'd found them in the Hulk and didn't know what they were. Count-books, she called them. The pages were ruled in columns and filled from edge to edge with tiny figures, and at the top of every single page, in the same square hand, was the same mark.

"That," said Haddie, tapping it with her pencil, "is what it keeps saying."

It was midnight by Lanthorn's clock. The herald sat on a folded blanket on her chart table, black and warm and purring in its slow breathing way, and every few minutes, without warning, it would fall silent and then speak its piece again in that calm, pleasant, inhuman voice. Haddie had a sheet of ledger paper in front of her, and she'd been writing on it since the ninth hour, crossing out, writing again. Her kelp-leaf had burned down to her fingers twice. She hadn't noticed.

"Read it to us," said Wren.

"It's not finished."

"Read what you've got."

Haddie looked at her over the top of her spectacles, which she'd made herself out of two lenses from a scope and a bent wire. She'd been looking at Wren like that, on and off, all night — a long, dry, measuring look, the look she gave the Door when she was trying to work out how much it had moved. Wren had looked back every time.

"All right," said Haddie. She picked up the sheet. "Mind, this is my best guess. Half these words I've only ever seen written, and some of them I've never seen at all, and the way it says things—" She shook her head. "It's like hearing a song you know sung backwards. But this is near enough."

She read it out.

*NOTICE OF RECOVERY.*

*To the debtors of account HV-VIGIL-0001, held by the Halloran-Vasquez Combine.*

*This account is in arrears. This account was placed in Recovery upon the closure of the passage. A receiver of the Combine has attended upon the passage since that date.*

*Upon the reopening of the passage, the receiver will attend upon the debtors. All assets of the account will be inventoried and recovered.*

*Debtors may present statements of payment, under the seal of an authorised officer of the Combine, for reconciliation.*

*Debtors are advised that resistance to recovery constitutes a further default.*

*This notice will repeat.*

She put the sheet down.

Nobody said anything for a while. The herald hummed. Out through the dome's scratched glass the Door hung where it always hung, a little smear of wrong stars on the black.

"What's a receiver?" said Wren.

"Somebody who receives," said Haddie. "What's owed."

"And who's the Combine?"

"I don't know." Haddie took her spectacles off and rubbed her eyes. "It's in the Count-books. Not much. The letters at the top — this, the H and the V — the old Count-keepers wrote it on every page, and there's a word for it in some of the oldest ones, and I've always read it *Combine*, but I never knew what it meant. A name. A family. A god. There are people in the Kettles still say it in their prayers, did you know that? The old believers. *For the Stewards, for the Combine.* They don't know what it means either."

"It means somebody we owe," I said.

They both looked at me.

"Well, that's what it says, isn't it?" I nodded at the sheet. "*Debtors.* That's us. *Assets.* That's ours. *Inventoried and recovered.* That's somebody coming through that Door with a list." I heard my own voice, flat and hard, a reeve's voice, and didn't like it. "I've done inventory, Haddie. I know what it sounds like."

Haddie didn't answer. Wren looked at me with her burned hands folded in her lap.

"What about the payment bit," she said. "Statements of payment. Under seal."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me ugly, the way it does when you're tired and frightened and it's the middle of the night. "Statements of payment. Look around you, girl. Look at this place. We haven't got payments. We haven't got seals. We've got nothing on this whole rock but debts and the people who owe them." I stopped. "Sorry."

"Don't be," said Wren. "You're right."

She got up. She went to Haddie's shelf, the one with the nine real books on it, and stood looking at them, with her back to us. She didn't touch them. After a minute she said, without turning round:

"Can you teach me to read that? The old hand?"

Haddie put her spectacles back on.

"I can try," she said. "It took me thirty years."

"I haven't got thirty years."

"No," said Haddie Coyle, "I don't suppose any of us have."

* * *

You can't keep a thing like that quiet on Lanthorn. I don't know who talked — I don't think anyone did, exactly. I think it just leaked out of us the way air leaks out of the lash, through a hundred little seams nobody can find. By the sixth hour of the next morning half the habitat knew that the old door-watcher had something up in her dome that had come out of the Door and was talking, and that the Margrave's reeve was mixed up in it, and that there was a vent-runner from Kell Deep with him with burned hands and a knife.

Lanthorn is fifty-three hulls lashed together with cable and foam and prayers, in the long shadow of Tarrow's rings where the old wrecks drifted and fetched up over the centuries. Nobody built it. It happened. Some of the hulls are ships, and some were stations once, and some are just pieces of things — a great curved shard of something that was probably a fuel tank, a section of spine with half a word on it in the old hand, a cargo pod the size of a hill. Eleven thousand people live in it. They're divers and scrubbers and pickers and tenders, mostly, and they're poor, and they're proud of being poor in the way people get when it's all they've got to be proud of. And they're governed — if that's the word — by the Lash Council, which is the eldest of every hull that has an eldest, sitting round a table in the old cargo pod and arguing until somebody gives up.

The Council sent for us at the eighth hour. We went, because there wasn't anywhere else to go. Haddie carried the herald in its blanket. Wren walked beside her. I walked behind, and I felt the eyes on me all the way down the spine — on my back, on my face — the way I'd felt them every day since I took off the reeve's coat seven years ago. Nobody on Lanthorn ever forgot who I'd been. I never asked them to.

There were thirty-odd elders round the table in the cargo pod, and three times that many people crowded in behind them, standing, and the air was hot and close and smelled of breath and fear. Old Mother Yelland of the Number Two hull was in the chair that morning. She was ninety, and blind, and had been the eldest of Number Two since before the breathing.

"Haddie Coyle," she said. "They tell me you've got a voice in a box."

"Something like that, Mother."

"Let us hear it."

So Haddie unwrapped the herald and put it on the table, and it lay there black and warm in front of all those people, and after a little while, the way it did, it went quiet and then spoke. Its calm voice filled the cargo pod. I watched the faces round the table while it talked — confusion, mostly, and then fear, as the strangeness of it got into them; that voice that was almost ours and wasn't. And then, at the back, among the standing ones, I saw something else. I saw an old woman with her hands pressed together under her chin, and her eyes shining. And a man beside her with his lips moving. And another.

When the voice stopped, Haddie read out her sheet. She read it plainly, without any feeling in it, and when she got to the word *Combine* there was a sound from the back of the pod, a kind of gasp, and a voice — a woman's, cracked with age — called out:

"*For the Stewards! For the Combine!*"

"Be quiet, Dorrit," said Mother Yelland, without turning her head.

"It's the Return, Mother! It's the Return they always promised! The Door's opening and they're coming to—"

"*To recover the assets,*" said Haddie, loudly, over her. "That's what it says, Dorrit. I don't know what your grandmother told you, but this thing doesn't say a word about taking anybody home. It says inventory. It says recovery. It says *resistance constitutes a further default.*" She slapped the sheet down on the table. "Whoever's on the other side of that Door isn't coming to save us. They're coming to collect."

There was uproar for a while after that. I'll not set it all down. People shouting, people crying, people asking questions nobody could answer. Somebody wanted to know if the voice could be made to stop. Somebody wanted to throw it into Tarrow. Somebody — a young tender from Number Nine, I think — wanted to know why the Margrave's reeve was standing in the Council pod, and that got a lot of agreement, and I stood there and let it.

It was Mother Yelland who stopped it. She didn't raise her voice. She just put her two old hands flat on the table, and waited, and one by one they quieted down, the way they'd been quieting down for her for sixty years.

"Where did it come from?" she said. "Not the Door. Before that. Who had it?"

Nobody answered for a moment. Then Wren stepped forward.

"The Margrave," she said. "Kask. Her tugs caught it, out by the Door. It was in her vault at Kell Deep. I took it."

The pod went very quiet.

"Then she'll be wanting it back," said Mother Yelland.

* * *

She wanted it back within the hour.

I was on the *Mendicant* when they came. I'd gone down to the docks to fuel her — I don't know why; I told myself it was habit, but I think part of me already knew — and I was standing on her wing with the breath line in my hand when the general band crackled on the dock speakers and the voice came on that I'd heard every day for nine years and hadn't heard for seven.

"People of Lanthorn," said the Margrave Kask. "This is your Margrave."

I put the breath line down.

She had a good voice for it. She always had. Low and slow and a little rough, a voice like a hand on the back of your neck, and she never shouted. Teodora Kask had been Margrave of Kell Deep for twenty-two years that spring. She had taken the refinery from old Margrave Hesk with eleven reeves and a cutting torch on a feast night, and she had held it since by being the person in the system who decided who got breath and who didn't. Breath was heat. Breath was air. Breath was the drive that moved every boat between every habitat. You could live without bread for a week and without the Margrave's breath for about three days. She knew that, and so did we, and she never once had to say it out loud. I'd stood behind her chair for nine years and never heard her say it.

"Something was taken from my house at Kell Deep four nights ago," she said. "It is property of the refinery. It was taken by a tithed runner, number Lanthorn twenty-one four-three-seven, who has broken her tithe and fled. I'm told she is on Lanthorn. I'm told the property is with her." A pause, just long enough. "I'm told she is in the company of my old reeve, Absalom Rook."

Out on the dock, the fuel hands had stopped working. They were looking at me.

"I'm a reasonable woman," said the Margrave. "I don't hold a hulk responsible for the stupidity of one runner. Return the property and the runner to my reeves, who are waiting off your lash, and Lanthorn's breath ration for this quarter will be increased by a tenth, in gratitude. Keep them, and the ration will be suspended until they are returned." Another pause. "You have until the twelfth hour. I'll be listening."

The speakers clicked off.

I looked out through the dock's big port. There they were: two cutters, sitting off the lash a few kilometres out, just past the edge of the ring shadow where the light was. Margrave's colours — the white open hand on the red, which I'd worn on my own coat once and painted on this very boat. Reeves' cutters, newer than mine, with the guns still on their noses.

I knew who'd be on them. I'd trained half of them.

I finished fuelling the *Mendicant*. Then I went back up the spine at a run.

* * *

I'll be honest about the next part, because I've promised to be: the Council voted to give her up.

Not all of them. Not Mother Yelland. But most. By the time I got back to the cargo pod they'd been at it for half an hour, and I could hear them from the spine, and when I came in through the hatch I saw how it had gone. Haddie was standing at the end of the table with the herald in her arms and a face like a cutting edge. Wren was beside her, very still. And round the table, elders who'd known Haddie Coyle for fifty years were looking at their hands.

"It's breath, Haddie," one of them was saying — old Fenn, from the tank hull. "It's the quarter's breath. We've got babies on Number Six. We've got the sick bay. Three days without the Margrave's breath and we're burning furniture—"

"She's nineteen," said Haddie.

"She's *tithed*. She's broken tithe. That's the Margrave's law, it's not ours to—"

"She's *nineteen*, Fenn, and you're going to hand her to the Margrave's reeves for a tenth more breath. What do you think they'll do with her? What do you think the Margrave does to runners who break tithe? Do you want to ask Rook? He knows."

They all looked at me.

I did know. I'd done it.

"Mother," I said, to Yelland. "Is it decided?"

The old blind woman turned her face toward my voice.

"It's decided," she said. "I'm sorry, Absalom. I voted against. But it's decided."

"Then I'm sorry too," I said, and I took Wren by the arm and Haddie by the other and walked them both out of the cargo pod, and nobody stopped me, because I'd been a reeve for nine years and they all remembered it.

We'd got as far as the dock spine when they came after us. Not the Council — they'd not have dared. A dozen of the younger ones, divers and tenders, who'd decided that if their elders weren't going to make it happen they would. They caught us at the last junction before the docks, in the narrow place where the spine bends round the old tank, and I put Haddie and Wren behind me and turned round.

I'll not dress it up. I hurt some of them. I hadn't been in a fight in seven years and I'd hoped I'd never be in another, but it turns out you don't forget, any more than you forget how to swim or how to stamp a tag. I broke a boy's wrist. I put a tender's head into the bulkhead hard enough that she sat down and didn't get up. I took a pipe across the shoulders from someone I never saw and kept my feet. And the whole time I could hear Wren behind me shouting something, and Haddie shouting at her, and when it was done and the ones still standing had backed off down the spine, I turned round and saw that Wren had my knife out again, and that she'd cut someone — a big lad from Number Nine, across the forearm — and was standing over him with it, shaking.

"Boat," I said. "Now."

* * *

The cutters saw us the moment we cleared the lash. I'd known they would. I lit the *Mendicant* and put her nose straight down into the ring shadow, into the dark, and I heard Haddie in the seat behind me say a word I'd not have thought she knew.

"They'll follow," said Wren.

"Let them."

"They've got guns."

"They've got guns and new boats and young pilots," I said, "and I've got thirty years of picking this skirt and a ship that doesn't care if she gets scratched. Hold on to something."

The ring shadow off Lanthorn is the worst place in the Tarrow system to fly, and the best place to hide. The rings themselves are dust and ice and gravel spread so thin you could fly through them for a week and never touch a stone, but in the shadow, where the old wrecks have fetched up for a thousand years, there's everything else: hull plates, tanks, spars, whole dead ships tumbling end over end, and between them the fine black grit that gets into your sensors and makes your scope a liar. Pickers fly it by feel. The Margrave's reeves fly it by instrument, because they're trained that way — I trained them that way — and instruments, in the shadow, will kill you.

I lost the first cutter in eleven minutes. Took her through a gap between two tumbling tanks that she didn't believe was there, and she broke off rather than try it, and by the time she'd gone round I was gone. The second one was better. She stayed on me for nearly an hour, through the wreck-field off the old ore hulk and down along the dark edge of the A-ring where the ice gets thick, and twice she fired on me — warning shots, close across the bow, the way I'd taught them. The second shot came close enough that Wren cried out.

"Who is it?" Haddie said. "Who's flying that thing?"

"Benno Strick," I said. I could tell by the way she turned. "I trained him. He was eighteen. He used to bring me tea."

I took the *Mendicant* down behind a moonlet — a lump of ice the size of the cargo pod, tumbling slow in the ring — and cut everything. Drive, heat, lights, scope. Let her drift in its shadow, dead as any other wreck in the field. And in the dark, in the cold, with Wren's breath fogging in the seat beside me and Haddie's in the seat behind and the herald purring on the deck between them in its blanket, we sat and listened to the band.

After a long time — forty minutes, maybe — the band crackled. A young man's voice. "*Rook.*"

I didn't answer.

"*Rook, I know you can hear me.*" A pause. "*She says it doesn't have to go like this. She says bring it in yourself and she'll forget about the runner. She'll forget about you. She says she always liked you.*" Another pause. "*She says you of all people should understand what it's for.*"

I didn't answer.

"*Fine,*" said Benno Strick, and the band clicked off, and after a while I saw the faint glow of his drive go past above us, a long way above, heading back toward Kell.

* * *

We stayed behind the moonlet for six hours. I let the heater up a little, once I was sure, and we ate the rest of the kelp cake, and nobody said much. Wren sat in the co-seat with her knees drawn up and my knife in her hand and looked out at the rings. Haddie, behind us, unwrapped one of the Count-books she'd stuffed into her coat before we left and sat reading it by the light of a hand-lamp, turning the pages carefully with her bent old fingers, her lips moving. Every so often she would stop and say a word out loud — an old word, in the ledger-tongue — and then, after a moment, Wren would say it after her, slowly, getting it wrong, getting it a little less wrong.

At the fourteenth hour the general band came on again. All channels. Every speaker in every habitat in the system, I found out later; every boat, every dock, every cell. The voice I'd stood behind for nine years.

"People of the system," said Teodora Kask. "This is the Margrave of Kell Deep."

Wren went still.

"Some of you will have heard by now that something has come out of the Door. That's true. It came out three weeks ago, and my people caught it, and I have had it read." I looked at Haddie; she had stopped turning pages. *Had it read.* So the Margrave had her own readers, her own Haddie, somewhere in her house at Kell — of course she did. "I want to tell you plainly what it says, before you hear it from thieves and old women. It says that the Door is opening. It says that there are people on the other side of it, and that they are coming, and that they believe we owe them a debt."

A pause. She was so good at pauses.

"I don't know if that's true," said the Margrave. "Neither do you. But I know this: when they come, they're going to want to talk to someone. And it had better be someone who can bargain. Someone who holds something they want. That's me. I hold the breath. I hold Kell Deep. I will treat with the Creditor on behalf of every habitat in this system, and I will get us the best terms that can be got." Her voice softened. "But to bargain, I need to know what we have. So I'm asking every habitat, every hull, every lash, to prepare for me a full and honest count. Every soul. Name, age, health, trade. My reeves will come round to collect them. Those who help, will be remembered when terms are made. Those who hinder—"

She let it hang. She didn't need to finish. She never did.

"Stand together," said the Margrave Kask, "and I'll get us through this. That's all."

The band clicked off.

I sat in the pilot's seat of my old reeve's cutter, in the dark behind a moonlet in the rings of Tarrow, and found that my hands had gone so tight on the yoke that I couldn't open them.

"A count," said Haddie, behind me, very quietly. "Name, age, health, trade."

"Inventory," I said.

"She's going to sell us." That was Wren. She hadn't moved; she was still looking out at the rings. "Isn't she? That's what treat means. She's going to find out what they want and she's going to give it to them, and she wants the list first so she knows what she's got to give."

"Yes."

"Every brat," said Wren. She said it so softly I almost didn't hear. "She said every brat in the Deep. That's what she'll pay with first. That's what she's got the most of, that's what costs her least." She turned round in the seat and looked at me, and her face in the dim light of the board was the face of a nine-year-old on a gangway, chin up, writing me down. "There are three hundred and twelve children in the vents at Kell Deep, Rook. You put a hundred and forty of them there. I counted. I've had ten years to count."

"It's not my business any more," I said.

I said it the way you'd say a thing you'd been saying for seven years, every night, to the ceiling. It came out easily. It always had.

Behind me, Haddie closed her Count-book. I heard the soft thump of the boards coming together.

"Absalom," she said. "It was your business once."

I didn't answer her. I sat there with my hands on the yoke for a long time, with the two of them not saying anything, waiting, and the herald on the deck between us purring away in its dark warm patient voice like something that had all the time in the world.

Then I lit the drive, and turned the *Mendicant*'s nose out of the moonlet's shadow, toward Kell.
