# Chapter 8: Catechism

*The Ledger · 2794*

The Prelate's rooms were at the very top of the Nave, in the old hub where the spin was gentlest, and they had a window.

Ysolde had heard of the window all her life, the way novices heard of everything in the Prelate's household — in whispers, embellished. It was said to be the largest pane of glass in the system. It was said to have been salvaged from a Steward's ship. It was said that the First Auditor herself had stood at it on the night she finished the first Count and wept. When the novice who had brought her the invitation showed her through the last door at the eighth bell and stepped back, she saw that at least the first of these things was true, and forgot the others entirely.

Tarrow filled it.

The Nave hung over Hesper's night side, and Hesper hung in Tarrow's shadow, and so the giant did not shine; it loomed. It was a darkness that took up the whole of the window and more, banded in the faintest possible reds and umbers by the light of the Vigil star coming round its limb, and across the middle of it, edge-on, the rings ran like a cut. Far down in the dark she could see the storms — the small pale flickers of lightning in the deep decks, where the divers went. Somewhere down there, tonight, someone's brother was falling through the red with a tag on a cord at his throat.

"It's too much, isn't it?" said a voice behind her. "I've lived with it for eleven years and it's still too much. I eat with my back to it most nights."

The Prelate-Auditor was standing at a small table by the far wall, in his shirtsleeves, with an apron over his white trousers and a wooden spoon in his hand.

"I cook for myself," said Aurel Quist. "My household think it's an eccentricity. It is. Sit down, Sister. It's only beans."

* * *

It was only beans. Hesper beans, from the hydroponic vaults under the Nave, cooked slowly with an onion and a piece of salt pork that must have cost him a month's ration and that he served to her without comment. There was bread. There was a jug of water, and a small flask of something gold that he poured into two tiny cups and that tasted of smoke and burned her throat. They ate at the little table with the giant at her back, and the Prelate talked.

He talked about arithmetic. He had said he would, and he did. He talked about the old masters — Brenner, whose *Tables* she had learned from, and Okafor, whose *Reckoning of the Starving* was the great history of the first century, and the anonymous keeper of the Fourth Ledger in the ninetieth year who had found a way to sum a column of a thousand figures by folding the page. He talked about the Count Board, and how it had been built, and the names of the nine smiths who had cut its plates. He talked about the beauty of a balanced ledger — he used that word, *beauty* — and the way a column of numbers that had been kept true for a century had a kind of grain to it, like wood, that you could feel under your hand if you had kept enough of them.

"You've felt it," he said. "I know you have. Anselm said you were a counter. Do you know what he meant by that?"

"I think he meant I'm careful."

"No. Plenty of keepers are careful. Careful's nothing. Careful's a clerk." He refilled her tiny cup. "A counter is someone who loves the Count. Not the number on the Board — the *Count*. The act of it. The writing down of every hour, every breath, every life, so that none of it is lost." He smiled. "Do you know, I think that's the most extraordinary thing about us. That's the thing nobody beyond the Door would understand, if they came. We're the only people in the history of the world who've written everything down. Every child born in the Lungs for three hundred and forty years is in a Ledger somewhere. Every hour they worked. Every mark they paid. Nobody is forgotten here. Nobody is allowed to be." He shook his head. "I find that very beautiful. I always have."

"So do I," said Ysolde, and found, to her surprise, that it was true. That it was still true.

"Of course you do. You came up from the Lungs at nine and wept for a week in the dormitory and then stopped and never wept again. Didn't you? Don't look like that; I've read your file. I read all their files." He sipped. "You stopped because weeping didn't balance."

She put her cup down very carefully.

"Forgive me," said Quist gently. "That was a cruelty. I'm an old man and I forget myself." He turned his cup in his fingers, and the light caught the seal ring on his right hand — the ring that every novice was taught to kiss and none was permitted to look at closely, black and heavy and old. "Let me tell you a story instead. You know it already. Everyone does. But I like the way I tell it."

* * *

"When the Door was shut," said the Prelate-Auditor, "there were thirty thousand people in the ice of Hesper and twelve Stewards in the Nave, and seed and stores for perhaps a third of them.

"The Combine had gone. It had shut the Door behind it — against us, for the debt we owed and could not pay — and left the Stewards here to keep the account until the debt was settled. That's all the old books tell us of it, and it's all we need to know. Twelve men and women, in an empty station, with thirty thousand starving people below them in the dark.

"You know what happened next. The Starving. Ten years in which half of the owed died. The scrubbers failing, the hydroponics failing, the cold coming in. Riots in the ice. Murder over a cup of meal. Children eaten — Okafor says it, in the *Reckoning*; I believe him. And through all of it, the Stewards held the seed vault. They didn't open it. They couldn't; if they had opened it to every hand that reached for it, it would have been eaten in a month and then there'd have been nothing to plant. So they held it, and they rationed it, and people cursed them, and people died at the doors of the Nave screaming for bread, and the Stewards stood behind those doors and kept the count of what was left.

"And there was a child," said Quist.

He put his cup down. In the window behind her, far down in the dark of the giant, the lightning flickered.

"We don't know much about her childhood. She was young when the Door was shut — very young. She was one of a number of children who came into the Stewards' care in the first years, orphans of the Starving, I suppose, or children whose parents gave them up so they'd be fed. And she was quick. She learned her numbers at the Stewards' knees. And when she was grown — when she was about your age, I think — she came to them and she said: *The people are dying because they don't believe their work is worth anything. They dig and they dive and they bring up the breath, and they don't know where it goes. Let me write it down.*

"And they let her. And she wrote the first Count. Every hour of every person in the ice, entered against the Holy Number. Every mouthful of meal from the vault, entered against it too. So that a diver who went down into the red would know, when he came up, that his hours had been written in the book, and that his children would eat because of them, and that one day, when there were enough hours, the Count would be zero and the Door would open." He spread his hands. "And people believed her. And they went back to work. And the Starving ended. Not all at once — it took another generation — but it ended. The Count ended it. That's not a story. That's history. Okafor proves it."

Ysolde said nothing. Her hands were folded in her lap.

"*Count what is owed and not a mark more,*" said Quist. "She wrote that at the head of the first page. I've seen it, you know. The page. It's in the Reliquary." He smiled. "I've always loved that she wrote *not a mark more*. As though she knew. As though she knew that somebody, some day, would be tempted."

"Tempted to what, Prelate?"

"To make it heavier," he said simply. "Or lighter. Either one. To change it." He picked up his cup again and looked into it. "Brother Anselm was troubled at the end. Did you know that?"

There it was. She had been waiting for it since the eighth bell, the way you wait in the dark of a dive for the floor to drop away, and still it came as a shock.

"No, Prelate," she said. "I didn't know him well."

"No. Few did." Quist turned the cup. "He came to me last winter. He was troubled about the Interest. He'd been reading the old books — the second-century volumes of the Ninth — and he'd found something about them he didn't like. He wanted to talk to me about it." He looked up. "I wonder if you've found anything you don't like, Sister. In the old volumes. Bailiff-Captain Strand tells me you've been in the stacks a good deal, these last nights. And down in the deep spokes on inspection. Very proper."

"The Ninth's a big book, Prelate. I'm trying to learn it."

"Of course you are." He held her eyes. They were brown, and kind, and very tired. "I'll tell you what I told Anselm," he said. "Because I liked him, and I like you, and I would not want you to make his mistake.

"Suppose," said the Prelate-Auditor, "that someone were to discover tomorrow that the Count was wrong. Never mind how. An error in the old books, a slip of some long-dead keeper's pen. Suppose they found that the Count ought to be — oh — a little less than it is. Or a great deal less. Or nothing at all." He watched her. "What do you think would happen?

"I'll tell you. The Lungs would stop diving. Not all at once. But they would. Why go down into the red, if the Count was zero and the Door was still shut? Why die at forty of the rattle, for nothing? And with no divers, there would be no breath, and with no breath there would be no heat in the ice and no drive for the drop shuttles and no power for the hydroponic vaults. And the Nave would stop sending meal down the ladders, because there would be no meal to send. And the Lungs would come up the ladders to take it. And the Bailiffs would meet them at the docks." His voice was very quiet. "We'd be back in the Starving inside a year. A hundred and ninety thousand people. I've done the sum, Ysolde. I've done it many times. I'm sure you could do it too."

Behind her, the giant hung in the dark. She could feel it there, the way you feel someone standing behind you.

"The Count," said Aurel Quist, "is not a number. It never was. It's the thing that holds us together. It's the only thing that ever has. And a man who would take it away from a hundred and ninety thousand people — however right his arithmetic — is not a counter. He's a murderer who hasn't done the sum." He smiled at her, sadly. "Anselm hadn't done the sum. I tried to show him. I don't think he understood."

"Is that why he went to the Breath?"

She had not meant to say it. It came out of her like a breath let go at depth.

Quist did not flinch. He looked at her for a long, long moment across the little table, with the beans gone cold in their bowls between them; and then he got up, slowly, leaning on the table, and came round it, and put his old hand on her shoulder, very lightly, the way her mother had in the corridor on Sorrow Day.

"Brother Anselm went to the Breath," he said, "because the rail at the Number Two gallery is low. I've had it raised. Good night, Sister. Thank you for eating my beans."

* * *

She did not sleep. She lay on the narrow bunk in her cell in the Third Chancery's dormitory wing with her eyes open in the dark and went over it, again and again, the way she went over a column that would not balance: every word, every pause. *I've done the sum.* *Not a counter. A murderer who hasn't done the sum.* *I've had it raised.*

He knew. That was the first thing. He knew about the Interest, about the iron-gall, about the pages cut out of the old books. He had not said so. He had not needed to. And he had told her, as plainly as a Prelate-Auditor could tell anyone anything, why it did not matter.

And the worst of it was that she did not know if he was wrong.

She lay and did his sum. She could not help it. A hundred and ninety thousand people. The breath that kept them warm. The divers who went down for it. If the Count were gone—

At the fourth bell of the morning there was a knock at her door.

It was a novice — not the same one; a girl this time, younger, from the Lungs by her accent — with a folded paper. "From the drop dock, Sister. A diver brought it up on the night shuttle and asked for you by name. He said it was family."

It was from Piet. It was written in pencil, in his big careless hand, on the back of a tally slip.

*Yz — don't be cross. Dutiful's crew got posted in default last night. Bailiffs say if we don't make it up by the Reckoning they'll take the boat and put us on refinery share. Fourteen months. I'll be all right. Don't do anything stupid. — P.*

She read it twice. Then she got up and dressed in the dark and went down through the sleeping Nave to the Third Chancery, and let herself in with her keeper's key, and lit the lamp at Anselm's desk — her desk — and opened the two hundred and sixth volume of the Ninth Ledger.

The *Dutiful* was there. Spoke Eighteen, the Harrow family's boat; crew of five, including *Marr, Piet, diver, share one-fifth*. She found the line. She found the red default mark beside it, entered by some Bailiff's clerk the evening before. She found the shortfall: forty-one hours.

Forty-one hours. That was all. Forty-one hours of diving, out of the four hundred and some the crew had logged that quarter. A lost dive to weather and a scoop relining. That was what would take her brother's boat away and send him into the refinery for fourteen months, to work the separators on the Number Two head beside the gallery where Anselm's lamp had been found.

She thought about the Count Board. She thought about three billion, nine hundred and seven million marks.

She dipped her pen.

It took no time. It was the easiest thing she had ever done, and her hand shook so badly while she did it that she had to stop twice and hold her wrist with her other hand. She entered forty-one hours to the *Dutiful*'s crew under the date of the last dive, in her own small careful keeper's hand, as though the dive had gone longer than it had. She carried the hours to the page total. She carried the page total to the volume. She struck the default mark through with a single neat line and initialled it, the way a keeper did when an error was corrected. It looked exactly like what it was not.

Then she sat back from the desk and looked at it for a long time.

It was a lie. It was the first lie she had ever written in a Ledger. In twenty-two years she had written perhaps sixty thousand pages, and every figure on every one of them had been true, and she had been proud of it, prouder of it than of anything; and now there were forty-one hours of nothing in the two hundred and sixth volume of the Ninth, in her hand, and her brother would keep his boat.

And — she understood it slowly, sitting there, the way the cold had come into her in Corin's tank — it was not a lie. Not really. Not by the Count's own law. The Count had been zero for thirty-nine years. Every hour her brother had worked in his life had gone into nothing. She had not taken forty-one hours from the account. The account had taken his whole life from him, and she had given back forty-one hours of it.

She turned the pages. Spoke Nineteen. Spoke Twenty. Red marks, here and there, all through the deep spokes. Default. Default. Arrears.

She dipped her pen again.

* * *

"Why?" said Corin Asch.

It was the next evening, in the back room of the bottle shop, among the great hollow tanks. His face was still swollen from the Bailiffs' visit, the cut over his eye scabbed black, and he was sitting on the bench with his mark-stick across his knees and looking at her as though she were a valve he could not get to seat.

"I told you why."

"You told me you've been cooking your own ledger. Taking families out of default. Nineteen of them, you said, in two nights." He turned the stick in his scarred hands. "I believe you. I just don't know why you'd do it."

"Because it's already paid."

"That didn't stop you before."

"I didn't *know* before."

"You knew three days ago. You stood in this room and told me Year three hundred and two." He leaned forward. "Something's happened."

She told him about the Prelate. She had not meant to. It came out in pieces, the beans and the window and the gold drink and the story of the child at the Stewards' knees, and at the end of it the old man's hand on her shoulder and his voice saying *I've had it raised*. Corin listened without interrupting, without moving, and when she was done he sat for a long time looking at the floor.

"So he knows," he said at last.

"He knows."

"And he thinks it's worth it. The lie."

"He thinks without it we'd starve." She heard her own voice, thin and strange. "And I can't prove he's wrong, Corin. I've been doing his sum for a day and a night and I can't make it come out any other way. If the Lungs stopped diving—"

"Then the Nave would have to come down the ladders and ask us nicely," said Corin, "instead of sending Bailiffs." He looked up at her, and his ruined face was very hard. "You think the Lungs would stop diving if the Count was zero? Sister, the Lungs dive because it's the only thing there is to do and the only way anybody eats. That's not faith. That's weather. We'd dive tomorrow if the Count Board fell off the wall. We'd just stop sending our children down at fourteen to pay a debt that doesn't exist." He set the mark-stick down beside him on the bench, carefully. "Your Prelate's sum has a wrong figure in it. He's counted us as cattle that only walk while you whip them. He's never once been down a ladder to check."

She did not answer. She found she could not.

"Anselm said something like that," Corin said, more quietly. "Near the end. He said the Prelate had all the right numbers in the wrong places." He was quiet a moment. "He said there was only one thing that would ever make the Nave listen. Not sums. They'd only ever answer sums with more sums. He said it would have to be something older than the Count."

"What?"

"The First Book."

She looked at him.

"*Per F.B.*," said Corin. "You saw it on his sheet. That's what it meant. The First Book. The ledger from before the First Auditor — the Stewards' own book, from the very beginning. When the Door was shut. It's supposed to have the Founding in it, the real one, the figure the Combine left. It's supposed to have what the Stewards swore when they took the account." He shrugged. "Anselm saw a page of it once. Forty-odd years ago. He was a novice copying for old Prelate Hanne, and the Prelate left a page out on his desk one night — just one page — and Anselm read it before he could stop himself. There was a number on it. The Founding. It wasn't the number in the primers." He looked at her. "He never forgot it. He built his whole sheet on it."

"Where is it?"

"Where would it be? Where everything is that you lot don't want us to see." Corin's mouth twisted. "In the Reliquary. Under your Ledger Hall. Where only the Prelate goes, on Founding Day, to pray."

"Anselm wanted to get in."

"He wanted to. He was sixty-eight, Sister, with knees like rotten ice. He couldn't climb a ladder without stopping halfway." Corin looked at her steadily. "He used to say it was a pity he'd never had a daughter. Somebody young. Somebody who could count."

* * *

She came up on the night shuttle and went to the Turning at first bell, because she always went to the Turning, and she stood among the pilgrims at the rail with her hands folded in her grey sleeves and watched the Count Board change.

The clatter came from the right, as it always did. The units, the tens, the hundreds, rushing over like rain; the thousands, slowing; the tens of thousands, slower. It ran out further left than it had in days. She knew why. She had done it. Nineteen families in the deep spokes, taken out of default — four hundred and thirty-eight hours of tithe, entered in the Ninth Ledger in her careful hand, summed in the Third Chancery last night with all the rest and carried up here, to the Board, and subtracted. The great plates fell and settled.

**3,906,871,104**

The pilgrims at the rail touched their tags. An old woman beside her, a diver's widow by her coat, let out a little breath of satisfaction at the size of the fall. *A good night,* she said to nobody. *A good night for the Lungs.* And she began the response, under her breath, as she always would, as they all always would.

*What are we?*

*The owed.*

Ysolde looked up past the Board at the words cut into the vault above it. COUNT WHAT IS OWED AND NOT A MARK MORE. She thought of a child at the Stewards' knees, learning her numbers in the Starving. She thought of a page in the Reliquary, under her feet, under this floor, in the dark.

*Why is the Door shut?*

*Because the account is open.*

Every person in this hall was watching a lie get smaller. She had made it smaller. Four hundred and thirty-eight hours' worth. It was still three billion, nine hundred and six million marks of nothing, and tomorrow it would still be that, and the day after, and at the Reckoning it would leap upward again by its invented sorrow and the Lungs would weep, and she could cook the Ninth Ledger every night for the rest of her life and never make a mark of difference to it.

*When will it open?*

*When the Count is zero.*

Sums. They would only ever answer sums with more sums. It would have to be something older.

*Who keeps the Count?*

She did not say the response. She stood at the rail with her hands in her sleeves and her eyes on the vault, and said, in her head, very quietly, a different one.

*I do.*
