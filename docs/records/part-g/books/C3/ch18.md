# Chapter 18: What I Owed

*The Door · 3190*

It was Benno Strick who opened the door, and Haddie Coyle standing behind him with a lamp.

I didn't understand it at first. I was on the floor of the dead storeroom where they'd left me, with one eye swollen shut and my ribs telling me things I didn't want to hear, and the light came in round the edge of the door and then flooded in, and there was Benno's long earnest face looking down at me, white, and behind his shoulder an old woman in two coats and cracked spectacles with her mouth set in a line like a cut.

"Get up, Absalom," said Haddie. "You're making a spectacle of yourself."

"How—"

"I walked." She came past Benno into the storeroom and knelt down beside me, slowly, with a crack of old knees. "Up from the core, the long way, through those corridors of yours. It took me most of a day. And then I came out on the main dock in front of a hundred of Kask's reeves and sat down on a crate and asked for Benno Strick by name." She put her hand on my face, the cold bent fingers, turning it to the light to look at my eye. "His grandmother was a friend of mine. Did you know that? Ilse Strick. We dived together on the *Fenwick* when we were girls. I held him when he was a day old." She looked up at Benno. "I told him that. And then I told him what you went to the Deep for, and what you were going to do at the Spider. And then I asked him what his grandmother would have said."

Benno was looking at the floor.

"She'd have said I was a bloody idiot," he said. "She said it most days anyway."

"There," said Haddie. "Now help me get him up."

* * *

They told me while I got my breath, sitting on a crate in the storeroom with the door shut and Benno's lamp turned down. They told me fast, because there wasn't time to tell it any other way.

The receiver was coming through.

Not the tenders. Not the drones. The thing itself — the big one Haddie had seen behind the Door, waiting. The Door had opened enough. Benno had heard it from Kask's own mouth, in the dock control room, four hours ago, where she'd been talking to the receiver on the band the way she'd talk to an old friend. It had told her that the passage was at threshold. That it would begin its transit within the day. That the factor should have the depot ready for loading.

"It'll take everybody," said Benno. His voice shook. "Every pod. There's eleven thousand on the outer arms already, Rook, and more coming in every hour. It'll load them all, and Kask's household, and go back through. She says it'll take two days to load. She says—" He stopped.

"Says what?"

"She says once it's through, she'll go out to the Spider." He looked at me. "To Gallows. The receiver told her. About the station. It said Gallows controls the passage — that whoever holds Gallows can close it. She asked it a lot of questions about that." He swallowed. "She said to me, Benno, once that thing's through and loaded, and it's sitting on this side of the Door with everything it's taken, it's going to want to go home. And I'm going to be sitting at Gallows with my hand on the Door. And then we'll talk about my commission."

"She's going to hold it hostage," said Haddie softly. "The receiver. She's going to sit at the Spider and threaten to shut the Door on it, unless it pays her more."

"And if it doesn't?"

Benno didn't answer.

"She'll shut it," I said. "With all of them on the wrong side. With every pod aboard." I looked at him. "She'll shut it and keep Kell Deep and be Margrave of a system with nobody in it but her reeves."

"I don't know," said Benno Strick miserably. "I don't know what she'll do. I've never known. Nobody has."

"Where's Wren?"

"In the holding bay on Arm Four. With the children. All the ones from the core." He looked up. "She's keeping them back. Not podding them. She wants to give them to the receiver herself, when it docks. As a gift. She says it'll look well." His face twisted. "She says it'll be remembered when terms are made."

I got up. My ribs said a number of things. I didn't listen.

"Arm Four," I said. "How many reeves?"

"Six. And drones." Benno looked at me. "You can't. You can barely stand."

"I've barely stood before," I said. "Get me a knife."

* * *

Arm Four was one of the Hulk's old docking arms, a long tube half a kilometre out from the station's main ring with berths along its length. They'd stacked pods along its whole outer wall, three deep, under the dead ports, so that walking down it was like walking down a corridor in a cemetery with the graves standing up. At its far end there was a bay — an old cargo hold with a heavy door — and outside the door, under a work-lamp, sat two of Kask's reeves on crates, playing cards.

Benno walked down to them. I watched from behind the last stack of pods with Haddie at my shoulder. He walked down the middle of the arm in his red coat with his lamp held up, easy, the way a reeve walks when he's on his own side, and the two of them looked up from their cards and one said *Benno*, and Benno said *Margrave wants a count of the kids*, and they both got up.

He'd told me there were six. There were six. Two at the door and four inside, and drones — three of them, standing in a row along the bay's back wall, quite still, like furniture. I went in behind Benno while the first two reeves were looking at his lamp.

I'll not tell it all. I've told enough of that kind of thing in this account, and I'm tired of it, and it doesn't get easier to set down. I'll tell you that I was fifty-two years old with two cracked ribs and one eye, and that it didn't matter as much as it should have, because the reeves at the door had been trained by me and I knew every single thing they were going to do before they did it. I'll tell you that Benno put one of them down with his lamp, and then stood there staring at what he'd done. I'll tell you that one of the four inside got a shot off, and it took Benno high in the left shoulder and spun him round, and he sat down against the bay wall with his hand pressed to it and said *oh* in a small surprised voice. And I'll tell you that when it was done, the six reeves were on the floor and I was standing in the middle of the bay with my knife in my hand and my breath coming in great tearing gulps, and the children were looking at me.

They were in the back half of the bay, behind a cargo net strung floor to ceiling like a fence. Forty-odd. The ones from the Deep and the ones from Number Two, the Fenn girl with her baby, Kesse with her burned ear and a split lip, Odo with his squint. They were pressed against the net, staring.

And Wren, at the front, with her wired hands, looking at me through the mesh the way she'd looked at me through the gap between two reeves on the main dock. No expression. Water gone still all the way down.

I didn't say anything to her. I didn't think I had the right. I went to the net and started cutting.

* * *

The drones moved while I was cutting.

I'd forgotten them. That's the truth. They'd stood so still along the back wall through all of it — the reeves, the shot, Benno going down — that I'd stopped seeing them, the way you stop seeing a stack of crates. And then I heard the click. One click, small, the sound of a jointed leg moving on a metal deck. And then another.

I turned round.

They were coming. All three. Not fast, not slow, in that patient orderly way, across the bay toward the net. Toward the children. Toward me.

"*Debtors will remain in custody,*" said the calm voice of the receiver, from somewhere, from everywhere — from the bay's dead speakers and the reeves' fallen radios and the little hand-band in Benno's belt where he sat bleeding against the wall. "*Resistance to recovery constitutes a further default.*"

"Rook," said Wren behind me, through the net. Very quietly. "Rook, they're going to pod us."

I had my knife. I stood between them and the net with my knife up, and they kept coming, and I knew — I knew the way you know the floor of a dive is about to drop away — that a breather's knife against those things was going to mean exactly nothing at all.

"*The door!*" That was Haddie. "Absalom! The bay door! It's on the panel — the old hand — it closes the inner hatch, it'll seal the back of the bay—"

I looked. She was at the wall by the bay entrance, at an old control panel I hadn't even seen, black glass and grey switches with lettering on them in the square dead hand that only she could read. She had her spectacles pushed up and her face an inch from the panel, and her bent old fingers were moving over the switches, fast.

"There's a hatch," she said. "A pressure hatch. Behind the net. It comes down from the ceiling. If I can make it come down—"

"*Then make it come down!*"

"I'm *trying*, you great fool, it's three hundred years old—"

The first drone reached me. It put out two of its rods — I saw them come, slow, almost gentle — toward my shoulders. I cut at them. The knife skated off them like a blade off ice. And then they touched me, very lightly, on both shoulders, and I felt it: a cold that went through my coat and my skin and into the bone, and in the middle of the black jointed body in front of me something began to open, pale and soft and glistening, like oil.

"*Haddie—*"

Something came down behind me with a sound like the end of the world.

It was the hatch. A great slab of grey metal, a hand's width thick, dropping out of the bay ceiling behind the cargo net on old tracks that screamed, and hitting the deck with a boom that I felt through my boots and my teeth and my broken ribs. The net was on our side of it. The children were on the other — in the back of the bay, behind a pressure door that had been built three hundred years ago to hold back vacuum.

The drone in front of me paused. Its rods were still on my shoulders. The pale glistening thing in its middle had stopped opening.

"*Debtors are not accessible,*" said the receiver's calm voice, after a moment, as if it were noting the weather. "*Re-routing.*" The rods came off my shoulders. The pale thing folded itself away. And all three drones turned, together, the way the tenders had turned in the Door — and went toward the bay entrance.

Toward the panel.

"Haddie," I said.

She'd turned round. She had her back against the panel and her hands spread out on either side of her, flat against the switches, the way you'd stand to stop someone getting at a cupboard. The drones were coming at her across the bay, all three, slow and patient. She looked at them, and then past them, at me.

"The hatch stays down while the switch is held," she said. Her voice was quite calm. "I can't tell how to lock it. I haven't got the word. If I let go it comes up again." She smiled at me over her cracked spectacles. "So I'm not going to let go."

"Haddie, *get out of the way—*"

"There's another way into the back of the bay," she said. "Through the cargo lock, on the outside of the arm. Benno knows it. Go round. Get them out. Get the girl out." She was looking at the drones now. They were very close. "And take the book, Absalom. It's in my coat. The old one. Give it to her. She can read it now. She's quicker than I ever was."

The first drone touched her shoulders.

I'll not tell you about the next part. I've decided. I've told you everything else in this account, every bad thing I ever did and most of the bad things I ever saw, and I'm not going to tell you this one. I'll tell you that she didn't let go of the switch. I'll tell you that the drone that touched her paused, as it had paused with me, as if something in it were doing a sum — and then the receiver's calm voice said, from everywhere, *"Debtor is aged. Debtor is of low recovery value. Debtor is obstructing recovery,"* and the drone did something else instead. Something quicker. And I'll tell you that she still didn't let go of the switch, even then, for longer than I would have believed anybody could.

When the drones had gone — when they'd stood over her for a moment, and then turned, and walked out of the bay into the arm in their patient row, re-routing — I went across the bay and knelt down beside her. Her hands were still flat on the panel. I had to take them off it, one finger at a time, the way I'd taken Garra's off his alarm.

The hatch behind the net didn't come up. It must have locked when it took her weight. I don't know. I couldn't read the panel.

Her spectacles had fallen off. I picked them up and put them in my pocket. And in the inside of her coat, against her chest, wrapped in a piece of oilcloth, I found the oldest of her nine Count-books, the brittle yellow one with the brown ink, open at the page with the word *Gallows* on it, where she'd been reading it when she came up to find Benno.

* * *

Benno took us round to the cargo lock. He could walk, just, with his arm strapped to his chest in his own belt and his face the colour of the pods. It was on the outside of the arm, the lock — we had to go out in suits, along the arm's skin on a line, among the stacked pods in the vacuum — and at the end of it there was an old round hatch with a wheel, which I turned, and inside, in the dark, the back of the bay.

They were all there. Forty-odd, pressed against the grey slab of the pressure hatch in the dark, with Wren in front of them with her wired hands. When the cargo lock cycled and the light came in, the little ones screamed, and then saw who it was, and stopped.

I cut the wire off Wren's hands. She let me. She didn't look at me while I did it, and when it was done she didn't say anything; she just went to the children and started getting them into suits from the bay's old emergency locker — there were a dozen; we'd have to go in relays — and I helped her, and for a long time neither of us said a word.

It was in the cargo lock, in the end, with the last relay of children suited up and gone out along the line to Benno's cutter and only the two of us left in the bay, that I told her.

I didn't plan it. I didn't stand there and work out the words. I'd had seven years to work out the words, and I'd never once managed it, and I didn't manage it then. I just found I was saying it. Standing in the dark of the bay, with the grey hatch behind us and an old woman on the other side of it who'd held it shut with her hands, I told Wren what Kask hadn't.

How Jory's name came up fourth on the roll. How I read it out in the cargo pod in front of the Lash Council in my reeve's voice, steady, and nobody moved. How I went home and sat on the edge of his bunk and watched him sleep for six hours, with the roll in my coat, and did the sum. How the sum came out. How I got up, at the end of the six hours, and put my coat on, and went to Kell, and stood in Kask's study with tears on my face and asked her for one name.

How I went back to Lanthorn with her yes in my pocket and sat down at the table in the cargo pod with the roll spread out in front of me, alone, at the third hour of the morning, and went down the list of every nine-year-old child on Lanthorn, looking for one nobody would miss.

"I was looking for a reason," I said. "I want you to know that. I didn't just pick. I went down the whole roll, three times, and I was looking for a reason. Somebody sick. Somebody whose family had something coming to them. Somebody who'd done something. I wanted it to be *fair*." I heard my own voice, flat and dead in the bay. "There wasn't anybody. There were four hundred and twelve children on that roll and not one of them had done anything at all. So I stopped looking for a reason and started looking for a cost. Who'd cost me least. Whose mother had no man and no friends on the Council and nobody who'd come to my door. And I found your name. And I crossed out his and wrote in yours, in my own hand, and stamped your tag the next morning, and put it on you myself on the gangway."

Wren was standing very still in the dark.

"You looked at me," I said. "The whole way down the gangway. With your chin up. Like you were writing me down." I stopped. "I've thought about that look every night for ten years."

"Good," said Wren.

"And he died anyway. Jory. Two years after. Red fever." I looked at her. "So I'd done it for nothing. That's what Kask said, and she was right. I did the sum and it came out, and I did it, and it was for nothing. And I've done that sum every night since, and every night it still comes out. That's the worst of it. It still comes out. Any father would have done it. Your mother would have done it, for you." I made myself hold her eyes. "That doesn't make it anything but what it was."

She was quiet a long while. The bay was very quiet. Through the hatch behind us I thought I could hear, faint, the receiver's voice in the arm, speaking its notice.

"Do you want me to forgive you?" said Wren at last.

"No."

"Good," she said again. "Because I don't." She looked at me in the dark, steady, nineteen years old, with her burned hands at her sides. "I don't forgive you. I'm not ever going to. Ten years in the vents. My mother dead with nobody to ask. Mattie. Haddie." Her voice didn't shake. "You owe me, Rook."

"Yes."

"You owe me more than you can pay. You owe me more than you've got."

"Yes."

"So pay what you've got," said Wren, "and don't stop."

She turned round and went to the cargo lock, and put her helmet on, and stood there with her hand on the wheel, waiting for me.

* * *

Benno took the children. He had a reeve's cutter at the end of Arm Four, a newer boat than mine with room in her for twenty at a squeeze and forty if nobody breathed much, and he said he'd take them to the Deep.

"The *Deep*?" I said.

"It's empty." He was sitting in the cutter's pilot seat with his strapped arm and his grey face, and he almost smiled. "She's moved everybody to the depot. Every reeve, every runner, every hand. There's nobody at Kell Deep but the rock and the heat. Nobody'll look there for days." He looked at the children packing in behind him, the Fenn girl with her baby, Kesse, Odo, the little ones. "I know the vents. I'll find somewhere warm."

"Benno."

"I know, Rook." He looked at me. "I know. I'll keep them. Go on." He looked past me at Wren, standing at the cutter's hatch. "Go and shut it."

* * *

The *Mendicant* was where I'd left her, in the shadow of the dead tower on the Hulk's dark side, with Teague's breath in her tanks. Wren and I went across to her on the line, and in through the lock, and I sat down in the pilot's seat and she sat down in the co-seat, and I put Haddie's spectacles and Haddie's book on the console between us. Neither of us said anything about them.

I lit the drive.

We came out from behind the Hulk low, the way I'd come in, and I put the old boat's nose toward the Door and pushed the throttles to their stops. Behind us the station fell away against the moon, lit up with Kask's work-lamps, crawling with tenders, its outer arms thick with the grey spawn of eleven thousand pods. Somewhere in there was a holding bay with a grey hatch, and an old woman on the far side of it.

Ahead of us was the Door.

I'd never seen it like this. It had always been a coin — a little round blur of wrong stars, low in the sky, the reason for everything. It wasn't a coin now. It was an eye, and it was opening. The black at its heart had spread until it filled a quarter of the forward glass, and round it the smeared stars were pouring inward in long bright arcs like water down a drain, and at its centre, in the black, something was moving.

"Rook," said Wren.

"I see it."

It was coming through. I couldn't see the shape of it; it was too big, and too black, and my eye kept sliding off it the way it slid off the herald. But I could see the edge. A long curve of darkness, sliding out of the black at the Door's heart into the bent starlight at its rim — slowly, so slowly, like a whale surfacing — and catching, here and there, along that endless curve, the faint far light of Vigil. And beyond the edge, more of it. And more.

"How far?" said Wren.

"Five days at a steady burn. Four if I push her." I looked at the board. "Teague's breath'll do four."

"And when we get there?"

"We find out what's at the Spider," I said, "and we shut it."

She didn't answer. She picked up Haddie's book from the console, carefully, the brittle yellow pages in their oilcloth, and opened it on her knees at the page with the word on it, and looked at it in the light of the board.

"*Gallows,*" she said slowly. Reading it. Not just saying it: reading it, the old square letters, one by one. "*G. A. L. L. O. W. S.*"

"That's it."

"She taught me that one on the second night," said Wren. "At the Hulk. Before we went down to the seats. She said I'd need it." She touched the page with one burned finger. "I didn't know what she meant."

On the aft scope, a chime. A new contact, far behind us, coming off the Hulk.

I looked. It was a big boat — bigger than the reeves' cutters, red and white, with the open hand painted on her flank so large I could see it on the scope at that range. The Margrave's yacht. The *Open Hand*. She'd lit her drive, and turned, and was coming out after us on the same line we were on, straight for the Door. Fast. Faster than the *Mendicant*.

"She knows," said Wren.

"Benno'll have been missed. And the bay." I watched the light grow on the scope. "She knows where we're going. She was going anyway."

"Can she catch us?"

"Before the Door? No. She's faster, but we've got half a day on her." I looked at the forward glass, at the great dark curve still sliding out of the black at the Door's heart, endless, and at the tiny dark speck hanging at the Door's rim — so tiny I'd never have found it if I hadn't known to look — with four long threads hanging down from it. "At the Spider? She'll be there half a day behind us."

"Is that enough?"

"I don't know what's there," I said. "I don't know if it's enough. I don't know if there's anything there at all."

Wren closed Haddie's book and held it against her chest, the way Haddie had.

"Then we'll go and count it," she said.
