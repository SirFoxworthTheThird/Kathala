# Chapter 17: The Long Fall

*The Ledger · 2794*

On the second day out, with the Nave a bright star behind them and Strand's cutter a fainter one behind that, Ysolde took Anselm's sheet out of her coat and unfolded it on the chart table in the *Small Mercy*'s cramped cabin, and began to write the receipt.

She did not call it that at first. She called it the audit. It was what an auditor did when a Ledger was closed: she took the account from its opening to its last line and set it out fair, in the forms, year by year, every debit and every credit, so that anyone who came after could check it and find it true. She had done it for small things a hundred times — a family's line closed by death, a crew's account closed when their boat was lost. She had never done it for this.

Anselm had done most of it already. That was what she saw, now, with the sheet spread out under the cabin lamp and the old boat humming round her. Every line on it was a year: the year's tithe from all nine Chanceries, summed, which he must have carried out of the stacks in his head night after night, the way she had carried the Compact; the Founding Terms charged on what remained; the balance carried. Three hundred and forty-one lines in his small upright pencil, from *Founding* to the present, and the box drawn round Year 280.

What he had not done was prove it. He had not had the First Book. He had built his whole sheet on a single figure he had read once, forty years ago, on a page left out on a dead Prelate's desk, and carried in his memory ever since; and he had been right — she had stood in the Reliquary and seen it printed, *a hundred and eighteen million, four hundred thousand* — but a figure carried in a man's memory was not a proof. Not to an auditor. Not to the Nave.

So she proved it. She wrote it out fresh on reckoning sheets from Corin's stock, in ink, in the Combine's own forms that every Ledger in the Nave still used, starting at the top of the first sheet with the heading she had seen printed on the First Book's first page.

*ACCOUNT HV-VIGIL-0001. STATEMENT OF RECONCILIATION.*

And under it, the Founding, as the Combine had printed it. The Founding Terms, as the Combine had printed them. And then the years, one by one, each line checked against Anselm's pencil and against her own memory of the Chancery summaries she had read in the stacks over twenty-two years, and where she could not check it, marked *per Ferrow*, so that anyone reading it would know whose figure it was.

It took her six days. She worked through the ship's nights and its days, which were the same, at the chart table with her back to the bulkhead and her knees drawn up, while Corin flew. Sometimes he came and stood behind her and read over her shoulder for a while, his lips moving, and went away again without speaking. Once he brought her a bulb of hot water with a little of the gold Nave spirit in it that he said he had been saving for twelve years, and she drank it without noticing and did not know until afterward what it was.

On the sixth day she wrote the line for Year 280, and drew a box round it as Anselm had, and wrote inside the box *Balance: nil. Account closed.*

And then she wrote the sixty-one lines after it, every one, each year's tithe entered as a credit against an account that owed nothing, until the last line, the three hundred and forty-first year, the year she was sitting in. And at the foot of that she wrote the total of all sixty-one: what had been paid into the Count since it was zero. What the Lungs had given, in hours and lives and children sent down at fourteen, to an account that had been closed before her mother was born.

She looked at the figure for a long time.

It was not a number she had a word for. It was larger than the Founding. It was larger, she thought, than anything the Combine could ever have imagined it was owed.

At the foot of the last sheet, under the total, she wrote: *Overpaid.*

And then, because it was what auditors did, and because there was a space for it in the forms, she wrote: *Reconciled by Y. Marr, keeper of the Ninth Ledger, Third Chancery, from the figures of A. Ferrow, keeper of the Ninth before her.* And under that, a line, and beside the line the words she had written at the foot of every closing she had ever made: *Seal of the Prelate-Auditor.*

The line was empty. Of course it was. Without the Prelate's seal a closing was only paper — the Nave's law, the Combine's law, every law there had ever been in the forms. She had known that when she started. She sat looking at the empty line, and the empty space above it where an old man's ring would have pressed its mark into the paper, and laughed, quite quietly, until Corin put his head round the cockpit door to see what was the matter.

* * *

He told her about Tamsin on the seventh night.

They were in the Shear's outer edge by then. She had not known what that meant until they were in it; nobody in the Nave did. It was a region, Corin said, a great slow whirlpool of wrongness round the Door, where space itself had been bent and torn when the Door was shut and had never quite healed — where the stars swam in the forward glass like reflections in a stream, and the *Small Mercy*'s instruments disagreed with each other, and every few hours the whole boat would lurch sideways as if a hand had pushed it. Corin flew it with his face set and his hands light on the yoke and did not sleep. On the seventh night she sat in the second seat beside him because she could not sleep either, and after a long silence, out of nothing, he began to talk.

"She was good," he said. "That's the thing. She was a better diver than me at fourteen than I was at thirty. She could read the red. Some people can — you can't teach it. She'd look at a deck of cloud on the scope and say *there's a seam under that, about nine hundred down*, and she'd be right, and I never knew how she knew." He moved the yoke a fraction; the stars swam. "She went down on the *Hesper's Daughter*. Old Rennet's boat, from Spoke Twenty-one. I wasn't diving any more by then, my lungs had gone. I was on the tally line at the head when they brought the boat back up without her."

"What happened?"

"Sink. Downdraft. She'd taken on a full draw from a seam she'd found and the sink took her and she had to dump." He was quiet a moment. "She didn't dump. Rennet said so after; he was on the tender. He said she came on the link and said, *I'm not dumping it, Rennet, it's three months of our share, they'll take the shop*, and he told her to dump and she didn't. She was fourteen. She'd done the sum." He looked at the glass. "Three months of our share. Against her. And she thought it came out."

Ysolde was silent.

"I went up to the Nave," said Corin. "After. I went up on the drop shuttle with her tag in my fist and I walked into the Ledger Hall and I stood at the rail in front of the Board at the Turning. And I watched it change. The units and the tens and the hundreds, all rushing over." He moved his hand, a little rippling motion. "And I knew that somewhere in there was her hours. Her last dive. The draw she wouldn't dump. Somebody in the Third Chancery had entered it that morning and summed it and sent it up, and it was in there, in the number, making it smaller." He smiled, very faintly. "It fell by about two thousand that morning. I remember. Two thousand marks. That was my daughter."

"Corin—"

"I scratched my first circle that night," he said. "On the wall outside my shop. I didn't even know what it meant, really. I'd seen them. I knew it was the Nullist sign. I just stood there with my knife and cut it as deep as I could, and when the Bailiffs ground it off a week later, I cut it again." He looked at her at last. "Twenty years. I've never once known whether it was true. Whether there was anything to it. Whether the Count was a lie, or just a weight. I just knew I had to cut it." He was quiet. "And then you came into my shop with Anselm's tag and his sheet, and sat down in my back room and told me Year three hundred and two."

"Two hundred and eighty."

"Two hundred and eighty." He nodded slowly. "Tamsin went down in the three hundred and twenty-sixth year." He turned back to the glass. "Forty-six years after it was paid."

They flew for a while without talking. The stars swam.

"When you write it," Corin said at last. "Your audit. Your closing. Put her in it."

"She's in it. All the years are in it."

"Not like that. Not in a sum." He did not look at her. "Put her name. Somewhere. In the margin. *Tamsin Asch, aged fourteen, Year three twenty-six.* So that whoever reads it knows that some of the numbers were people." His hands were very still on the yoke. "That's all. I'd like her written down somewhere that isn't the Count."

Ysolde got up, and went back to the chart table, and found the line for the three hundred and twenty-sixth year, and dipped her pen.

* * *

On the eighth day they saw it.

Corin saw it first. He had the boat's old long-glass rigged to the forward scope and he had been sweeping the dark ahead of them for hours, through the swimming stars, looking for what Grandad had called the Hanged Men; and in the middle of the ship's afternoon he made a small sound in his throat and sat back from the eyepiece and said, "There."

She looked.

The Door first. She had seen it all her life — a faint smudge low in Tarrow's sky, a place where the stars were a little wrong, that the novices were taught to bow to at the Turning. From here, at the end of the Shear, it was not faint. It filled the middle of the scope: a great round lens of bent light, stars smeared round its rim in long arcs like the grain in polished wood, and at its heart a place where there was nothing at all. Not black. *Nothing.* The eye would not stay on it. It slid away, the way it slid off a thing in the corner of a room that was not there when you turned.

And at its edge, small against that enormous wrongness — so small she would never have found it if Corin had not put the crosshair on it — something hung.

It was a station. She could see that much. A long thin spine, dark against the bent light, lying along the Door's rim like a bar laid across the mouth of a well. And from the spine, at four points, on long threads so fine she could only see them where they caught the light, hung four great dark cylinders. They did not move. They hung there round the rim of the Door at equal distances, quite still, their tethers taut, like weights on a set of scales.

Like four bodies on a gallows.

"Gallows," she said.

"The Hanged Men." Corin's voice was strange. "Grandad's Hanged Men. He never saw them. He said he never had a good glass." He was quiet a moment. "Old man would've liked to see this."

She looked at it. There were no lights on it. There was nothing to show that anyone had been there in three hundred and forty years. Frost on the spine, maybe, where the faint far light of Vigil caught it — a pale rime along its upper edge. A darker patch near its centre that might have been a docking ring.

"What was done there?" she said. "What could anyone have done there?"

"We'll know in a day," said Corin. And then, without changing his tone at all, looking not at the forward scope but at the aft: "If we get there."

* * *

Strand's cutter had been closing all through the Shear. It was faster than the *Small Mercy* — anything in the system was faster than the *Small Mercy*, Corin said, except possibly the Count Board — and in open space it would have caught them in three days. In the Shear it had been slower, because the Bailiffs did not know the currents and Corin did. But Strand had good pilots, and the Shear's outer edge was behind them now, and the inner reaches round the Door were calmer, and the cutter's light on the aft scope had been growing all through the eighth day like a slow sunrise.

At the ninth bell of the ship's evening it hailed them.

"*Small Mercy.*" Strand's voice, pleasant and patient, crackling through the Shear. "*This is the Prelate's cutter* Recompense*. Heave to and prepare to be boarded. Sister Marr, Mr. Asch. You've had a long trip. Let's not make it longer.*"

Corin did not answer. He was watching the scope.

"*Mr. Asch,*" said Strand. "*I know you can hear me. You've no weapons on that boat. I've four. I've been asked not to use them, but I've also been asked not to let you reach that station, and I've been told which request comes first.*"

Still Corin did not answer.

There was a pause on the band. Then a different voice, older, slower, very tired, that made Ysolde's hands close on the arms of her seat.

"*Ysolde.*"

It was the Prelate.

"*Ysolde, it's Aurel Quist. I'm on the cutter. I came myself.*" A pause; she could hear him breathing. "*I asked you not to. In the chapel. Do you remember? I asked you to leave it shut. You gave me your pages and I thought — I hoped — that meant you'd heard me.*" Another pause. "*Come back. Please. It's not too late. Come back with me and we'll go home, and I'll strike the default, and no one will ever know you came out here. I'll say you were lost in the Shear. I'll say anything.*"

She reached for the band switch. Corin's hand closed over hers.

"Don't," he said quietly. "He's keeping you talking. He's got you on a bearing."

"*I don't know what's on that station,*" said the Prelate's voice. "*I've told you that. No one knows. But I know the Stewards closed it, and I know they were wise, and I know that the one thing they ever forbade — the only thing in three hundred and forty years that they ever wrote down as* forbidden — *is the place you're going. Think, Ysolde. You're a counter. What sort of thing would twelve people who'd lie to thirty thousand without flinching be too frightened to say?*"

She sat with Corin's hand over hers on the band switch and did not move.

"*Come back,*" said Aurel Quist. "*Leave it shut.*"

The band clicked off.

Corin let go of her hand. He looked at the aft scope, and then the forward, where Gallows hung tiny against the Door's bent light. And then — she saw it happen; she saw him do the sum — he looked down at the third screen on the console, the one he had been glancing at all through the Shear, a little dark display with a scatter of faint red points on it that she had never asked about.

"Ysolde," he said. "Can you fly the pup?"

* * *

The pup was the *Small Mercy*'s jolly-boat: a squat little cargo skiff that lived clamped in a cradle under the old boat's belly, with room in her for a pilot and six full-draw bottles and not much else. Corin used her for running the last kilometres into the far relays, where the *Mercy* was too big to dock. He had shown Ysolde how she worked on the fourth day, because there was nothing else to do and because, he said, nobody should be on a boat they couldn't get off. Yoke, throttle, a docking arm, a simple scope. She had flown her once round the *Mercy*'s hull, at a crawl, and been sick afterward.

"Why?" she said.

He tapped the dark screen. The scatter of red points.

"Pickets," he said. "Mines. Old ones. Combine. They're all over the inner reaches round the Door — have been forever. Most of them are dead. Some of them aren't." He put his finger on one, a little apart from the others, glowing a little brighter. "That one's not. It's been tracking us for an hour. If we go near it, it'll wake up and do what it was made to do." He looked at the aft scope. "The *Recompense* doesn't know it's there. Strand's never been out here. Nobody has."

"Corin—"

"I'm going to take the *Mercy* past it," said Corin Asch. "Close. Slow. Like I'm trying to slip through the picket line. And Strand's going to follow me, because he'll think I've found a way through, and because he's been told not to let me reach that station. And when he's close enough—" He opened his hand, slowly, fingers spreading. "It'll wake up."

"And you?"

"I'll be closer."

She stared at him.

"It's the sum, Ysolde," he said. "Do it. You're the counter." He was very calm. "The *Mercy* can't outrun that cutter. Not in clear space. Not for the last day. He'll be on us by morning, and then he'll board us, and then the old man will take you home, and nobody will ever know what's on that station. And everything in your coat goes into the Prelate's stove." He looked at her. "Or you take the pup, and the breath, and you go in on your own while he's chasing me. He won't see you. The pup's too small and too cold. And then you're at Gallows, and he's not."

"I can't fly the pup."

"You flew her round the hull."

"I was *sick*."

"So be sick at Gallows." He was already unbuckling. "Come on. The bottles are loaded, I did it this morning. I've been thinking about this since we entered the Shear." He stood, stooping under the cockpit's low roof, and held out his hand. "Come on, Sister. Your brother told you not to do anything stupid. I'm telling you to do this."

She did not move.

"Tamsin did the sum," she said. "You told me. Three months of your share, against her. And she thought it came out."

"Yes."

"And it was wrong. You said. It was wrong because she'd done it with the Count's numbers, and the Count's numbers were lies." She was shaking. "How do you know yours aren't?"

Corin Asch looked down at her for a long moment in the dim cockpit, with the Door's bent light coming through the glass behind him and laying its long smeared arcs across his ruined face.

"Because they're mine," he said. "Not the Count's. Not the Nave's. Mine. I've counted them myself." He held out his hand again. "Come on."

* * *

The pup's cradle was under the cargo belly, through a hatch in the floor of the hold, among the racked bottles. He helped her down into the little cockpit and strapped her in himself, with his scarred hands, tugging every buckle twice. Six full-draw bottles were lashed in the space behind the seat — breath, he said, enough to start an engine or wake a dead machine or do whatever she thought she was going to do — and her satchel was wedged beside them, with her audit in it, and Anselm's sheet, and her copies of the Compact.

He checked the board. He set the pup's simple scope to Gallows. He showed her, again, the throttle and the yoke and the docking arm, slowly, as if she were a novice.

"Cold drift for the first hour," he said. "No drive, no heat, nothing. Let the *Mercy* carry you clear and then just fall. He'll be watching me. After an hour, light her up gentle and steer for the spine. There's a docking ring near the middle; you'll see it. The arm'll find the collar on its own if you get it close." He looked at her. "Don't hurry. You've got all the time in the world, once I'm done."

"Corin."

"What?"

She did not know. She had thought there would be something to say and there was not. She looked up at him out of the pup's cockpit, at his long hollowed face and the scar down his throat and the red line over his eye where the Bailiffs had cut him in his own shop, and found that she had put her hand up and laid it flat against his chest, over his heart, the way she had once laid it against the glass of the novice dormitory's window on her first night in the Nave, to feel the cold.

He covered it with his own.

"Put her in the margin," he said. "That's all."

"I did."

"I know. I watched you." He took her hand off his chest and put it on the pup's yoke, and closed her fingers round it. "Count what's owed, Sister."

"And not a mark more," she said.

He smiled — the first time, she realised, that she had ever seen him smile; it made him look like someone else entirely, someone young — and closed the pup's canopy over her, and she heard him go up through the hatch, and heard the hatch seal, and then nothing.

* * *

The *Mercy* dropped her at the ninth bell of the ship's night. A clank, a lurch, and the pup fell out of her cradle into the dark, tumbling slowly end over end, and through the canopy Ysolde watched the old boat's scarred belly slide away above her and turn and go.

She did not light the drive. She did as he had said. She lay in the pup's cold cockpit with her hands off the controls and her father's tag in her fist, and let the dark take her, and watched.

The *Mercy* went out toward the picket line slowly, her drive lit low, like a boat feeling her way through shallow water. On the pup's little scope Ysolde could see her — a bright point — and behind her, coming on, the brighter point of the *Recompense*. And off to the side of both, faint and red and alone, the mine.

She watched the two lights draw together. She watched the *Mercy* turn, gently, toward the red point, as if she had seen something there — a gap, a way through. She watched the *Recompense* turn after her.

On the band, very faint, Strand's voice: "*Mr. Asch. Heave to. Mr. Asch—*"

And Corin's voice, for the first time, answering. She would remember it for the rest of her life. Not loud. Not angry. Just a little dry, a little tired, the voice of a man behind his counter with a valve in pieces in front of him, telling an auditor the shop was shut.

"*Count it, Captain,*" said Corin Asch.

The red point on the scope went white.

She saw the light of it through the canopy — not on the scope; with her own eyes, far off, a soundless flower of white fire opening in the dark among the swimming stars, very bright, and then less bright, and then fading, the way a lamp fades when you turn it down. In its light, for an instant, she saw the *Recompense*: tumbling, its drive out, a long gash torn down one flank, venting a pale plume of air into the dark. Crippled. Not dead.

And of the *Small Mercy* there was nothing at all.

* * *

She lay in the pup in the dark for the hour he had told her to, and then for another, because she could not make her hands move. Then she made them move.

She lit the drive gently, the way he had said. She steered for the spine. She was sick once, as she had known she would be, and cleaned it up with the sleeve of Bett's coat, and steered again. The Door grew in the canopy until it filled it, that great lens of bent light with nothing at its heart; and against it, growing too, the long dark spine of Gallows, and the four great cylinders hanging from it on their threads.

They were enormous. She had not understood how enormous. Each of the Hanged Men was the size of a Chancery, of a refinery head, of the Ledger Hall itself — dark smooth cylinders as tall as towers, hanging motionless on tethers as thick as the Count Board's chains, round the rim of a hole in the world. The spine they hung from was longer than the Nave. It was frosted along its whole length, a pale rime thick as snow, and it was dark. Not a light. Not a sign.

She found the docking ring near the middle. She got the pup close, and closer, and then the arm found the collar on its own, as Corin had said it would, with a clank that went through the little boat like a struck bell. She sat in the cockpit with her heart going and listened to the seals take.

They held.

She put on the pup's spare suit, which was Corin's, and too big. She put her satchel over her shoulder. She looked at the six breath bottles lashed behind her seat, and decided they could wait. She opened the pup's hatch, and the docking collar beyond it, and stood in front of the station's outer door: a round plate of grey metal, rimed with three hundred and forty years of frost, with a wheel at its centre and a line of the old square lettering across its top.

*H-V PASSAGE CONTROL. GALLOWS.*

She put her gloved hands on the wheel. It would not turn. She put her weight on it, and then her whole body, braced against the collar's frame, and felt something give deep inside the door with a long groan that she heard through her boots.

The wheel turned.

The door swung inward. Beyond it was a darkness so complete that her lamp, when she lifted it, seemed to make no impression on it at all — only a little pool of light on a frosted floor, and frost on the walls, and air so cold it came through the suit, and silence.

She stepped through.
