# Asset Seven

## The order

[
  Place: #The Cells
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Mattias Ebb
  Items: ^Isaura's gauge:@Isaura Venn
  When: 20 July 2654
]

The order comes down on the twentieth of July, on a slip of foil with the Council's seal, and my section chief brings it to me herself, which she has never done before.

Paske stands in the Cells corridor with the slip in her hand and doesn't give it to me at once. She's a tall stooped shared woman of sixty with a long grey face, and she's been running the South wardens for nineteen years, and I've never seen her look at a piece of foil as if it might bite her.

"It's for the eleventh," she says.

"The lieutenant."

"The Council's interrogators have had him four times. He hasn't given them anything they can use. Fleet dispositions. Picket rotations. Where the *Steadfast* sits. They think he knows." She holds out the slip. "They want his allowance set to the floor. Today. And then below it. A step a day, until he does."

I take the slip and read it. It says what she says, in the Council's language, which is gentler. *Allowance to be adjusted in accordance with interrogation requirements.* There are numbers. The last number is ninety litres an hour.

"Ninety's not a floor," I say.

"No."

"Nobody can live on ninety."

"Not for long," Paske agrees. "That's rather the point." She looks at me with her long grey face. "You don't have to do it, Venn. I can send Ostrander down. He'd do it."

I know Ostrander. He'd do it. He'd set the valve and log it and go and get his dinner.

"I'll do it," I say.

The eleventh cell's valve is set into the wall beside the grille, a small brass wheel under a frosted window, like every valve on the Ninth. Mattias is sitting on his bunk when I come. He sees the slip in my hand and the key on my belt, and he doesn't say anything. He watches me fit the key.

I turn it to the floor. Two hundred and twenty. The needle goes down and settles on the red, and the cell's little fan changes its note, very slightly, the way they all do. I log it.

Then I take the key out and stand there with it in my hand, and don't turn it any further.

"That's not ninety," says Mattias.

"No."

"They'll check."

"They'll check tomorrow." I put the key back on my belt. "Today it's the floor. Nobody dies on the floor."

He laughs, a short surprised breath. "Is that true?"

"It's in the Ordinance."

"Then it must be true." He leans back against the wall and closes his eyes. Already his breathing has changed, a little slower, a little deeper; he's conserving, the way they taught him. "What do they want?"

"Fleet dispositions. Pickets. The *Steadfast*."

"I don't know where the *Steadfast* sits. Nobody tells lieutenants where the flagship sits. I've told them that four times." He opens his eyes. "They don't believe me."

"They believe you'll say anything at ninety."

"They're right," says Mattias. "I would." He looks at me through the grille for a long moment. "I'll tell you something instead. Something they haven't asked. Because they don't know to ask it."

"Why?"

"Because you set it to the floor," he says simply, "and not to ninety. And because I've been lying in here three weeks thinking about it, and I'd like somebody in this city to know before they find it." He takes a breath. "Come closer. Sound carries."

I come close to the grille.

"I told you about the briefing," he says, low. "The commander, at the end. *If you find it, don't open it.* What I didn't tell you was that they gave us a card." He holds his hands up, a little apart. "A recognition card. Every lander crew. Laminated, the size of your palm, to keep in your thigh pocket with the survival kit, in case you came down somewhere. On one side there's a picture. On the other there's a number and four lines." He closes his eyes, as if reading them off the inside of his lids. "*Asset Seven. Halloran-Vey Combine. Sanitation Agent, Pattern H. Do not open. Do not approach. Report and withdraw.*"

"Sanitation."

"That's the Combine's word. The Concord uses it too. It means—" He stops. His breath is coming a little harder now; he isn't used to the floor yet. "It's a weapon, warden. The old companies used them on workers who wouldn't work. You put it in the air and everybody in the habitat goes to sleep and doesn't wake up, and the machines keep going. That's what sanitation means. You clean the people out and keep the place."

The corridor is very quiet. At the far end, the tannery thief is snoring.

"The picture on the card," says Mattias, "was a grey egg. In a cryostat. With those four lines stencilled on it, and the Combine's wheel." He opens his eyes. "My captain on the *Steadfast*'s lander deck had been on the staff before. He talked, when he drank. He said the Combine lost it here, at Corva, the year it lost Corva. He said the Combine's register says *seized by mutineers*. Not sold. Not bought. *Seized.*" He looks at me. "Your Purchase, warden. Your three hundred who bought their air. The Combine's books say they took it. And they took that with it."

I hold on to the bars of the grille.

"Why now?" I say. My voice sounds very far away. "Why come for it now? After two hundred and sixty years?"

"Because the lane opened properly again, last decade, after being half-shut a long time. Because the Concord bought the Combine's books and somebody read them. Because somebody worked out that whatever the mutineers did with it, the city they made is still here, and might still have it." He's breathing slowly, carefully. "That's what the siege is, warden. It's not the claim. It's not your accounts. It's that. They want it back. And the Commodore has orders. My captain told me that too, when he was very drunk." He swallows. "If the occupation has Asset Seven, recover it. If the occupation will not yield, *sanitise the claim*."

"Sanitise—"

"With it. On you. If they can get it." He holds my eyes through the grille. "They don't want this city with you in it, warden. They want it empty. With the docks and the refineries and the dipper fleet all still working. They'll ship in their own people from Corran in a year." He leans his head back against the wall again, tired. "That's the something else. That's what I didn't tell the interrogators. I'd like it if you didn't either. I'd like to live long enough to see what you do with it."

I stand at the grille for a long time after he's closed his eyes again. Then I go and do the rest of the round, every cell, every meter, every seal, reading each one aloud into the ledger in a perfectly steady voice, and at the end of the corridor the tannery thief wakes and asks me if the Lamp's still in the sky.

"Yes," I say.

"Good," he says.

## The Lamp's own orbit

[
  Place: #The Archive
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Malachy Orr
  Items: ^The Share Plate:#The Archive
  When: same day
]

Malachy has run the figures.

He's been running them for three days, it turns out, on an old reckoning engine in the back of the Archive that nobody else in the city remembers how to use, a big cabinet of brass and glass with a keyboard like a church organ's and a paper tape that comes out of its side in a long curling ribbon. The ribbon's all over the floor when I get there. Malachy's sitting in the middle of it on his stool, with a length of it in his lap, and when I come in he doesn't get up; he just holds the tape out to me, and his hand is shaking.

"It's the Lamp," he says.

I take the tape. It's columns of figures, meaningless to me.

"The elements on the Plate," he says. "Run forward, with the giant's pull, with the perturbations, the way the old dippers did it. They come out at this. At now. At the comet that's hanging over the Oculus." He takes the magnifier off his head and rubs his eyes. "Somebody in the founding year scratched the Lamp's orbit into the back of the Share Plate with an awl, and then painted it over, so that nobody would see it until it mattered. Until the Lamp came round."

I sit down on the floor in the paper ribbon.

"Malachy," I say. "I've got something to tell you."

I tell him. All of it: the slip, the valve, the floor, the grille, the card. *Asset Seven. Sanitation Agent, Pattern H. Seized by mutineers.* I tell him what sanitation means. I tell him what Grieve's orders say. He sits on his stool and listens with his eyes shut, and doesn't interrupt once, which in thirty years I've never known him do.

When I've finished he doesn't say anything for a long while.

"Mutineers," he says at last.

"That's what the register says."

"Not the Purchase. Not *we bought our air*." He opens his eyes. They're wet. "Do you know, I've half-known that for forty years? There's a gap in the Archive. Around the founding. There are the dip logs, right up to the November of ninety-one, and the Camp's ration books, and then there's a month with nothing at all, and then the Charter of the Shares, all very tidy, in a beautiful hand, describing the Purchase. And nothing in between. Not a receipt. Not a letter. Not a single record of any sale." He laughs, a dry crackle. "I used to tell myself the records had been lost. Archivists always tell themselves that."

"They took it," I say. "They took the ship. And they took that."

"And they hid it." Malachy looks at the Plate on its table under the lamps, the long cleaned strip of scratched figures, the grey paint over the rest. "They didn't keep it in the city. If they'd kept it in the city it would be in the Archive or in the Spine or under the floor of some shared family's chapel, and the Concord wouldn't need thirty-eight ships, they'd need one spy. They didn't use it. If they'd used it, we'd know; you don't use a thing like that and keep it out of the histories." He gets up off his stool, slowly, with a hand on the table, and stands over the Plate. "They wrote the Lamp's orbit on the back of the founding Plate and painted it over, Isaura. Why would you do that? Why would you make sure that whoever was standing here when the Lamp came round again could find out where the Lamp would be?"

I know what he's going to say. I can see him getting there, the way I used to watch him get to the end of a proof on the board when I was nine, the chalk squeaking.

"Unless," he says, "there was something on it."

We look at each other across the table.

"It's a guess," I say.

"It's a guess." He nods. "It's only a guess." He puts out one spotted old hand and lays it flat on the grey paint of the Plate's back, beside the cleaned strip, the first time I've ever seen him touch it with his bare skin. "The rest of this is words. Under here. I've got letters coming through in the middle now, a few. Not enough to read." His hand presses down on the steel. "I'd give everything I have to know what they say."

"Lucan will want to know," I say.

"Lucan will want the egg," says Malachy.

The words fall into the Archive like a dropped tray.

"He mustn't have it," says Malachy, quite calmly. "Whatever it is, if it's there, if it's anywhere. He mustn't. Do you understand me? Not Lucan, not the Council, not the Shares." He looks at me over the Plate. "You said reserve's at what, today?"

"Thirty-one."

"Thirty-one days. And the Concord's slugs every hour. And the Council in the old bridge with its *measures*." He shakes his head slowly. "A man like Lucan, with thirty-one days, and a thing that could make thirty-eight ships go quiet? He'd never think twice. He'd call it the Founders' Gift and he'd thank them for it in the nave."

"Then we don't tell him."

"I'm an archivist," says Malachy, and smiles, the thin bleak smile I've known since I was six. "My stewards report to his stewards. He's had a man in my outer office since the day the Plate came down. He knows I've had the reckoning engine running for three days." He looks at the door. "He'll know it's the Lamp before the end of the bell. And he'll send for us."

He's wrong. It's before the end of the half-bell.

## Measures

[
  Place: #The Spine
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Malachy Orr @@Lucan Strand @@Corin Strand
  When: same day
]

The Council meets in the old bridge now, deep in the Spine, below the nave and above the Archive, where the old skimmer's officers once stood over their consoles watching the giant. The consoles are still there, dead and dusty, with brass rails round them; the shared families sit on benches between them, the heads of the forty oldest Shares, in their good coats, under work-lamps strung from the ceiling on cables. The bridge's forward window, which once looked out at Gall, looks out now at sixty metres of ice, black and shining.

They don't invite us to sit. Malachy and I stand at the back by the door, between two stewards, and Lucan Strand stands at the front by the old helm, with his son beside him, and listens to Malachy tell the Council what the numbers on the back of the Plate are.

Malachy tells them the truth, as far as it goes. He tells them the numbers are an orbit and the orbit is the Lamp's, written in the founding year, and painted over. He doesn't say anything about Asset Seven. He doesn't say anything about mutineers. He doesn't look at me. When he's finished, the forty heads of the Shares sit on their benches in the lamp-light very quietly, and then one of them, an old woman in a fur collar near the front, says "*The Lamp comes round*," in a wondering voice, and somebody else hushes her.

Lucan lets the silence go on a little.

"I'll tell you what I think it means," he says.

He doesn't raise his voice. He never does. He stands with one hand on the old helm, the rail polished bright by two centuries of hands, and talks to them the way he talks to the nave on feast days, warm and certain and sad.

"I think the founders knew," he says. "I think they knew that a day would come when this city would be in danger. They'd thrown out a company. They knew companies. They knew a company that has lost something never stops wanting it back. And I think that in the year of the Purchase they made provision for that day, as careful people do, and put it where no company could ever reach it, and wrote down for us where to find it, on the one thing in this city they knew would never be thrown away." He lifts his hand from the helm. "I think the founders left us a gift. And I think it's on the Lamp."

The bridge murmurs.

"What kind of gift, Lucan?" says a man on the left, heavy, red-faced, a refinery Share.

"I don't know," says Lucan. "A weapon, I'd think. Something the Company had, that the founders took from them. Something that would let a city of a hundred and eighty thousand stand against a company with ships." He smiles very slightly. "Something they were wise enough not to keep in the house."

"A weapon," the red-faced man says, "that's been sitting on a comet for two hundred and sixty years."

"In the cold. Where nothing changes." Lucan turns to his son. "Corin?"

Corin Strand steps forward. He's in his flight jacket, as always, and he looks the way he looks in the posters: tall, fair, all cheekbones, with a pilot's squint lines round his eyes even though he's twenty-eight. He doesn't look like a man who has ever been afraid of anything, and I think he probably hasn't.

"The Lamp makes its closest pass to Gall on the ninth of August," he says. He has a clear, quick, slightly impatient voice. "Twenty days. It's coming in fast and it'll go out fast; after the eleventh or twelfth, nothing we've got could catch it. But for about four days either side of the ninth it'll be close enough to reach with a dipper skiff, if the skiff goes in deep round Gall and uses the giant to throw it. The old way." He shrugs. "It's the run my great-grandfather used to talk about. Nobody's done it in a century. I've done it in the simulator forty times."

"And the Concord's pickets?"

"The Lamp's coma is thousands of kilometres across and full of dust. Their sensors will be half-blind in it. If we go out under the tail, with the Lamp between us and the fleet, we'll be a fleck in a snowstorm." He grins. "Mostly."

"You'd need to know where their pickets sit," says the woman in the fur collar.

"We have a Concord lieutenant in the Cells," says Lucan smoothly. "A lander pilot. He'll know."

I keep my face very still.

"And somebody who can read what the founders wrote," Lucan goes on. "Brother Malachy has done remarkable work, but the back of the Plate is still mostly under paint, and the Archivist tells me it will take a week or more of work to clear it. We don't have a week. The skiff must go by the end of the month. So the Plate goes with the skiff, and somebody goes with the Plate, to finish the work in flight and read us the founders' directions when they're needed." He looks across the bridge, over the heads of the forty Shares, at the door. "Brother Malachy is seventy-six and could not survive the sling. His pupil is thirty-one, a warden with surface rating, and has been at the Plate since the day it fell."

Every head in the bridge turns to look at me.

"You'll go, warden," says Lucan Strand. It isn't a question. "With my son, and a rigger of his choosing, and the lieutenant to show you the pickets. You'll find what the founders left us and bring it home."

I don't say anything. I stand there between the stewards and look at him, and he looks back at me with his silver tired face; and then, while I'm still looking for my voice, he turns back to the benches and goes on, in exactly the same voice, as if he were moving to the next item on a list.

"There is the other matter," he says. "Reserve stood at thirty-one days this morning. The Concord took out the fourth intake overnight. At the present rate of loss, reserve will fall below twenty within the week." The bridge has gone very still. "You all know what that means. We've discussed it in this room before. The Council adopted the provision in principle ten days ago. I'm asking you now to adopt it in fact, to take effect when the reserve reaches twenty."

The red-faced refinery man looks at his hands. The woman in the fur collar looks at the black window. Nobody says anything.

A steward hands a slate down the front bench. It goes along the row, from hand to hand. I can see it from the back, over their shoulders: a list. A short list, a dozen lines. The Ninth Tier, North and South. The Eighth Tier, upper levels. Parts of the Seventh. Numbers beside each one. Thirty-eight thousand. Forty.

"Relocation to the surface shelters," says Lucan. "The old Rime domes. They're sound. They're not warm, but they're sound, and the Council will supply them with what air can be spared. It isn't what any of us would wish. It is a temporary measure, until the dipper fleet can fly again. It is the only way the city lives." He looks round the bridge. "The founders understood that. I think it's what they would have done. The few for the many. Cut the line to save the ship."

"*Cut the line to save the ship,*" says somebody on the back bench, quietly, the way you'd repeat a proverb, and a few others murmur it after him.

They vote. They vote by raising their hands. Thirty-four hands go up.

I watch them go up, one by one, the good wool sleeves of the forty oldest Shares, and I think of the Ninth South, and the eleventh door with its crooked painted number, and a grandmother with her hands folded on a blanket, and a girl in cracked grey gloves wiping the frost off a meter. I think of my mother at her bench under her lamp, picking soot out of a filter with a pick.

"First Shareholder," I say.

It's very loud. The whole bridge turns again. Malachy, beside me, closes his eyes.

Lucan raises his silver eyebrows. "Warden."

"My mother lives on the Ninth South," I say. "Ama Venn. Filter-mender. She's sixty-three and she's had the ice cough for twenty years." My voice is perfectly level. I have been an air-warden for twelve years. I know how to say a number in a room. "If I'm to go on your skiff, First Shareholder, I want her moved. Down. Today. To a shared tier, on shared air, where she won't be relocated anywhere."

The bridge is utterly silent.

Lucan looks at me for what feels like a long time. Then his face softens, very slightly, into the gentle sorrowful look he gave the Plate when it lay face down on the deck of the nave.

"Of course," he says. "Of course, warden. That's only right." He nods to a steward. "See to it. Ama Venn, Ninth South. To the Third Tier, on Strand air." He turns back to me. "Your mother will be looked after. You have my word."

## Coming round

[
  Place: #The Spine
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn
  When: same day
]

That night I go up to the nave.

It's empty. It's been empty since the siege began. The benches stand in their rows with nobody on them, and the old drum lies on its side at the far end with its skin of names going brown under the resin, and the Plate's mount on the drum's end is bare, four sheared pins, a pale rectangle on the scarred metal where the Plate hung for two hundred and sixty years. Ice dust from the slugs lies on everything. My boots leave marks in it.

I stand under the Oculus and look up.

The Lamp fills it. It fills it completely: a blaze of white at the centre so bright I can't look at it straight, and round it, and away from it, the tail, the four tails now, splayed out across the whole great lens of fused glass like the ribs of a fan, green and gold and white, so that the nave is lit by it, faintly, as if by a moon. My shadow lies on the dusty floor at my feet. It's the first time in my life I've ever had a shadow from the sky.

Twenty days, Corin says, to its closest pass. Then out. Then two hundred and sixty years in the dark. The founders looked up at it, from the deck of the old skimmer, in the year they took their air. They looked up at it and they made a decision, and wrote it down with an awl, and painted it over.

*Seized by mutineers.*

*Sanitise the claim.*

*Cut the line to save the ship.*

My mother is on the Third Tier tonight, on Strand air. A steward told me so at the end of the bell, at my door, very courteously. I believe him. I'm almost sure I believe him.

I don't know what's on the Lamp. Nobody does. It might be nothing. It might be an old dipper's bones and a joke scratched on a rock. It might be a gift. It might be a grey egg in a cryostat with four lines stencilled on it and the Combine's wheel. I stand in the comet's light in the empty nave and I find, to my surprise, that I'm not frightened of it, whatever it is. I'm frightened of the thirty-four hands. I'm frightened of the list on the slate.

"Whatever you are," I tell the Lamp, out loud, in the empty nave, "I'm coming."

It doesn't answer. It burns on, coming round, as it has come round for a million years; and somewhere under it, out past the Concord's pickets and Corin's simulator runs and the old dippers' sling, somewhere on that long bright falling body of ice, is the thing my city was built on.
