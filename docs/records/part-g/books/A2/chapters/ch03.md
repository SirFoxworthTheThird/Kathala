# Collateral

## The counting-house

[
  Place: #Szabo's counting-house
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Ilona Szabo @@Juno Brask
  Items: ^Juno's bond:@Ilona Szabo
  When: 3 April 2917
]

Mother Szabo keeps her ledgers in stone.

Til Brask has always found this funny, in the way she finds most things on Cinderhaven funny, which is to say not at all but with her mouth. The counting-house sits on the Ledger Ring, a long curved gallery of debt-houses with their shutters down and their lamps lit, and every one of them has a vault behind it cut into the ring's ballast, and in every vault, on shelves of fused grey rock, the paper. Real paper. Bound. The Directorate's courts burn down every few years, and the Directorate's data-houses get wiped every time the Directorate changes hands, which is more often than the courts burn; and so the debt-houses of Cinderhaven went back, a century ago, to writing their debts on something that does not care who is in charge. Paper keeps. Stone keeps. Debt keeps best of all.

Szabo's vault is the deepest on the Ring. Her counting-room is in front of it, a long low room lined with clerks' desks, warm and dim and smelling of ink and old tea, with Mother Szabo herself at the far end of it behind a desk the size of a bunk, writing. She doesn't look up when Til comes in. She never looks up when anybody comes in. She lets them stand.

Til stands.

She stands with her hands in the pockets of her deck coat and her weight on her good leg and looks at the clerks, because it's better than looking at Szabo. There are eleven of them, bonded every one, mostly young, mostly women, in the grey half-sleeves of the house, each bent under a lamp over a ledger. The fourth from the end is her niece. Juno has her hair cropped short now, like a rigger's. She's taller than she was at Mid-year. She has a pen in her hand and an ink-stain on her jaw and she's adding a column of figures with her lips moving very slightly, the way her mother used to, and she has not looked up either.

"Captain Brask," says Mother Szabo, finally, still writing.

"Mother."

"You've had my notice."

"I've had it."

"Then you know where we are." Szabo puts down her pen and lifts her head. She's a small, soft, round woman of sixty or so with a face like a kindly baker and eyes like a pair of rivets. "Eleven thousand two hundred marks, principal and interest, against the hull and engines of the salvage tug *Dray*, registered Dock Nine. Six months in arrears. Three notices served. The hull is mine on Thursday, Captain. My men will be at Dock Nine on Thursday morning with the yard-master and the seals, and they will put the seals on your hatches, and you will step off, and that will be that."

"I'll have it by Thursday."

"You will not have it by Thursday." Szabo says it kindly. "You have three hundred marks in your account at the Chandlers' and a job lined up in the Tarn yards that pays six hundred if it pays at all. You have a crew of one, who is sixty-three years old and owes me nine hundred himself. You have a tug that is fifty years past her rating and a licence that expires in May. I know what you have, Captain Brask. It's my business to know." She folds her soft little hands on the ledger. "I am not an unkind woman. I've carried you longer than I would carry anybody else. I've carried you because I liked your sister, and because I have your sister's girl, and because I thought, for her sake, you might make something of yourself. You haven't."

Down the room, Juno's pen has stopped.

Til looks at her own boots. They are old boots. Everything she owns is old. "What do you want, Mother?"

"What makes you think I want anything?"

"Because you asked me here. You could've sent the yard-master on Thursday and saved yourself the conversation. You never have a conversation you don't make money on."

Szabo smiles. It's a real smile, warm and pleased, the smile of a teacher whose slowest pupil has finally said something right.

"I have a client," she says. "Who wants a tug with a deep-salvage rating. Armour, reach, a long winch, a crew that can do a hard landing on an irregular body, and a captain who will go where she is sent and not ask the client his business." She tilts her head. "And who is desperate enough to go through the Corva lane."

There's a silence in the counting-room. One of the clerks drops a pen.

"No," says Til.

"I haven't told you what it pays."

"It doesn't matter what it pays. The Corva lane's raw. It's been raw since it opened. Three ships have gone through it in two years and one's come back, and that one came back without its crew."

"It came back without most of its crew."

"I'm not going through the Corva lane."

"It pays," says Mother Szabo, as if Til had not spoken, "thirty thousand marks. Ten in hand when you sign, twenty on delivery. Of which I am to have forty in the hundred, for the introduction and the risk." She lets that sit. "Which leaves you eighteen thousand. Which clears the *Dray*. Which leaves you, by my arithmetic, six thousand eight hundred."

Til doesn't say anything.

"And," says Szabo, "the balance of a certain bond held by this house, against the debts of the late Margit Brask, salvager, deceased, and carried to her daughter. Which stands at"—she does not look it up—"seventeen thousand nine hundred and forty marks."

At the fourth desk from the end, Juno Brask lifts her head.

She doesn't look at Til. She looks at Szabo. Her face is very still. She has her mother's face, broad across the cheekbones, with her mother's straight black brows, and when it's still like that Til can't look at it for long.

"Six thousand eight hundred doesn't clear seventeen nine," Til says.

"No. It doesn't. But it would bring it within reach, wouldn't it, Captain? Two more years of honest hauling. Three, perhaps. And there might be"—Szabo spreads her hands—"bonuses. My client is generous. He's also old, and ill, and in a hurry. A captain who did him good service might find him very generous indeed."

"Who is he?"

"That," says Szabo, "is between you and him. His agent will be at the Roundsmen's chapel on Dock Nine at the second bell. You can tell him no to his face. I would rather you didn't." She picks up her pen again. "That's all, Captain Brask."

Til doesn't move.

"Mother."

"Hm?"

"Why me?"

Szabo writes a line. Then she looks up again with her rivet eyes, and her voice is still kind, and Til would rather she had been anything else.

"Because nobody else I own is stupid enough," she says. "And because I've heard the stories about you. On the Harrow. They say when it came to it, you cut the line." She looks down the room. "A client likes to know his captain can do what's needed."

Til walks out of the counting-house without saying anything else. As she passes the fourth desk she slows, without meaning to, and Juno's eyes come up and meet hers for the first time in eleven months, black and level and without any expression in them at all; and then Juno looks down again and goes back to her column of figures, and Til goes on out into the noise of the Ring.

## The Roundsmen's chapel

[
  Place: #Dock Nine
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Cato Marchetti @@Ozias Mbeki
  When: same day
]

The Roundsmen keep a chapel at the end of every dock on Cinderhaven, because Roundsmen are dock people, and dock people die.

The one on Dock Nine is a converted lock, a round steel room with a domed ceiling and the old outer hatch welded shut and painted over with stars. There are benches. There's a little brazier in the middle, kept going day and night by whoever's nearest, with a dish of salt beside it. Round the walls, in chalk, the Roundsmen write the names of the dead and the dates they went out, and under each name a little loop, a circle not quite closed, which is the sign for *on its way*. Some of the loops are fifty years old. Some are from last week. Til knows eleven of the names on these walls personally, and one of them very well, and she does not look at that one.

Oz is sitting on the front bench with his hands in the salt.

"I heard," he says, without turning round.

"Everybody on the Ring heard. Szabo's clerks have mouths."

"Corva." Oz rubs the salt between his fingers and lets it fall into the brazier, where it spits blue. He is a big man gone soft and grey, with a face like a bag of stones and the most delicate hands Til has ever seen, an engineer's hands, an instrument-maker's; he's been her engineer for nine years and her friend for thirty, and he has followed her into places no sane man would go and out of them again, and he has never once said *I told you so*, which is the only reason she still speaks to him. "The Lamp's going round. They're saying it's the last time. They're saying Gall's going to eat it."

"Who's saying?"

"Everybody who reads the Observatory sheets. Which is me and four old Roundsmen and a boy who sweeps the observatory. It's coming in too close this time. Inside the giant's reach. It'll come apart." He lets the last of the salt fall. "The Lamp takes the dead out and brings them back. That's the saying. Nobody ever said what happens when it doesn't come back."

"I don't need a sermon, Oz."

"You're getting one anyway. I'm sixty-three. I've earned it." He wipes his hands on his knees and turns round on the bench. "Are you going to do it?"

"I'm going to hear the man out."

"That's yes."

"That's hear the man out."

"That's yes," says Oz, comfortably, and turns back to his brazier.

The agent arrives on the second bell exactly. He comes in from the dock side, ducking under the lintel, and stops just inside the door to let his eyes adjust; and Til, who has spent her whole life around men who are dangerous, files him immediately and without effort in the drawer where she keeps the worst of them. He's not big. He's not anything much. Forty, perhaps, slender, in a good grey coat with the collar turned up, close-cropped hair going silver at the sides, and a long pale scar running down from his left ear into his collar like a seam in a glove. He has a quiet, courteous, attentive face. He stands very still. That's the thing. Most people fidget when they come into a chapel, out of reverence or embarrassment. He stands like a man who has been taught that the first person to move is the first person to be seen.

"Captain Brask," he says. "My name is Cato Marchetti. I represent Mr Hollis Vey. Thank you for coming."

"I haven't said yes."

"No. Of course." He comes and sits on the bench across the brazier from her, and nods to Oz, who nods back. "Mother Szabo will have told you the terms."

"She told me the money. She didn't tell me the job."

"The job is a retrieval." Marchetti puts his hands on his knees. "You know of the comet the Corva people call the Lamp."

"Everybody on Dock Nine knows about the Lamp. Half the drunks on this dock have a map to the Lamp Hoard. I've bought two."

"Then you know the legend." He doesn't smile. "The Lamp is a periodic comet in a resonant orbit with Corva's gas giant. It comes in every two hundred and sixty-odd years, passes close by the giant, and goes back out. Last year the Tarn Observatory sent a probe through the Corva lane, which had just reopened, and the probe came back with its pictures, which is more than the crews of the two ships that followed it did." He reaches into his coat and brings out a slim grey slate and lays it on the bench between them. On the screen is a smear of light: a bright head, a long dim tail. "The Lamp is inbound now. It reaches the giant on the second of May. This time it will pass inside the giant's limit. It will be torn apart, and what's left of it will fall into Gall's atmosphere, and it will be gone."

"So I hear."

"Mr Vey believes there is something on the Lamp," says Marchetti, "that belongs to his family. He would like it retrieved before the second of May."

"What is it?"

"I don't know exactly."

"You don't know."

"Mr Vey's family were—are—an old one. They had interests in Corva a very long time ago. They lost something there, in the Corva system, in a way that has never been fully explained, and Mr Vey has spent a large part of his life and most of his fortune trying to understand what. He believes it went out on the Lamp. He believes this is the last chance anyone will ever have to bring it back. He is"—Marchetti chooses the word—"devoted to the question."

"He's mad."

"He's dying," says Marchetti, without any change in his voice. "It amounts to much the same thing, in my experience. He has perhaps three months. He would like to be there."

Til looks at him. "He wants to come?"

"He insists on it. He'll travel in his own medical cradle, with his own supplies; he won't be a burden on your ship. I will accompany him." Marchetti turns the slate off and puts it away. "You'll take us through the lane, Captain. You'll take us to the Lamp, using whatever approach you judge best. You'll land, and assist Mr Vey—assist me—in locating and recovering whatever is there. And you'll bring us home. Thirty thousand marks. Ten in hand."

"And if there's nothing there?"

"Then you'll have been paid ten thousand marks to fly a dying man to look at a comet. Many people would consider that a fair day's work."

Oz makes a sound into the brazier that might be a laugh.

"Szabo wants her girl aboard," Til says. "That's the deal, she said. One of her people, to witness."

"So I'm told."

"Which one?"

Marchetti considers her for a moment with his quiet, attentive face.

"I believe," he says, "she means to send your niece."

Til had known that. She had known it from the moment Szabo said *your sister's girl*, the way you know you've stepped on a loose plate on a hull a half-second before it goes. It doesn't help. She sits with her hands on her knees and looks at the brazier and the salt and the loops on the walls, the circles not quite closed, and one name in particular that she is not looking at, three rows up on the left.

"Mother Szabo," says Marchetti, "has a sense of humour."

"Mother Szabo," says Til, "has a sense of collateral."

She signs at the fourth bell. The ten thousand marks are in the Chandlers' by the fifth, and by the sixth eleven thousand two hundred of them are gone again, out through the Chandlers' wall and into the stone vault on the Ledger Ring, and the yard-master's seals are cancelled. That's what money is on Cinderhaven. You never touch it. It goes through you like weather.

## Dock Nine

[
  Place: #Dock Nine
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Ozias Mbeki @@Juno Brask @@Hollis Vey @@Cato Marchetti
  When: +2 days
]

The *Dray* is not beautiful.

Til will be the first to say it. She's a deep-salvage tug of the old Tarn pattern, built sixty years ago for hauling dead hulks off the Harrow and the other wreck-fields of the outer Reach, and she looks it: a squat, blunt, armoured wedge of a hull, scarred and patched and painted over so many times that nobody knows any more what colour she started, with a stubby drive cluster at the back, a long grapple-and-winch boom slung under her belly like a folded insect's leg, and a forward cabin so small that two people can only pass in it if one of them is asleep. She has a fusion core that runs hot and an air plant that runs cold and a winch that will haul a hundred kilometres of cable and has hauled most of it. She is slow. She is ugly. She cannot be killed. Til has tried.

Oz spends two days going over her from the drive cones to the forward lamps, while Til spends two days spending other people's money. They buy cable. They buy reaction mass. They buy air, eleven tanks of it, more air than the *Dray* has carried at one time in her life; they buy ice-anchors and screw-pitons and a thermal lance and two new pressure suits, one of them small, which Til buys without saying anything and Oz watches her buy without saying anything either. They buy food. Cato Marchetti comes down to the dock twice to look things over and goes away again, courteous, attentive, saying almost nothing; on the second visit he brings four long crates of his own, sealed, and asks for them to go in the cargo bay, and Til opens one with Oz standing by, because it's her ship, and finds medical supplies, and closes it again.

"He didn't mind," says Oz, afterward. "You opening it."

"No."

"He wanted you to."

"Yes," says Til.

They're in the cargo bay, on the night before departure, stowing the last of the cable. The bay's lit by work-lamps clipped to the ribs and smells of old grease and cold metal and the dust that gets into everything on Cinderhaven, fine grey dust from the ring's ore-mills that you can taste for a week after you leave. Oz is coiling cable on to a drum with his delicate hands, talking to himself in the old Roundsman cant his mother taught him, half prayer and half swearing.

"My mother's people came out of Corva," he says, unexpectedly.

Til looks up from the anchors. "You never said."

"You never asked. It was a long way back. Two hundred years and more. Before the lane went dark." He finishes the coil, ties it off. "Dippers. They used to go down into the giant's air on ropes, she said, forty kilometres of rope, and scoop the weather up in a bucket. And when they died they gave them to the giant. Lowered them down on the rope and let them go. And they had the comet, and they had a saying about it. The Lamp takes the dead out and brings them back." He picks up the next coil. "That's where we got it. The Roundsmen. All of it. The salt and the loops and the going-out. It's dipper stuff. It's from Corva."

"Then the Hoard's dipper stuff too."

"The Hoard's drunk stuff," says Oz. "There's no Hoard. Dippers didn't have anything to hoard. They had debts." He tugs the cable. "But it's funny. That we're going there. Back round."

"It's not funny. It's thirty thousand marks."

"That too."

The hatch at the forward end of the bay grinds open and lets in a slab of yellow dock light, and a figure in it with a bag over its shoulder.

Juno has a grey house coat on over a rigger's undersuit, and a cheap pair of dock boots, and the bag, which is small and old and has belonged to someone else before her. She stops in the hatch. She looks at Oz, and at the cable, and at the work-lamps, and at the stained ribs of the hull, and finally at Til.

"Mother Szabo sent me," she says.

"I know."

"I'm to witness the recovery. And log the cargo. And report back to the house." She says it like something learned off a slate. "I'm not crew. I'm not under your orders. Mother Szabo says if you put me to work I'm to log it and she'll bill you."

"That sounds like her."

"Where do I sleep?"

"There's a bunk aft of the galley," Til says. "It's the warm one. There's a new suit in the locker by it that ought to fit you. You'll need to try it before we go through the lane. Oz will check your seals."

Juno looks at her for a long moment. Til waits for it, whatever it's going to be, and is ready for it, she thinks; she has had eleven years to get ready for it.

"Don't talk to me," says Juno Brask, quite politely. "Unless it's about the job."

She goes past them down the bay toward the galley hatch, and Oz moves out of her way with his coil of cable, and she doesn't look at either of them; and then the galley hatch closes behind her and it's just the two of them in the work-light with the cable and the cold.

Oz goes back to his coiling. After a while he says, without looking up, "She's got Mags's walk."

"I know."

"Tall, though. Taller than Mags. That's from the father, whoever he was."

"I know, Oz."

"She'll come round."

"She won't," says Til. "And she shouldn't."

The client comes aboard at the first bell of the morning, an hour before the departure slot. Til has been expecting a sick old man in a chair. What comes up the ramp out of the dock's grey half-light is a coffin.

It's a medical cradle, she knows that; she's seen them before, on the Directorate's hospital ships, though never one this fine. It's a long, low, gleaming shell of white composite on a powered sled, with a clear canopy over the upper half and racks of quiet instruments along its sides, humming. Two dock porters walk it up the ramp, and Cato Marchetti walks behind it with a hand resting lightly on its rim, the way a man walks beside a horse. Under the canopy, propped on pillows, is Hollis Vey.

He is very thin. That's the first thing. He's a long man, and the illness has eaten everything from him but the length, so that he lies in his white shell like a heron in a nest, all bones and joints. His skin has the papery yellow-grey of the very sick. His hair is white and wispy and was once, you can see, thick and fair. He's seventy, perhaps, or a hard sixty. His eyes are pale blue, and wet, and completely alert, and they move over the *Dray*'s cargo bay, its ribs and its lamps and its drums of cable, with an expression that Til, after a moment, recognises as delight.

"Captain Brask," he says. His voice comes through a speaker grille in the cradle's rim, thin and dry and slightly delayed. "Forgive me for not getting up."

"Mr Vey."

"This is a very ugly ship."

"Yes."

"Good." He smiles. His teeth are perfect and slightly too large for his face. "Ugly ships last. My family built beautiful ones. You'll notice we haven't got any left." He turns his head on the pillow, with some effort, to look at her properly. "Mother Szabo tells me you're the best deep-salvage captain on the Ring."

"Mother Szabo tells people what makes her money."

"Then we understand each other. She also tells me you've no wish to know my business, which I very much appreciate, and which I don't for a moment believe." The pale eyes are amused. "We have a long way to go together, Captain. I'll tell you my business, when I've decided how much of it I can bear to say aloud. In the meantime, I would be grateful for a place to plug this thing in, and a window, if your ship runs to windows. I'd like to see it, when we get there."

"The Lamp?"

"Corva," says Hollis Vey. "All of it. My family hasn't seen it in five hundred years."

They undock at the second bell. Til takes the *Dray* out herself, by hand, the way she always does, backing her off the dock on her manoeuvring jets with the yard-master swearing at her on the traffic channel for doing it by hand, and swings her round in the long shadow of the ring, and lights her drive. Cinderhaven falls away behind them, a great grey wheel turning slowly in the light of Tarn, with its docks and its chapels and its stone vaults full of paper. Ahead, two days off, is the Throat of the Corva lane.

Oz comes up into the cockpit as the ring shrinks. He leans on the back of her chair and looks at the rear display for a while without saying anything.

"Something just came off the ring behind us," he says. "Dock Two. Big hull. Running quiet."

Til looks. She knows the hull. Everybody on Cinderhaven knows the hull: a long black repossession cutter with a ram bow and four turrets, the house mark of a ledger and a pen painted small on her flank, the *Carrion Hymn*.

"Well," says Til. "Mother's coming too."
