# Lampfast

## The Ocampo valve

[
  Place: #The Ninth Tier
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Pilar Ocampo
  Items: ^Isaura's gauge:@Isaura Venn
  When: 12 June 2654
]

The first air I cut that year belongs to a family named Ocampo, and I cut it on the morning the Lamp comes back.

Their door is the eleventh on Ninth Tier South, a hatch in a wall of grey ice-brick with the number painted on it by somebody who had good intentions and no brush. The meter is set into the frame at chest height, under a little frosted window, and I don't need to wipe the window to read it, because I have read it every month for three years and I know what it is going to say. I wipe it anyway. You wipe the window. You read the numbers aloud into the ledger. You give people the courtesy of being measured.

"Ocampo, Ninth South eleven," I say, and the ledger on my wrist takes it down. "Household of five. Draw this cycle, four hundred and twelve litres an hour at the mean. Tithe owing, nineteen days. Tithe paid, none." I look at the door. "Prior notice served, twice."

Nobody answers. Somebody is behind the door; you can hear a household breathing, if you know how, a low composite sound under the hum of the warren fans, like a sleeping animal. I knock with the end of my valve key.

"Air-warden."

The fans hum. Up here, on the Ninth, they never stop, and they never quite manage; the Ninth is the highest tier of the city, cut so close under Kettle's surface that the ceiling sweats frost and the walls ache with cold, and the air that reaches it has been breathed by eight tiers of people on its way up. It tastes of them. You can taste the Third Tier's cooking in it, the Fifth's tannery, the Seventh's old men. Nobody shared lives on the Ninth. Nobody shared has ever been up here, except to look.

"Air-warden," I say again. "Isaura Venn. I'm required to put your feed to the floor. I'll do it from the frame. You don't have to open."

The hatch opens.

It's the girl. I knew it would be the girl. They always send the youngest, as if I were a dog that could be talked down. She is nine years old, Pilar Ocampo, small for nine the way the Ninth's children are all small, in a jumper with the sleeves rolled four times and a pair of cracked grey gloves she has had on all winter. She has a face like a coin, round and flat and very still, and she looks at me with it.

"Mama's at the plant," she says. "Papa's at the plant. Abuela's asleep. Tomás and Juli are asleep."

"I know."

"If you put it to the floor they won't wake up."

"They'll wake up," I say. "The floor is three hundred an hour for five. It's enough to sleep on. It's enough to live on. It isn't enough to work, or cook, or run about. It's the floor. Nobody dies on the floor."

This is true. I have to say it because it is true, and because it is in the Ordinance, and because if I don't say it then I am just a woman with a key standing in a corridor turning off a child's grandmother. Nobody dies on the floor. People die under it, and nobody is ever put under it, by me or by anybody, not by law; the floor is the floor. If you are on the floor and you get the ice cough, or your heart is weak, or you are very old or very new, then you may die, and that is not the floor's doing. That is the cough, or the heart. I read the Ordinance when I was nineteen and swore my oath as a warden, and I have read it every year since, and I am very clear on the point.

"Nobody dies on the floor," says Pilar, as if she is trying it in her mouth.

"Step back from the frame, please."

She doesn't step back. She stands in the hatch with her cracked gloves at her sides and watches me fit the key. The valve housing is cold enough to burn through my own gloves. It turns hard, the way they all turn hard up here, the brass furred with frost, and I have to put my shoulder into it, and the needle on the gauge goes down, and down, and settles on the red line, and the sound of the fans behind the hatch changes, very slightly, from a hum to a sigh.

"Ocampo, Ninth South eleven," I say to the ledger. "Feed set to floor at eight-oh-four, twelfth of June, fifty-four." I turn to the girl. "When your mother pays, it goes straight back. The ledger tells the plant. She doesn't have to find me."

"It's Lampfast," Pilar says.

"I know."

"Everybody's going to the Spine to see the Lamp. Mama said it's the first time in two hundred and sixty-three years. She said nobody alive's seen it. She said it's lucky."

"It's very lucky," I say. "You should go and see it."

"We can't go now. Abuela can't walk on the floor."

I don't have anything to say to that. I have the key in my hand and the ledger on my wrist and a list of forty-one doors to go before the end of shift, and I can feel the cold of the Ninth going down into my boots. I put the key back on my belt.

"Close your hatch," I tell her. "Keep the heat in."

She closes it. I stand for a moment looking at the number painted on it, the brave crooked eleven, and then I go on to the twelfth.

I am good at this. I want to be clear about that from the start, because I will say other things later that might make it seem I wasn't. I was a good air-warden. I was the best in my section three years running, which is to say I collected the most tithe and filed the fewest complaints and was never once reported for leniency, which is the only thing wardens are ever reported for. I knew every meter on the Ninth South by its sound. I knew which households would pay on the first notice and which on the second and which would never pay until the needle touched red, and which had a cousin on the Seventh who could be leaned on, and which had nobody at all. I did not cut a single feed that wasn't owed. I did not leave a single door without saying the words. I believed that what I did was hard and necessary and that it kept the city breathing, and that if I didn't do it somebody else would, worse.

All of that is true. I still believe some of it.

## Lampfast in the Spine

[
  Place: #The Spine
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Ama Venn @@Malachy Orr @@Lucan Strand @@Corin Strand
  When: same day
]

The Spine is the oldest thing in Patience and the only thing in it that was not built here.

It stands upright in the ice at the city's heart, two hundred metres of rust-red hull rising out of the lower warrens and up through every tier like the trunk of a tree up through the floors of a house. The founders sank it here, in the year of the Purchase, when they brought the old skimmer down out of Gall's sky and set it in Kettle's shell, and the city grew around it the way a pearl grows round grit. You can touch it on every tier. On the Ninth it comes through the floor of the South Market as a great curved wall with frost-flowers on it, and children lick the frost for luck. Inside it are the Council's chambers and the Archive and the old docks, and at the top, under the surface, the nave.

The nave was the drum hall of the ship, once. The drum is still there: a vast scarred cylinder lying on its side at the far end of the hall, as long as a street, with its ancient bearings seized solid and its skin covered from end to end in writing. Names. The founders' names, and the names of the dead before the founding, chalked by hand and then sealed under a skin of clear resin so that nothing would ever rub them off, layer on layer of capitals going brown with age. Nobody can read most of them any more; they are too old, too overwritten, too far up. Children are told they are the names of the Three Hundred. I have always liked one particular patch near the bottom, where somebody long ago drew, beside a name I could never make out, a small round shape with a tail. A seed with a tail. I didn't know what it was when I was little. I know now.

Above the drum, in the roof of the nave, is the Oculus. It's the only window in the city that looks at the sky. The founders cut it through forty metres of surface ice and lined it with a single great lens of fused glass, and for most of the year it shows you nothing but black and, if you're lucky, a slice of Gall going over like a striped wall. Today it shows the Lamp.

I come in late, after shift, with my key still on my belt, and the nave is fuller than I have ever seen it. The shared families are on the floor, where the benches are, in their good coats; the unshared are packed into the side galleries and the stairwells and up on the old maintenance gantries, hanging on to the rails, standing on each other. The air is thick and warm with them. Everybody is looking up.

It is not what I expected. I suppose I expected a star. It's a smear: a smudge of pale light in the black, very slightly green, no bigger than a thumbnail at arm's length, with a long thin fan of fainter light stretching away from it toward the bottom of the lens, like breath going out of a mouth on a cold day. It doesn't twinkle. It doesn't move. It hangs there, very quiet, looking down at us through forty metres of ice, and the whole of the nave has gone still to look back at it.

Two hundred and sixty-three years. The last time it was in that sky, the founders were alive. They looked up at it from the deck of the old skimmer, in the year they bought their air. It was here when the city began, the catechism says. *The Lamp was in the sky in the year of the Purchase.* Every shared child learns that line, and most unshared ones, and none of us has ever seen the Lamp, until now.

I find my mother on the east gallery, at the rail, near the top. She has come down from the Ninth with her neighbours in the morning to get a place, and stood all day for it, and she is grey with tiredness and with the cough, and she is looking up at the Oculus with her mouth a little open like a child's.

"You came," she says, when I put my arm through hers.

"I said I would."

"You've been on the Ninth." She can smell it on me; she can always smell it on me. She doesn't say anything else about it. My mother is a filter-mender, which is the kind of job the unshared do on the Ninth, sitting all day at a bench with a lamp and a pick, picking the sooted mesh of the warren fans clean enough to be used again. She's done it for forty years. She has the cough the filter-menders get, which is a dry deep bark that never quite goes, and she has my father's absence, and she has me, and she has her religion, which is the Lamp.

"Look at it, Isa," she says. "Look. It's come round."

"I'm looking."

"Your grandmother's grandmother told her about it. And her grandmother told her. All the way back." Her eyes are wet. "All the way back to the beginning. And now it's here. And we're here."

My mother is a Lamper. Not a loud one. She doesn't go to the meetings in the tannery yards on the Fifth or stand on the stairs with a placard; she doesn't tattoo comet-tails on her wrists, like the riggers do. She just believes, quietly, in her chair by her lamp, the thing the Lampers believe, which is that the city's comfort was bought with something, and that the something went out on the Lamp, and that it will come back. *The Lamp comes round,* she used to say to me when I was small and couldn't sleep, *the debt comes due.* It was a lullaby to her. It took me until I was fifteen to understand that it was meant to be a threat.

Down on the floor of the nave, the First Shareholder has climbed on to the old tether gallery to speak. Lucan Strand is a tall man with silver hair and a voice like a good instrument, the kind that doesn't need to be raised. He has his son with him. Corin Strand is the best pilot in the dipper fleet, everybody knows it; he flies the deep runs into Gall's cloud-tops that bring up the city's fuel, and he has the face for it, all cheekbones and confidence, and he stands at his father's shoulder in his flight jacket like the illustration in a schoolbook.

Below them, on its mount on the drum's end, is the Share Plate.

It's smaller than you'd think, the first time you see it. A rough rectangle of grey steel about the size of a door panel, cut from the old hull of the skimmer by somebody who did not have the right tools for it, its edges blackened and wavy. Three hundred names are cut into it, in fifteen columns, by three hundred different hands. Some are deep and neat; some are barely scratches. Some are just marks. The first line, at the top of the first column, is blank: not empty but scraped, a gouged and roughened patch of metal where something was once written and somebody took it off again. The Blank Share. Every child knows what it is. It is the Company's share, the one share the Halloran-Vey Combine kept in the ship when the Three Hundred bought her, and which was struck off the day the Purchase was complete. It's the most famous scratch in the world.

"Two hundred and sixty-three years ago," says Lucan Strand, in his good voice, "that light was in this sky."

He doesn't need to raise it. The nave is silent.

"Two hundred and sixty-three years ago, three hundred men and women stood where I stand now, on the deck of this ship, and looked up at it, and made a decision. They decided that they would not be owned. They had nothing. They had a ship that was not theirs, and air that was not theirs, and lives that were written on somebody else's ledger. And they bought them back. Not with blood. Not with theft. With labour, and with bargaining, and with the long patience that gave this ship its name and this city its name. They bought their air." He lifts a hand toward the Plate. "And here are their names."

"*We bought our air,*" the floor says back, the shared families, in their good coats; and some of the galleries say it too, a little behind, the way you sing a hymn you don't quite know.

My mother doesn't say it. She is looking at the Lamp.

"Today," says Strand, "the Lamp has come round again. It has come round to a city of a hundred and eighty thousand souls, where there was once a camp of two thousand. It has come round to deep docks and a dipper fleet and the finest refineries in eleven lanes. It has come round to a free people. If the founders could see us—"

Somebody near the top of the west gallery shouts something. I don't catch it. Heads turn. A second voice takes it up, and a third, ragged, from the stairwells on the north side where the riggers have crowded in: *The Lamp comes round, the debt comes due.*

Strand lets it go by. He is good at that. He waits with his hand still raised, mild, patient, until the wardens on the north stairs have moved in and the voices have stopped, and then he lowers it and smiles.

"There are always those," he says, "who would rather the past were a debt than a gift. I don't blame them. It's a hard city to be poor in." A small murmur of approval from the benches. "But it is the only free one there is. Happy Lampfast."

"Happy Lampfast," says the nave.

Afterward, when the crowd is breaking up and the stairwells are jammed solid, I take my mother down the east gallery the long way, past the Archive doors, because it's quieter. Brother Malachy is in his doorway. He's always in his doorway on feast days, watching the crowds go by like an old crane watching a river. He taught me my letters on the Fourth Tier when I was six and my numbers when I was eight, and argued with me about everything from nine to fifteen, and then got me my warden's place on his own recommendation, which I've never decided whether to forgive. He's seventy-six. He looks a hundred, and has done for twenty years.

"Isaura," he says. "Mistress Venn. You saw it?"

"We saw it," says my mother.

"Remarkable thing." He turns his head and looks up the length of the nave at the Oculus, where the smudge of green light hangs. "Do you know, they kept records of it? The founders. There's an orbit for it in the Archive, in somebody's hand, on the back of a dip log. They knew it would come round. They worked out to the week when." He smiles his thin smile. "They knew more than we give them credit for. They were dippers. Dippers know about things that go out and come back."

"The Lamp takes the dead out," says my mother, softly, "and brings them back."

Malachy looks at her for a moment. "So they say, on the Ninth," he says. "So they say."

## The ultimatum

[
  Place: #The Ninth Tier
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Ama Venn @@Anselm Grieve:phone @@Lucan Strand:phone
  When: same day
]

The Concord comes through the Throat during the evening bells.

I'm on the Fourth by then, taking my mother home the long way round by the lifts, because she can't do the stairs any more. We're in the lift queue at Fourth Central when every speaker in the city clicks at once. That's what I remember: the click. Every speaker on every tier, all together, a single dry sound like a knuckle on a table, and then the hiss of an open channel, and then a voice.

"This is a message to the people of Kettle from the Concord of Vey."

The queue goes quiet. Everyone looks at the nearest speaker, which is a grey grille high on the wall above the lift doors, as if they might see the man through it.

He has a dry, educated, slightly tired voice. It sounds as though it is reading.

"My name is Anselm Grieve. I am a commodore of the Concord's Lane Service. At eighteen hundred today, Kettle time, a fleet under my command entered the Corva system through the lane mouth your people call the Throat. It consists of thirty-eight vessels. Their positions are being transmitted to your traffic control as I speak, as a courtesy, so that there may be no misunderstanding about our strength or our intentions."

My mother's arm tightens on mine.

"The Concord of Vey is the lawful successor to the Halloran-Vey Combine. It holds, by acquisition from the Combine's receivers, all of the Combine's titles, contracts, claims and accounts, including the claim to the Corva system and everything in it. The Combine never relinquished that claim. No instrument of sale exists. The occupation of the moon Kettle, and of the installations on it, and of the Combine vessel *Patience*, is therefore unlawful, and has been unlawful for two hundred and sixty-three years."

Somebody in the queue laughs, a short shocked bark of a laugh. Nobody joins in.

"The Concord has no wish to harm the people of Kettle. You are, in law, the Concord's tenants. Your labour and your debts, like your city, are its property; it has a lawful interest in your welfare. Accordingly I am instructed to offer the following terms. The governing council of the occupation will stand down. The Concord's officers will be admitted to the city and its installations. The accounts of all residents will be assessed and regularised. In return, there will be no reprisals, and no resident will be removed from Kettle without due process." A pause. A rustle, very faint, like paper. "You have three days to consider these terms. On the fourth day, if they are not accepted, I am instructed to take such steps as are necessary to secure the Concord's property. I would ask you to believe that I take no pleasure in saying so. This message will repeat."

It repeats. We stand in the queue and listen to it again, word for word, the same dry tired voice, the same small rustle of paper in the same place. Halfway through the second time the lift comes, and nobody gets in.

"Thirty-eight ships," someone says.

"They can't. They can't just—"

"Tenants." An old man near the front, a dye-worker from the Fifth by his hands, spits on the floor. "Tenants. We'll see about tenants."

My mother says nothing. She is looking at the speaker grille with the same face she turned up to the Oculus: wet-eyed, open-mouthed, almost glad.

"The Lamp comes round," she says, very quietly, to nobody, "the debt comes due."

"Mama. Don't."

"I'm only saying."

"Don't say it here."

The First Shareholder answers the Concord forty minutes later, on the same speakers. I'm in my mother's room on the Ninth by then, sitting on the end of her cot with my boots still on, and we listen to it together by the light of her work-lamp, with her filters laid out on the bench under it in their neat sooty rows.

Lucan Strand doesn't sound tired at all. He sounds the way he sounded in the nave: warm, unhurried, certain.

He says that the people of Patience have heard the Concord's message. He says that the city of Patience is a free city, established by lawful purchase in the year twenty-three ninety-one, and that it does not recognise the claim of the Concord of Vey or of any other body to its people, its labour, or its air. He says that he has spoken with the Council of Shareholders, and that the Council is unanimous. He says that the city has weathered worse than a fleet, that it was built by people who knew how to be patient, and that it will be patient now. He says that the dipper fleet is recalled and the surface locks are sealed and the city's defences, which have not been tested in a hundred years but which have been maintained, every one of them, are manned. He says that the people of Patience are not tenants. He says it the way you would say a thing about the weather.

"In the meantime," he says, "the Council has adopted certain emergency measures, which will be posted on every tier tonight. I ask every citizen of Patience to observe them, for the common good. Our air is our life. We must husband it. Happy Lampfast, and good night."

The measures are posted at midnight. I read them on my wrist, sitting on my mother's cot, while she sleeps.

There are eleven of them. Most are what you'd expect: curfews, rationing of heat, a ban on unlicensed movement between tiers. The ninth says that the dipper fleet is suspended until further notice, which means that nobody will be going up into Gall's sky for fuel while there are thirty-eight Concord ships between us and it. The tenth says that the refinery reserves are to be husbanded accordingly.

The eleventh says that, for the duration of the emergency, the air-tithe payable by unshared households is doubled, and that the floor is lowered by a third, and that air-wardens are to apply the new rates from the first of the next cycle.

I read it three times. Then I turn off my wrist, and sit in the dark with the fans sighing, and listen to my mother breathe.

The first slug hits the surface just before dawn.

I don't know that's what it is. Nobody does. I'm asleep sitting up, and I wake because the whole city rings: a deep enormous note that comes up through the floor and the walls and the cot and my own bones, like being inside a struck bell. It goes on a long time. Ice dust sifts down from the ceiling and settles on my mother's filters. Somewhere above us, forty metres up, something has hit the shell of Kettle at a speed I can't imagine, and the whole moon has taken it and passed it down to us, tier by tier, all the way to the bottom of the sea.

My mother sits up in her cot with her hair full of frost.

"Isa," she says. "Is it the Lamp?"

"No, Mama," I tell her. "It's the Concord."

The bell is still ringing.
