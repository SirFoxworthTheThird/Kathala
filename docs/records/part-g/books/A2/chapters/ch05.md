# The Blank Share

## Under the floor

[
  Place: #The Ninth Tier
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Pilar Ocampo
  When: 30 June 2654
]

On the eighteenth day of the siege the Ocampos' grandmother dies on the floor.

I'm the one who finds her. That's the way it falls out; I'm doing my rounds on the Ninth South with the new rates loaded in my ledger, and when I get to the eleventh door I knock and say *air-warden* and nobody answers, and I knock again, and then I put my ear to the hatch the way the old wardens taught us, to listen for the household breathing, and I hear only the fans. So I open it with my key. We're allowed to. The Ordinance says a warden may enter any unshared household to read its meter, at any hour, without notice, and I have never once done it before.

The room is dark and very cold. The Ninth's rooms are all one room: a sleeping platform at the back, a bench and a heater and a basin at the front, a curtain between. The heater's off, because heat costs air. The curtain's open. On the sleeping platform, under three blankets and a coat, lies an old woman with a grey face and her mouth a little open, and beside her, sitting on the edge of the platform with her knees drawn up and her cracked grey gloves on, is Pilar.

"Abuela's asleep," she says.

I go and look. I'm not a medic, but you learn what you need to learn on the Ninth. The old woman's been dead some hours. Her hands are folded on the blanket, which means somebody folded them.

"Where's your mother, Pilar?"

"At the plant. They're doing double shifts. Because of the Concord." She looks at me. "Papa's at the plant too. Tomás and Juli are at the crèche on the Seventh. Abuela was sleeping. I was minding her."

"How long has she been sleeping?"

Pilar thinks about it seriously. "Since before the morning bell," she says. "She said she was tired. She said her chest was tight. She said it was like breathing through a sock." She looks back at her grandmother. "Then she stopped saying things."

I stand in the cold dark room with my ledger on my wrist. The meter by the door, under its frosted window, reads two hundred litres an hour for five people. That is the new floor. Two hundred for five. It came in on the first of the cycle, along with the doubled tithe, by Council order, and I loaded the new figures myself and turned the valves myself, every door on my section that was in arrears, which on the Ninth South was very nearly every door. I put the Ocampos to the new floor on the second. I remember the valve. It was stiff.

Nobody dies on the floor.

"She had the ice cough," I say. "Your grandmother. She'd had it for years. I heard it every time I came. Didn't she?"

"She coughed," Pilar agrees.

"It's the cough. When people get old, the cough gets into their chest, and their heart gets tired. It isn't anybody's fault." I hear my own voice, very even, very kind. The warden's voice. "I'm going to call the medics on the Seventh. They'll come and look after her. And I'm going to send a message to the plant, to your mother. All right?"

"All right," says Pilar.

I call the medics. I send the message. I file the death in my ledger, as the Ordinance requires, under *cause*, in the space provided: *Ice cough, pre-existing. Age.* My finger hovers over the line for a moment before I submit it, and then I submit it, because it's true. She did have the cough. She was old.

While I wait for the medics I sit on the bench by the dead heater, and Pilar sits on the platform beside her grandmother, and neither of us says anything. Through the ceiling, faintly, very far up, I can hear the surface being hit. It's a sound we've all learned to stop hearing, these eighteen days: a long dull boom like a door slammed in another house, every hour or so, as one of Grieve's slugs comes down out of the black and strikes the shell of Kettle somewhere over the city. He is careful, the Commodore. Everyone says so. He doesn't hit the tiers. He hits the refinery vents and the dipper docks and the old surface sheds where the fuel was stored, and the city's lungs on the far ice, the great intakes where the water is cracked for air. He is not trying to break us. He is trying to make us breathe less.

On the wall of the corridor outside, every tier now has a board, posted by the Council at the start of each shift. It says RESERVE, and then a number of days. When the siege began it said ninety-four. This morning it says sixty-one.

"Warden," says Pilar.

"Yes."

"When Mama pays, does it go straight back?"

"Yes."

"Even now?"

"Even now. The ledger tells the plant. She doesn't have to find me."

Pilar considers this. Then she gets down off the platform and goes to the hatch and looks at the meter under its frosted window, the needle lying on the red, and wipes the frost off the glass with her cracked glove, the way I do.

"It's very low," she says.

"It's the floor."

"Nobody dies on the floor," says Pilar, to the meter, quite seriously, as if she were reminding it.

## The fall of the Plate

[
  Place: #The Spine
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Malachy Orr @@Lucan Strand
  Items: ^The Share Plate:#The Archive ^Isaura's gauge:@Isaura Venn
  When: same day
]

In the afternoon I'm sent to the Spine.

The order comes down from the Wardens' Office with no explanation, which is how orders always come down: *Venn, I., to report Spine nave, air survey, priority.* When I get there I find out why. A slug in the night came down closer than any before it, on the old dock heads over the Spine, and the shock went down through the hull like a hammer down an anvil, and somewhere in the nave's air system something cracked. The nave's air is the Council's air. It's shared air, untithed, drawn straight from the city's best intakes and scrubbed three times on its way; it's also, like all old systems, a mess of pipes and baffles and valves that nobody has drawn a proper plan of in a century. Somebody has to walk it with a gauge and find the leak. The Council's own engineers are busy on the refinery vents. So they send a warden from the Ninth.

I walk it for three hours. I go up into the roof spaces above the nave on the old maintenance ladders, which were built for riggers two centuries ago and have been painted a dozen times since and never once mended, and crawl along the trunking with my gauge in one hand and my lamp in my teeth, listening. The nave below me is empty. Since the siege began the Council has met in the old bridge, which is deeper and safer, and the shared families have stopped coming up to sit on the benches under the Oculus and look at the Lamp. You can see it through the lens even from up here, sideways: a smear of greenish light much brighter now than it was at Lampfast, with a tail so long it crosses half the lens. The Lamp doesn't care about the Concord. It comes on in.

I find the leak in the end over the drum, where a flange on the main trunk has cracked across its face and is whistling out the Council's good air into the roof void. I can feel it on my cheek. I mark it with chalk and log it, and I'm climbing back down the ladder at the drum's end when the next slug comes.

It's close. Much closer than the one in the night. I feel it before I hear it, a shove from the ladder against my hands and my knees, as if the whole Spine had been kicked; then the sound, which isn't a boom at all this close but a crack, enormous, a sound like the sky being split down the middle; and then the nave rings. The old hull takes the blow and passes it down its whole length like a struck bell, and the ladder hums under me so hard my teeth buzz, and dust comes down out of the roof in a grey sheet, and somewhere below me something lets go.

I hang on. That's all you can do. When the ringing has gone down enough for me to hear myself think, I look down.

The Share Plate has fallen off the drum.

It must have been hanging on the same old fixings since the founding: four steel pins set into the drum's end, with the Plate's top corners hooked over two of them. The hull has rung and the pins have sheared. It's lying on the deck at the drum's foot, face down, in the dust, with one corner bent.

I climb the rest of the way down and stand looking at it. I don't touch it. Nobody touches the Plate. There are shared children who have been taken up to it on their naming days and allowed to lay one finger on the edge of their ancestor's name, and that is all; there are unshared families who have lived in this city for a hundred years and never been within ten metres of it. And now here it is at my feet, face down on the deck like a dropped tray.

The back is grey.

That's what I notice. I've never seen the back of it, nobody has. It's covered, edge to edge, in a coat of thick old grey paint, the kind of hull sealant they used to put on the outside of ships, gone hard and crazed with age, like the skin on old milk. Somebody painted the back of the Share Plate with ship's sealant, a long time ago, carefully, all over. And under the paint, where the light from the Oculus falls across it at a low angle, there are lines.

I crouch down. I still don't touch it. The lines are very faint, just a ridging in the sealant, the way the shape of a coin shows through a sheet of paper laid over it. Rows of them. Short marks, grouped. Something cut into the metal underneath and then painted over, so that only the ghost of the cutting comes through.

"Don't touch it," says a voice behind me.

It's Malachy. He's come up from the Archive in his slippers with his old grey coat thrown over his nightshirt, though it's the middle of the afternoon, and his white hair is full of dust, and he's out of breath. He comes across the nave to me at his slow crane's walk and stands beside me looking down at the Plate.

"I wasn't going to," I say.

"Good. Your hands are filthy." He crouches down beside me, which takes him some time, and puts his face close to the grey back of the Plate, and tilts his head to catch the light the way I did. He looks for a long time. He doesn't say anything. Then he lets out a long breath through his nose.

"Well," he says. "Well, well."

"Is that writing?"

"It's something."

There are footsteps on the nave floor, a lot of them. The Council's stewards, in their grey coats, four of them, with the First Shareholder walking behind. Lucan Strand looks tired. He has dust on his shoulders, too, and a cut over one eye with a dressing on it, and he moves like a man who has been awake for most of eighteen days; but his face when he sees the Plate on the floor goes very still, and then very gentle, the way a man's face goes when he sees a child hurt.

"Is it damaged?"

"One corner bent, First Shareholder," says Malachy, not getting up. "The fixings sheared. I'd like to look at it before it goes back up."

"It must go back up." Strand comes and stands over it. "Today. The people need to see it there. With what's happening—"

"It'll go back up," says Malachy, "on new pins, in a proper frame, when I've made sure that bend hasn't cracked the steel. If you hang it today on the old pins it'll be on the floor again in a week, and next time it might break." He looks up at the First Shareholder with his mild old eyes. "You wouldn't want to be the Shareholder who broke the Plate, Lucan."

Strand looks at him. I think for a moment that he's going to say no. I can see him weighing it: the steward at his elbow, the empty nave, the Lamp over his head in the Oculus, the slugs.

"How long?" he says.

"A week. Two at most."

"In the Archive."

"In the Archive, under lock, where it's safe. You can come and look at it any time you like."

Strand lets out a breath. "Very well. A week." He turns to the stewards. "Help the Archivist carry it. Gently." He looks down at me as if noticing me for the first time. "You found it?"

"I was surveying the air, First Shareholder. There's a cracked flange on the main trunk, over the drum. I've marked it."

"Thank you." He's already turning away. "Thank you, warden. Good work."

The stewards pick the Plate up between them, carefully, face down still, the grey back uppermost, and carry it toward the Archive stairs like a stretcher, with Malachy hovering behind. Nobody but me and Malachy looks at the back. The stewards are looking at their feet on the stairs. Strand is looking at the pins.

As Malachy goes past me, he puts his hand on my arm for a moment, very lightly.

"Come and see me tonight," he says. "After curfew. Bring your lamp."

## South Lock

[
  Place: #South Lock
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Mattias Ebb @@Dasha Imre
  Items: ^Isaura's gauge:@Isaura Venn
  When: same day
]

I don't get to the Archive that night. At the eighth bell, as I'm coming off shift, the city's guns open up for the first time in the siege.

I didn't know we had guns. I don't think most people did. There are batteries, it turns out, in old pits on the surface ice around the city, built a hundred years ago in some war scare that never came to anything and maintained ever since by a few dozen shared families who have the right to do it the way other shared families have the right to the dipper fleet. They've waited eighteen days. Now, all at once, they fire, and the whole city hears them: a sound like heavy doors being slammed, very fast, one after another, coming down through the ice.

Everybody stops in the corridors and looks up.

The word comes down the speakers a few minutes later, crackling and excited. A Concord lander has come down low over the south ice, probing, the first time any of Grieve's ships has come close enough to touch, and the batteries have hit it. It's down. It's on the ice south of the city, a kilometre from South Lock. All damage-control parties to South Lock. All air-wardens with surface rating to South Lock.

I have surface rating. All wardens do; we read the meters on the intakes. I go.

South Lock is a vast old chamber cut in the ice at the bottom of a sloping ramp, with the surface doors at its top end and a long low gallery down one side where the suits hang on their racks like a row of hanged men. When I get there it's already full of people. Damage-control crews in orange. Wardens in grey. Militia in their blue armbands with their old rifles. And riggers, a lot of riggers, the big tattooed men and women of the dipper fleet, who've been grounded eighteen days by the Council's order and have nothing to do and are in the mood for something to do it to.

The doors are open when I get there. The lock's cycled. A team in surface suits has gone out across the ice on a crawler and is coming back, and on the big screen over the gallery we can all see the feed from the crawler's lamps: white ice, black sky, Gall's banded edge on the horizon, and a long smear of wreckage going away across the snow, burning green and orange where its fuel has caught. In the middle of the wreckage, something moving.

"There's one alive," someone says.

"Not for long."

The crawler comes back down the ramp with the outer doors grinding shut behind it, ice streaming off its tracks. The lock cycles. When the inner doors open, the crowd surges forward, and I'm pushed forward with it, and I see the damage-control crew climbing down off the crawler's bed with a body between them in a scorched white Concord pressure suit.

They put it down on the floor of the lock. The suit's helmet is cracked across the visor. There's frost on the inside of the crack. The figure inside it moves its hands, weakly, at the clasps of the helmet, as if it wants to get it off.

"Leave it," says somebody. "It's on its own air."

"Its air's gone," says one of the damage-control crew, a woman with a burned face, crouching by the helmet. "Tank's holed. It's breathing what's left in the suit." She looks up. "Maybe ten minutes."

"Then leave it ten minutes," says a rigger at the front of the crowd, a huge man with comet-tails tattooed up both forearms, and there's a growl of agreement behind him. "Let it breathe what's left. That's what they're doing to us."

"Put it out on the ice."

"Let it have the floor."

That gets a laugh, an ugly one. It goes round the lock like a wave. *Let it have the floor.* The Concord pilot's hands have stopped moving at the clasps. They lie on its chest. Through the cracked visor I can see a face, very young, very pale, with its eyes open, looking up at all of us.

I'm not brave. I want to say that clearly, as I said the other thing clearly before. What I do next isn't brave. It's habit. I'm an air-warden. I have spent twelve years kneeling beside meters, and what I'm looking at is a meter reading red.

I push through to the front, and kneel down beside the burned-faced woman, and take the spare bottle off my own belt. Every warden carries a spare, a little twenty-minute flask with a universal coupling, for when we're out on the intakes. I find the suit's emergency port under the left armpit, where every suit in eleven lanes has had it since before the founding, and I knock the frost off it with my glove and screw the coupling in, and open the valve.

The suit hisses. The frost on the inside of the visor starts, very slowly, to clear.

"What are you doing?" says the tattooed rigger.

"Reading a meter," I say.

There is a silence in the lock. I can feel it on the back of my neck, a hundred people looking at me. I don't look round. I watch the frost clear on the visor, and the young pale face behind it blink, and its lips move.

"Warden," says the rigger, in a voice I don't like.

"It's my air," I say. "It's on my account. If you want to take it off me, you'll have to take it off me."

Nobody moves. I don't know what would have happened next. I don't know, to this day, whether they would have; I think perhaps they would. But the militia have got to the front by then, three of them with rifles and blue armbands and an officer with a whistle, and the officer is shouting that the prisoner is the property of the Council and anybody who lays a hand on him will be on the floor for a year. The crowd backs off, growling. The burned-faced woman helps me get the cracked helmet off. Underneath it the pilot is a man, young, younger than me, with a soldier's cropped fair hair and a soldier's frightened eyes, and a smear of blood under his nose.

He looks at me. He breathes. It's the first thing I ever hear him do.

"Thank you," he says, in the clipped accent of the inner lanes.

"Don't," I say. "It's on your account."

The militia take him. They lift him on to a stretcher and carry him up the ramp toward the inner city, and the officer with the whistle tells me to report to the Wardens' Office in the morning with my bottle receipt, and goes after them. The crowd breaks up, slowly, sullenly, back to whatever it was doing. The tattooed rigger stands looking at me a while longer and then goes. I'm left kneeling on the wet ice floor of the lock with my empty bottle in my hand.

Somebody crouches down beside me. A woman, a rigger, forty-odd, small and wiry, with her sleeves rolled up over forearms tattooed from wrist to elbow with the long thin fans of comets, one inside another, like the rings in a tree.

"You gave him your air, warden," she says.

"I gave him a bottle."

"That's a first." She looks at me with a sort of amused curiosity, as if I were a dog that had started to talk. "Twelve years I've lived on the Ninth North and I never once saw a warden give anybody air."

"It's not a warden's job to give air."

"No," she agrees. "It's not." She stands up and dusts off her knees. "I'm Dasha. Dasha Imre. Rigger. Grounded." She holds out her hand to pull me up. "You want to be careful, warden. Giving people things. It gets to be a habit."

I let her pull me up. "Isaura," I say. "Venn."

"I know who you are," says Dasha Imre. "Everybody on the Ninth knows who you are." She grins, quite friendly. "That's why it's a first."

When I get back to my room on the Fourth it's past the tenth bell, and there's a message waiting on my wrist. It's from the Archive. It's very short, in Malachy's old-fashioned way, the way he used to write notes in the margins of my exercises when I was ten.

*Isaura. There's writing under the paint. Come tomorrow. Come alone. M.*
