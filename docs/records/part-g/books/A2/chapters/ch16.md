# The Sling

## For luck

[
  Place: #The Patience
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Abel Tsolo @@Ekene Adu
  Items: ^The Hush:#The Gull ^Tether-chief's coat:@Abel Tsolo ^Maren's hook-knife:@Abel Tsolo ^Quist's key:@Maren Tsolo
  When: 29 November 2391
]

The Gull was a scoop-tug, which meant that she was not meant to go anywhere.

She was built to live in the *Patience*'s belly, in a cradle under the Bucket dock, and to go out on the end of the line when the Bucket fouled, or tore, or lost a scoop vane, and nudge it, and hold it, and bring it home. She was squat and ugly and enormously strong, a blunt armoured box with four big thrusters at the corners and a pair of grapple arms folded under her nose like a praying insect's, and a fuel tank that would carry her perhaps two hundred kilometres from the ship and back. She had a heat shield on her belly for the cloud-tops and an ice auger on her nose for clearing rime off the Bucket's intakes. She had one seat. She had air for four days, because the Combine's rules said a tug had to carry air for four days, and nobody had ever needed more than four hours.

For two days the dock crew had been making her into something she was not.

They had pulled the second grapple and put in a fuel bladder where it had been. They had stripped out everything inside her cabin that could be stripped, to save mass, and welded a cradle into her cargo well for the cryostat, with its own feed from her power cells. They had moved the auger to her belly, angled down, so that she could sit on ice and drill straight into it. They had fitted a spool of light line behind her seat, two hundred metres of it, and a winch. They had checked her thrusters four times. Senna and Ivo had not slept. Teska had sat in the dock on an upturned crate for the whole of both days, watching, wrapped in her two coats, saying nothing at all, which in Teska was a kind of prayer.

The cryostat went in on the twenty-ninth, at the second bell of the night. Ekene walked it down from the infirmary himself on its sled, with two riggers pushing, and watched it lowered into its cradle and clamped and plugged into the Gull's cells, and watched the ring of status lights go from amber to green. Then he stood in the dock with his hands at his sides and looked at it for a long time.

"It'll hold," said Senna. "Four days on the cells. Five if she's careful."

"She won't need five," said Maren.

She was in her coat. The tether-chief's coat, the heavy quilted pressure-coat that had been Hesketh's before her and somebody's before Hesketh, with its hood and its rings for the helmet and its patched knees, and TSOLO stencilled in white across the back where it had said HESKETH for twenty years, and before that some other name. She had put it on in the gallery at the start of the night and come down the ladder in it, and the dock crew had gone quiet when they saw her, the way the drum hall went quiet when a runner's crawler came back empty. Her line knife hung on its loop at her hip, the hook-bladed tether knife every chief carried, in its sheath. She had her helmet under her arm.

The plan was simple, as all the best plans on the line were simple. In the morning the *Patience* would dip, deep, deeper than she had dipped in ten years, down into the high cloud on Gall's dawn side where the line could take the most strain. The Gull would go down the line, clamped to the Bucket's coupling like a crawler. At the bottom of the dip, with the Lamp coming in over Gall's limb at its closest, the drum would spin the line, faster and faster, with the Bucket and the Gull on the end of it, swinging them out and round in a great arc like a stone in a sling; and at the one right moment, the moment only a tether-chief could feel, the release would fire, and the Gull would fly. Off the end of the line. Up out of the cloud and away, faster than anything the *Patience* or the *Admonition* or any ship in Corva could make on its own, on a long curve that would meet the Lamp where it swung past Gall.

And then the Gull would land on the Lamp, and drill, and put the cryostat deep in the ice, where it would sleep in the cold for two hundred and sixty years.

And then the Gull would go on with the Lamp, out past the cold. Because the sling would throw her fast enough to catch it, and not one metre a second more, and she had fuel to land and none to come home.

"You'll run the throw from the Gull," Ekene said. He hadn't looked at her since she came down the ladder. "Not the gallery."

"Odile's running the gallery. Under Teska. I've taught her the count." Maren checked the rings on her collar. "Odile's the best feel for the line on this ship after me. She'll do it."

"Odile is nineteen."

"I was nineteen once."

"You were never nineteen," said Ekene, and turned round at last, and she saw his face, and looked away.

There was a sound at the dock hatch. Abel.

He had a bandage across his face, from the eyebrow to the cheekbone, white against his grey skin, and his eyes above it were red-rimmed and very bright. He was in his rigger's coveralls. He had a flask in his hand, a little dented steel one, and he held it up as he came across the dock floor, and smiled at her, the old smile, crooked under the bandage.

"For luck," he said. "It's the *Clement*'s brandy. The good stuff. The factor's own. I nicked it out of his cabin the night we took the ship and I've been saving it." He held it out. "Mum always said you drink before you go down the line. For luck. So Gall knows you're coming."

"I'm not going down the line."

"You're going further down than anybody ever went," said Abel. "Go on. One. For Mum."

She looked at him. He was standing in front of her with the flask held out, and the bandage, and the bright red eyes, and his hand was not shaking. It had been shaking for a month. It was not shaking now.

She took the flask and drank.

It was brandy. Real brandy, sweet and hot and smelling of fruit, the factor's own. She felt it go down and spread warm in her chest, and she handed him back the flask, and he drank too, a long swallow, and wiped his mouth.

"For Mum," he said.

"For Mum."

"And for Lusine," said Abel quietly, and looked at her with his wet bright eyes over the bandage. "I'm sorry, Mar. I'm so sorry. I can't—there isn't enough sorry. There won't ever be." He put the flask in his pocket. "But I can do this."

"Do what?"

She knew, as she said it. She knew because the warmth in her chest had gone wrong, had gone heavy, had gone down into her legs and her arms like water into sand; because the dock floor had begun, very slowly, to tilt; because Ekene, beside her, had turned and was looking not at her but at Abel, and his face had gone from grief to horror to something else, something that was almost, terribly, relief.

"It's from the infirmary," said Abel. "The green bottle in the cage. Ekene keeps it for surgery." He caught her as her knees went. "I took it when he was stitching me. He didn't see. I'm sorry, Mar. Twenty drops. Ekene says that's eight hours for a big man. You're not a big man."

"Abel—"

"It's my line," said Abel. "It was always my line. You said so. In the infirmary, to Ekene. *Somebody has to carry it.*" He was lowering her to the deck, very gently, his arm under her shoulders, the way she had lowered him into his bunk when he was three and asleep in the Belly. "I heard you. I was lying there behind the curtain with my face stitched up, and I heard you say it, and I thought, *no*. Not her. Not you. You've carried everything. You carried me." He was unbuckling her coat. "You can't carry this as well. Let me."

"No—"

"Shh." He was pulling the coat off her arms. She couldn't stop him; her arms were stone. "You've got to run the line. Nobody else can run the line. Odile's good, but she's not you. If you're on the Gull, who throws it?" He got the coat free and stood up with it in his arms, the heavy quilted pressure-coat with her name on the back. "And who's going to look after them after? The Camp. Teska. Odile. Him." He looked at Ekene. "You can't be on the Lamp, Mar. They need you here. Nobody needs me anywhere."

"Ekene," said Maren, or tried to. "Stop him."

Ekene didn't move.

He was standing by the cryostat's empty sled with his hands at his sides, and his long gentle face was wet, and he was looking at Abel, and he didn't move. And Maren, going down into the dark with her cheek on the cold deck plates of the dock, understood that he would not; that he had seen the flask, and seen Abel's steady hand, and seen what was in Abel's eyes, and had made his choice in the time it took her to drink, the way she made her choices on the line, and had chosen her.

Abel put on the coat. It fitted him. They had always been the same height.

He crouched down beside her on the deck, in her coat, with her line knife on its loop at his hip, and put his hand on her hair.

"Run the line, Chief," he said. "Throw me straight."

The last thing she saw was the white of his bandage in the dock lights, and the stencil on the back of the coat as he stood up and turned away toward the Gull, TSOLO in white capitals, going away from her.

## The throw

[
  Place: #The tether deck
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Ekene Adu @@Odile Arda @@Corwin Hale @@Bram Kovac @@Grandam Teska
  Characters: @@Abel Tsolo:remote
  When: 30 November 2391
]

She woke on the gallery floor, with the line singing.

It came up through the deck into her cheek, into her skull, the long low note of the tether under load, and for a moment she was nine years younger and a runner asleep on her crawler at twenty kilometres and somebody was shouting at her to clamp on. Then she opened her eyes and saw the board. The long green trace of the tension, high and steady. The fat amber band of the Bucket's intake, empty. And at the bottom of the trace, at forty kilometres, where the Bucket's dot sat in the cloud-tops, a second dot clamped beside it. Small and green. Labelled, in the board's neat capitals, GULL.

"She's awake," said somebody.

Ekene. He was kneeling beside her on the gallery floor. He had a cup in his hand, and a hypo in the other, and he looked as if he had not slept for a year.

"I gave you a counter," he said. "Twenty minutes ago. You've been out six hours. I'm sorry."

She sat up. The gallery swung round her once, sickeningly, and steadied. It was full of people. Teska on her stool by the board, very small, wrapped in her coats. Odile at the tether-chief's station with her hands on the damper and her face white and set, staring at the trace. Three of the runners. Senna. Two riggers she didn't know well. The long window full of Gall, ochre and purple, the dawn side, the great southern storm turning on the limb with its single dark eye; and over the storm, filling the whole upper half of the window, so bright that the gallery's own lamps looked dim, the Lamp.

"Where is he?" Maren said.

Nobody answered. Odile, at the board, didn't turn round.

"Chief," said a voice from the speaker over the board. Channel four. The chief's private, unlogged, where tether-chiefs had always gone to swear. "Morning, Chief. Sleep well?"

She got up. Her legs nearly went; Ekene caught her arm and she shook him off and went to the board and put both hands on its rim and looked at the trace.

"Abel," she said.

"Here, Mar."

"Come up. Come back up the line. Now."

"Can't do that. Sorry." His voice was quite cheerful, quite clear. Under it she could hear the Gull's cabin, the hiss of its air, the deep thrum of the line coming up through its clamps. "I'm clamped on at the bottom and Odile's had me here two hours waiting for you. We're eleven minutes from the throw. The Lamp's coming over the limb. Can you see it?"

"Abel, come up."

"If I come up," said Abel, "it's two hours up the line, and the Lamp's gone past, and that's it. No throw. Not ever. And the *Admonition*'s been creeping toward us all night, Odile says. She's eighty thousand kilometres off our bow and closing. If the Gull's still on the line when she gets here, she takes it." A pause. "You know all this, Mar. You worked it out. You just haven't caught up."

She looked at Odile. Odile was staring at the trace, her hands on the damper, her jaw set so hard Maren could see the muscle in it jump.

"You knew," said Maren.

"I found him in the dock," said Odile, without turning round. "At the third bell. In your coat. With you on the deck and the medic sitting by you. I had my knife." She swallowed. "He said, *let me go, and I'll go where she can't follow me, and nobody'll ever see me again*. He said, *that's better than you can do with a knife.*" Her voice cracked. "He was right."

"Eleven minutes," said Abel on channel four. "Chief, I need you on the board. Odile's good but she says she can't feel it. Not the throw. She says it's yours."

"It's yours," said Odile. She took her hands off the damper and stepped back from the station, and looked at Maren for the first time, with her swollen fierce face. "I can't. I can feel it but I can't do it. Not to him. Not even to him." She held out the throw handle, the long brass lever on its cable that fired the release on the Bucket's coupling, and her hand was shaking. "You do it."

Maren looked at the handle.

The gallery door slammed open.

Hale came through it first. Then Bram Kovač, with his shock-stick, and four other trusties behind him, and one of them had a flechette carbine; and behind them in the doorway, with his hands tied in front of him with a length of medical tubing and a trusty's hand on his arm, Aurelian Quist, very pale.

"Reel it in, Chief," said Hale.

He said it pleasantly. He stood just inside the door with his slate under his arm, as always, and his big hands loose at his sides, and his soft voice raised just enough to carry over the singing of the line.

"Reel it in," he said again. "Bring the Gull up. Bring the cryostat back aboard. It's over." He nodded at the window, toward the *Admonition*, which was somewhere out there beyond the Lamp's glare, closing. "I've been talking to Captain Lachance on the long beacon. Since the night of the vote. He's a very reasonable man. He'll take the Sanitation Agent back, and the factor, and the *Clement*, and he'll leave us the *Patience*. All of us. And the Camp. On terms." He spread his hands. "We'll be the Corva Shareholders. We'll buy the operation from the Combine, the whole thing, for one share. The Combine writes it off and keeps a share and walks away, and nobody hangs, and nobody goes to Sorrel. We just have to give it back."

"You sold us," said Teska from her stool.

"I bought us, Grandam." Hale didn't look at her. He was looking at Maren. "The Lamp was always a beautiful idea, Chief. I voted against it because it's beautiful. Beautiful ideas get people killed. This one's going to get your brother killed, out on that ice, for nothing; because the Combine will still come. They'll come for the ship and the Camp and every one of us, the way you said, and they won't talk, because they'll have nothing to talk about any more. We'll have thrown away the only thing they want." He took a step forward. "Reel it in. Let me trade it. It's the only thing we've got."

"No," said Maren.

"Bram," said Hale.

Bram Kovač came across the gallery with his shock-stick, fast, and Odile went to meet him.

Maren didn't see it. She didn't see any of it, afterward, except in pieces, the way she remembered the night in the cold hold: the crack and blue spark of the shock-stick, a runner going down, Senna shouting; the carbine going off once into the ceiling with a sound like a fist on a drum, and plaster and ice dust raining down; Teska, unbelievably, up off her stool and swinging it by one leg into the knees of the trusty with the carbine; Ekene, who had never in his life hit anybody, standing in front of Maren with his arms spread like Danek in front of the cryostat, between her and the fight, and taking a shock-stick across the shoulder for it and going down on one knee and getting up again. She didn't see any of it, because she was looking at the board.

The trace was walking.

Not much. A slow long wave going down the line, from the drum to the Bucket, the way it went in the moment before a sling, when the drum began to spin up; somebody had put a hand on the spin control in the struggle. The wave reached the bottom. The Gull's dot shivered.

"Chief," said Abel on channel four. "Mar. What's happening up there?"

"Hale," said Maren.

"Ah." A breath. "He wants it back."

"He wants it back."

"Then don't let him have it." Abel's voice was quite calm. "Five minutes, Mar. The Lamp's right over the limb. I can see it out of the port. It's—God, it's big. It's the biggest thing I ever saw." A pause. "Spin me up."

She put her hand on the spin control.

Behind her in the gallery somebody was screaming. She didn't turn round. She rolled the spin control on, slowly, the way Hesketh had taught her twenty-six years ago, feeling it come up through the board into her palms; and down under the gallery the great drum began to turn faster, and faster, and the line began to swing.

You could not see it. You could only feel it, in the deck and the board and the soles of your feet: forty kilometres of carbon braid, with the Bucket and the Gull on the end, beginning to swing out and round from the keel of the *Patience* in a vast slow arc through the dawn cloud-tops of Gall, like the arm of a man throwing a stone. The trace climbed. The note rose. The whole ship leaned into it. She had done this four times in her life, and never with anyone on the end.

"That's it," said Abel softly. "That's it, Mar. That's lovely. I can feel it."

"Two minutes."

"Two minutes." A pause. "I'm recording something. On the Gull's log. For whoever comes. When it comes round. Somebody ought to know what it is and who put it there and why." Another pause. "I'm going to tell them about Lusine. I'm going to tell them it was me."

"Abel—"

"I want to. Let me." The line was singing so high now that his voice came through it thin and strange. "And I took your coat. I want you to know that. It's warmer than mine. You never let me borrow it. Not once in my life."

"You can have it," said Maren. Her voice was gone. She was watching the trace. "You can have it, Abel."

"Ninety seconds."

There was a hand on her shoulder. Big, heavy. She knew it without looking.

"Chief," said Corwin Hale's soft voice in her ear, very close. "Take your hand off the board."

She didn't.

"I don't want to hurt you," said Hale. "I never wanted to hurt anybody. Take your hand off the board and let the drum run down and let him come home. That's all. He can come home, Maren. Nobody has to go out there."

"You had Lusine killed," said Maren, without turning round.

The hand on her shoulder went very still.

"With a jeweller's file," she said. "Two-thirds through. You told him it would go at the lock check. You knew it wouldn't. Twenty years on this ship, Hale. You know how a clip fails." She was watching the trace, the needle climbing to the mark. "You had her killed because she read the dispatch and the factor said *contain*. And then you came to the Belly and offered us your sticks."

"Sixty seconds," said Abel, very far away.

"I'm going to throw him," Maren said. "And when he's gone I'm going to stand up in the drum hall and tell them all what you did. All of it. And then you can do whatever you like to me."

Hale's hand left her shoulder.

She never knew why. Ekene told her afterward that Odile had come up behind Hale with her line knife, the hook-knife she'd cut Abel's face with, and laid the point of it against the side of his neck under the ear, and said something to him very quietly that Ekene didn't hear; and that Hale had stood there for a moment with the point against his neck and then taken his hand away, slowly, and stepped back. Maybe that was it. Maybe it was something else. Maybe Corwin Hale, who always dealt with whoever held power, looked at the board and the trace and the woman with her hands on it and saw, in that moment, who held it.

"Thirty," said Abel. "Mar. I love you. Throw me straight."

She put her hand on the throw.

"Twenty. Ten."

The needle touched the mark. The note of the line went up to a pitch she had never heard, a long high singing like a bell struck under water, and the whole ship leaned, and she felt it, the way she had always felt it, the way her mother had and Hesketh had and every chief back up the line to the first chiefs on the first skimmers: the moment. The one right moment. Not a number. Not a count. A thing in the hands and the spine and the soles of the feet that said *now*.

She pulled the throw.

Forty kilometres below her, in the dawn cloud of Gall, the release on the Bucket's coupling fired, a guillotine of hardened steel slamming through the clamp that held the Gull; and the line, which had been carrying the Gull's weight at the bottom of its great swing, let go of it.

It was a cut. That was what the old chiefs called it, the throw. *The cut.* You cut the line and let go.

On the board, the Gull's green dot left the Bucket's. It went out and up off the end of the trace, slowly at first, and then not slowly, in a long clean curve away from Gall and the *Patience* and everything, up out of the cloud and into the black, toward the blaze of the Lamp.

The line, with nothing on its end but the Bucket, sang down from its high note to a low one, and to a lower, and settled.

The gallery was silent.

"Straight," said Abel's voice on channel four, thin and full of static and very happy. "Oh, Mar. That's straight. That's the straightest throw anyone ever threw."

## Channel four

[

  When: 30 November 2391
]

The *Admonition* tried.

Maren watched her on the board, all that day, a small grey mark coming round from beyond the Lamp's glare, burning hard, swinging in toward the Gull's track. Captain Lachance had good engines and a good crew and a ship built for speed, and for three hours she closed. And then she stopped closing. She was going the wrong way, as Maren had told the drum hall she would; she had to kill all her own speed and build it again in the Lamp's direction, and the Gull had the whole of a forty-kilometre sling behind her, and the Lamp's own fall to ride. At the fourth hour the *Admonition*'s mark turned, very slowly, and fell back, and lay off in the dark, doing nothing at all.

"She's given up," said Odile. She hadn't left the gallery. None of them had.

"She's waiting," said Teska. "That's different. Lachance is waiting to see what he can get." She sniffed. "Let him wait."

Abel talked.

He talked all day. Channel four held as long as the Gull was in range of the *Patience*'s big antenna, and he talked into it, to Maren, as the Gull fell away from Gall and the giant dwindled behind him and the Lamp grew ahead. He talked about nothing. About the Gull's terrible seat. About the cryostat in its cradle behind him, humming, green. About Mum, and the third shaft, and the time he'd fallen into the refinery sump at seven and Maren had gone in after him. About Lusine, a little. Not much. About the Lamp.

"It's not white," he said, at the sixth hour. "Close up. It's black. Did you know? It's all black, like soot, like old cable, with white coming out of it. Jets. Big ones. Like breath. And there's a crack down the middle where the two halves join, like it's been put together out of two stones. And the light's coming off it everywhere." A pause. "It's beautiful, Mar. It's the ugliest beautiful thing I ever saw."

He landed at the ninth hour, near the bottom of the Lamp's swing past Gall, at its closest. He talked her through it, the way a runner talked a chief through a fouled Bucket: thrusters, contact, the grapple biting into the ice. The Lamp had almost no gravity. The Gull sat on it like a fly on a wall.

"There's a ridge," he said. "On the south end. The bottom lobe. A big black ridge, like a spine, like a broken wall. It throws a shadow. It's cold in the shadow, the Gull's thermometer says eighty kelvin on the surface and colder down. I'm going to drill under it. The south side of the black ridge. Out of the sun."

"How deep?"

"As deep as the auger goes. Forty metres, near enough." She heard him unclip from the seat. "Then the cryostat goes down. And then I go down."

"You don't have to go down."

"I'm not sitting out here on top of it in the sun watching Gall go away, Mar." His voice was gentle. "I'll go down with it. I'll cut a room at the bottom, out of the shaft, a little one, and sit with it. Keep it company. Till its cells run out and it's just the cold keeping it." A breath. "Till mine do."

She didn't say anything.

He drilled. It took him six hours. She listened to it, on channel four: the deep shuddering whine of the auger biting down, the pauses while he cleared the cuttings, his breathing in his suit. At the tenth hour Ekene brought her a cup of something and she held it until it was cold. At the twelfth Teska fell asleep on her stool and Odile put a coat over her. At the thirteenth the auger stopped.

"Forty-one metres," said Abel. "That's the end of the auger. That's as deep as she goes."

"Can you get it down?"

"I've got the winch. I'll lower it on the line." She heard him working. Clamps. A grunt. The cryostat's hum, louder, as he unclipped it from its cradle and walked it, in that nothing gravity, to the hatch. "God, it's heavy. Even here. It doesn't want to go."

"Go slow."

"I'm going slow, Chief." Almost a laugh. "I'm not new."

He lowered it. Forty-one metres down a shaft the width of a man's shoulders in the dark black ice of the Lamp's south limb, under the shadow of the ridge, on two hundred metres of light line. Then he went down after it, on the same line, hand over hand, the way a runner goes down a tether; and at the bottom, he told her, he cut a room, with the Gull's hand-pick, a little room off the side of the shaft, big enough for a man to sit in with his back against the wall and his knees up, and a cryostat beside him.

"It's quiet down here," he said, at the seventeenth hour. His voice was very faint now, through the relay he'd left on the Gull at the top of the shaft, and through all that ice, and across the widening gulf between the Lamp and Gall. "It's quiet. I can't hear the jets. I can't hear anything. Just the cryostat. It's got a hum like the drum, when the drum's running slow, at the end of a dip. You know the one."

"I know the one."

"It's cold. It's not so bad. The coat's warm." A long pause. "I've done the recording. On the Gull's log, and on my own, the little one, the rigger's log. I'll keep that one with me. In case somebody comes down." Another pause. "I told them everything. Lusine. Hale. The jeweller's file. You. I told them your name, Mar. Maren Tsolo. I said you were the tether-chief. I said you threw me straight."

"Abel."

"I said it was your plan and your line and you were going to come yourself and I wouldn't let you. I said it so they'd know. Whoever comes. So they'd know it wasn't you sitting here. So they wouldn't think you'd—" The static rose and swallowed it. "—you're there. You're on the board. Somebody needs to be."

"I'm here."

"I know. I can tell. You've got your chief's breathing." He laughed, very faintly. "Mar. Is the Lamp round yet? Have we gone round?"

She looked at the board. At the long curve of the Lamp's track on Oz's—on the navigator's—on the old projection the riggers had drawn up for the throw, the falling curve coming in past Gall's limb and swinging round the giant, closest, closest, and beginning, now, to go out.

"We've gone round," she said. "It's going out."

"Good," said Abel. "That's good. That's the long round." A breath, very slow. "Teska says it brings them back."

"Teska says a lot of things."

"I hope she's right," said Abel. "I'd like to come back. I'd like to see what they make of it." The static rose. "Mar. Tell Odile I'm sorry. Tell her I said it. Tell Ekene thank you; he'll know. Tell Teska—"

And the channel was full of static, and stayed full of it.

She sat on at the board for a long time, with her hands on its rim. The gallery was quiet around her. Somebody had turned the lamps down. Through the long window Gall turned, ochre and purple, the dawn side gone round to day, and over it, already a little smaller, already a little further, going out, the Lamp burned on, with its tails fanned across half the sky; and somewhere on its black south limb, under a broken ridge, forty-one metres down in the cold, her brother sat in her coat beside a grey egg in its humming cryostat, and kept it company.

At some point Ekene came and sat on the deck beside her chair and put his head against her knee, and she put her hand on his hair, and they stayed like that.

At some point, much later, she found she was crying. She had not cried in nine years. She cried at the board, with her hand on Ekene's hair and the static hissing on channel four, without any sound at all, for her brother, who had killed Lusine Arda with a jeweller's file and taken her coat and gone out on the Lamp in her place; and Odile Arda, at the next station, sat with her hands in her lap and watched the Lamp going out, and after a while, without saying anything, reached across and took Maren's other hand, and held it.
