# Sanitation

## The Belly

[
  Place: #The Belly
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Odile Arda @@Grandam Teska @@Danek Sorell @@Corwin Hale @@Bram Kovac @@Abel Tsolo
  When: 11 November 2391
]

Under the drum there was a space nobody had ever found a use for.

The drum sat in a cradle of girders that went down through three decks to the keel, and between the girders, under the drum's great curved belly, the shipwrights had left a hollow the size of a chapel: a ribbed, dark, echoing void full of cable runs and bearing housings and old grease, where the noise of the drum when it was turning was so loud you could not hear your own voice, and when it was not turning, so quiet you could hear the hull tick. The crew called it the Belly. You got into it by a maintenance hatch behind the number four brake, and a ladder, and a long crawl on your hands and knees along a cable tray. Riggers went there to sleep off shifts and to do things they did not want logged. Wardens did not go there, because wardens were big men and the cable tray was narrow.

Maren called the meeting for the fourth bell of the night, in the dead hour between dips, when the drum was still.

Eleven of them came. She had asked for twelve. They sat on the girders and the bearing housings in a rough ring, with one lamp turned low on the deck plates between them, and their faces came and went in its light: Teska, wrapped in two coats, with her white eye shining; Odile, very upright, very quiet; Danek Sorell, too big for the space, folded up on a girder with his knees under his chin; old Pirro from the Bucket crew; two dock hands, a married pair called Senna and Ivo, who between them knew every valve on the forward collar; three riggers Maren trusted and two she trusted less, because you needed the ones who talked as well as the ones who didn't. Ekene was not there. She had not asked him. A free man could be hanged for what the bonded only had their accounts reviewed for.

Abel was not there either. She had asked him. He had said yes, and smiled, and not come.

"Read it," said Teska. "Read the paper. I want to hear it in my own ears."

Maren read it. She read it in the tether-chief's voice, without any expression at all, every line, the routing codes and the board resolution and the numbers, *est. 300*, *est. 2,100 households*, *supply to cease*. When she had finished nobody said anything for a long time. Somewhere in the hull below them a pump came on and ran and went off again.

"Sorrel," said Danek at last, in the same voice he had used in the hall. "The forts."

"The forts."

"And the Camp."

"Three months. Four if they're careful."

"Tamar's got the cough already. She had it last winter." Danek looked at his own big hands. "Four months she won't make."

"Nobody'll make it," said Pirro. "Not the old ones. Not the little ones. The plant'll go first, then the heat. You'll have people burning their bunks." He spat into the dark. "I was in the Camp when the third shaft caved in sixty-eight. We were nine days without the plant. I saw what nine days did."

"So we don't go," said Senna, from the dock. "We refuse. Strike. They can't move three hundred people on to the *Clement* if three hundred people won't walk."

"They can if they've got sticks."

"They've got six marines and a dozen trusties. We're three hundred."

"And the *Admonition*," said Maren. "Two weeks behind the factor. Sixty marines and a torpedo bay. Due on the twenty-second." She looked round the ring. "And then they don't need us to walk."

"Then we take the ship," said Teska.

They all looked at her. She was sitting hunched on her bearing housing in her two coats, very small, with her good eye glittering in the lamp.

"We take her," she said. "She's ours. We built her. Well, they built her, but we've had her sixty years; we've kept her flying when they wouldn't send the parts. Every bolt in her's been turned by one of us. We take her and we don't sink her. We keep dipping. We keep the Camp breathing." She shrugged inside the coats. "Let the Combine come and get her."

"The Combine will," said Maren.

"Then we'll be dead in a month instead of four. I'm eighty-three. I can spare a month."

"Danek's children can't."

"Danek's children," said Teska, with great gentleness, "are going to die on the ice anyway, Maren. That paper says so. All you're doing is choosing which way."

Nobody said anything to that.

Maren had known it was coming. She had known since the night in the Shelf, with the foil in her hand and Odile's face in front of her, that it would come to this: a ring of tired people in the dark under the drum, with nothing in front of them but a wall, and somebody saying the thing out loud. She had spent four days running the arithmetic, in the gallery, at the board, in Ekene's bunk with her eyes open. It came out the same every time. Strike, and they would be broken. Comply, and the Camp would die. Fight, and the *Admonition* would come, and then the Camp would die and so would they.

"If we take her," she said, "we have to take the *Clement* too. Same night. Same hour. Otherwise the factor gets on her and casts off and he's two weeks from the *Admonition* and he tells them everything. And the *Clement*'s got the marines, and the marines have guns."

"How many?"

"Six. Sergeant called Oyelowo. They're good. They're not like the trusties. They've done this before."

"We'll need sticks," said a voice from the dark at the edge of the ring. "At least."

The lamp didn't reach where it came from. It didn't need to. Everybody knew that voice, soft and patient and reasonable, and the ring went still the way a rigger goes still on the line when the trace jumps.

Corwin Hale came forward into the light at his slow walk, ducking under a girder. Behind him, in the dark, Maren could see the shape of Bram Kovač crouched at the end of the cable tray, blocking it.

"I'm sorry to interrupt," said Hale. "I didn't mean to listen. Sound carries in here. It's a terrible place for a secret."

"How did you get in?" said Odile. "The tray's too narrow for you."

"There's a hatch from the keel side, if you know where it is." Hale smiled faintly. "I've been on this ship twenty years, Arda. I know where everything is." He looked round the ring. His eyes rested on each of them in turn, mild and unhurried, as if he were taking a register: Teska, Danek, Pirro, Senna, Ivo, the riggers. Last of all he looked at Maren. "May I sit down?"

Nobody answered. He sat anyway, on the end of Pirro's girder, so that the old man had to shift up.

"You've read the dispatch," he said. "I know you have. Arda's sister had it in her boot. I've been waiting for you to do something about it." He folded his big hands on his knee. "I'm on the list too, you know. I'm bonded. I've got an account. My mother's in the Camp, on the second shaft, and she's sixty-eight, and she doesn't have four months either." He looked at Danek. "Trusties go to Sorrel with everybody else, Sorell. They don't need wardens on the forts. They've got officers for that. I'll be filling sandbags beside you."

"You'll be filling sandbags," said Teska, "when Gall freezes."

"Grandam," said Hale, "I know what you think of me. I know what you all think of me. You think I'm the Combine's dog. You're right. I've been the Combine's dog for twenty years, because the Combine fed me." He opened his hands. "The Combine's stopped feeding me. That's all. I'd like to eat."

"What are you offering?" said Maren.

"Twelve trusties with sticks. The keys to the bridge and the armoury, such as it is. Two old flechette carbines that the Combine forgot it issued. And the warden's office, which means the logs, which means that whatever happens, the Combine won't hear about it from this ship until we want it to." He met her eyes. "And me. Who knows where everything is."

Maren looked at him. She thought of Lusine's voice on channel four. *I told someone.* She thought of Quist across Moberly's desk, turning his coffee cup: *I'd look at who on this ship would have wanted the Combine to be grateful to them.* She thought of the form on Hale's slate, already filled in, in his neat hand, before she ever came down the gallery ladder.

She thought of six marines with guns.

"What's Schedule H?" she said.

Hale blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"In the dispatch. Paragraph five. *Contingency authority, Schedule H, confirmed.* What's Schedule H?"

"I've no idea," said Hale. "Factors' business. I'm a warden." He shrugged. "Contingency means *if things go wrong*. I expect it means the *Admonition*."

He said it easily. Maren watched him say it. She could not tell. She had been watching Hale for twenty years, and she had never once been able to tell what was behind that mild patient face, and she could not tell now.

"We'll think about your offer," she said.

"Don't think too long," said Hale, getting up. "The twenty-second isn't far." He looked round the ring once more, taking his register. "And none of you need worry about me. I can keep a secret. I've kept a great many." He ducked back under the girder into the dark, and they heard him go, slow and heavy, toward his hatch in the keel; and after a moment Bram Kovač's shape at the end of the cable tray went too.

The ring sat in silence.

"Well," said Pirro. "Now we have to."

## A crushed hand

[
  Place: #The infirmary
  Characters: @@Ekene Adu @@Maren Tsolo
  When: 11 November 2391
]

The *Clement*'s crew were not bonded. They were Combine contract hands, hired for the run at Halloran, and they kept to their own ship mostly, and looked at the *Patience*'s bonded the way people look at animals in a pen: without malice, without much interest. But the *Clement*'s medic had been left at Halloran with a fever, and so when one of her cargo hands got his left hand caught in the closing door of a cold-hold hatch, on the morning of the eleventh, they brought him across the collar to Ekene.

Maren heard about it from Ekene that evening. She came to the infirmary after the second dip and found him at the counter, not writing, sitting with his reading lenses in his hand and his face the colour of the counter.

"His name's Brandt," Ekene said. "Yusuf Brandt. He's twenty-three. He's from Halloran, from the yards. He's never been out of the inner lanes before. He thinks Corva is the most boring place he has ever been in his life." He turned the lenses over. "I had to take two fingers. The hatch was a cryo-hatch, the heavy kind, with the frost seals. It crushed them flat. So I gave him a lot of the good painkiller, which the Combine charges at nine marks a dose, and while I was working, he talked."

"What about?"

"About his ship. About the cold hold. About how he's on cold-hold duty this run, which means checking the cryostats every four hours, and how it's the worst duty on the ship because the hold's at sixty below and the gloves are useless. About how there's nothing in the cold hold this run except the usual medical stocks and one other thing." Ekene put the lenses down on the counter, very carefully. "About how the factor comes down to look at the other thing every day. Every single day. With the sergeant. And stands and looks at it. And how the hands call it the egg."

Maren said nothing.

"I asked him what kind of egg," said Ekene. "He laughed. He said a grey one, about this long"—he held his hands apart, the length of a forearm and a half—"in its own cryostat, with its own power and its own alarm, and a stencil on the casing that he'd read so many times on cold-hold duty he could say it in his sleep. And then he said it for me. In his sleep, nearly. The painkiller was very good." Ekene looked at her. "*Sanitation Agent. Pattern H. Factor's authority only.*"

"Pattern H."

"Schedule H. Pattern H." His voice was steady and quite empty. "I've seen that stencil before, Maren. I didn't think I'd ever see it again. I think I came to the end of the lanes so that I wouldn't."

"Tamsin's Rock," said Maren.

He closed his eyes.

"Tell me," she said.

"Not here." He opened them again and looked toward the curtained bay where Yusuf Brandt was asleep, two fingers lighter, dreaming his painkiller dreams of Halloran. "Not tonight. I need to know you've seen it first. I need to know I'm not a frightened old man hearing things in a boy's babbling." He put out his hand and took hers on the counter, hard, the way he never did, his long gentle fingers cold as the cold hold. "If it's there, Maren, then everything you've been planning in the Belly is something else. Everything. Do you understand? You can't take a ship with that thing on the other end of the collar. You can't do anything."

"I can look at it," said Maren.

## The cold hold

[
  Place: #The cold hold
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Odile Arda @@Marta Oyelowo @@Aurelian Quist
  Items: ^The Hush:#The cold hold
  When: 12 November 2391
]

The *Clement* was mated to the *Patience* at the forward collar, a great ring of armoured coupling with its own lock and its own guard, and on the *Clement*'s side of the collar there was a marine on duty all day and all night. There was no going through the collar.

There was going round it.

"It's eleven metres," said Odile, in the suit lock on the forward hull, checking Maren's harness for the third time. "Outside. Along the collar fairing, then down the *Clement*'s spine to her number two maintenance hatch, which opens from outside, because they always do, in case somebody's stuck in a hold. Senna says the hatch is never alarmed. Senna says nobody on the *Clement* thinks anybody's mad enough to go outside."

"Senna's never been outside."

"Senna's never been mad." Odile snapped the last clip and pulled on it. "We're runners, Chief. Eleven metres is nothing. Eleven metres is the length of the drum hall."

"Eleven metres with Gall underneath it."

"Gall's always underneath it." Odile looked at her through her visor, her young fierce face very pale in the lock lights. "You were a runner. You told me. You did four years at twenty kilometres. Before Hesketh made you chief."

"I was younger."

"You were braver," said Odile, and opened the outer door.

It was the second bell of the night shift, the dead time, and the hull was dark. They went out on to it on their hands and knees, clipped to a running line that Odile had rigged the night before, along the dorsal fairing of the collar, where the two ships' hulls met in a seam of scarred armour. Above them, to the left, the black was full of stars and the Lamp, which had grown again, a long pale streamer of light now with its tail split in two. Below them, to the right, was Gall.

Maren had not been outside the *Patience* in nine years. She had forgotten. You forgot, on the gallery, with the board in front of you and the line a green trace on a screen. You forgot that the line was real, that it hung down out of the keel slot into that, into the endless ochre floor of the world, banded and boiling and so close it seemed you could reach down and put your hand in it. You forgot the size of it. Lusine had fallen into it from eleven kilometres. From here, Maren thought, holding the running line with both hands and not looking down, from here you would fall for a long time.

"Don't look at it, Chief," said Odile on the suit channel. "Look at your hands."

"I know."

"I know you know."

They went along the collar fairing and down the *Clement*'s long dull spine, hand over hand, past the green wheel of the Combine painted twenty metres high on her flank and lit from below by Gall-shine, so that it looked like a rusted sun. The number two maintenance hatch was where Senna had said it would be, a square of grey armour set flush in the hull with a recessed handle and a stencil that said NO ENTRY EXCEPT IN EMERGENCY. It was not alarmed. Odile opened it, and they went in feet first, and pulled it to behind them.

The cold hold was very dark and very cold, so cold that the moment the inner door of the maintenance lock opened, Maren's visor frosted at the edges and her suit heaters came on with a whine. They switched their lamps on low.

It was a long low hold, ribbed like the inside of a fish, with racks of sealed medical crates along both sides under a rime of frost, and at the far end, by itself, in a clear space on the deck, a cryostat.

It was smaller than she had imagined. A cylinder of brushed grey steel on a squat base, waist-high, with a ring of status lights round its upper rim glowing a steady green, and a frosted window in its lid. A thick power cable ran from its base to a socket in the hold wall. A second, thinner cable ran from its side to a box on the wall beside the socket, a grey box with a single red light on it and a keyhole.

Maren went and stood beside it. She did not touch it. She bent and looked through the frosted window in the lid.

Inside the cryostat, cradled in a nest of white foam that smoked faintly with cold, lay an egg.

It was grey. Matte grey, the grey of good ceramic, without any shine at all, so that the lamp's light seemed to sink into it rather than lie on it. It was about the length of her forearm and a half, smooth, slightly irregular, like a real egg, an egg from some enormous bird; and it had a seam running round its widest part, very fine, like the join of a cup and a saucer. On its upper surface, stencilled in black, in neat Combine capitals, were three lines.

  SANITATION AGENT
  PATTERN H
  FACTOR'S AUTHORITY ONLY

Beneath the lines, small, the green wheel of Halloran-Vey.

Odile came and looked over her shoulder. Her breath frosted the inside of her visor.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"I don't know," said Maren.

That was true. It was also, she understood even as she said it, the last true thing she would be able to say about it. Because she was looking at it, at its smooth grey indifferent surface in its nest of smoking foam, and the cold of the hold was coming in through her suit, and something in the back of her skull, some old animal thing that had lived through twenty-six years on the line, was telling her very clearly and very quietly to back away.

There was a sound at the hold's forward end. A door.

Odile caught Maren's arm. They killed their lamps together and stood still in the dark, by the cryostat, with its ring of green lights glowing between them; and the forward door of the cold hold slid open and let in a wedge of yellow light, and in the wedge, two figures in heavy cold-weather jackets with the green wheel on the shoulder. A short broad woman with a sidearm. A tall thin man.

"Every day," said Sergeant Oyelowo's voice, flat and bored, echoing down the hold. "You know it's still there, sir. The board says it's still there. The light's green."

"I know," said Quist. "Humour me, Sergeant."

He did not come in. He stood in the doorway in the wedge of light with his hands in the pockets of his jacket, looking down the long dark hold toward the cryostat; looking, it seemed to Maren, straight at her, though he could not have seen her, with her lamp out, behind the glow of the status lights. He stood there for a long moment. His face was in shadow. Then he took one hand out of his pocket and touched something at his throat, a cord, a small shape on it that caught the light for an instant, the size of a key.

"Thank you, Sergeant," he said, and stepped back, and the door slid shut.

They stood in the dark for a long time afterward.

## Tamsin's Rock

[
  Place: #The infirmary
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Ekene Adu
  When: 12 November 2391
]

"They called it the Hush," Ekene said.

It was the fourth bell. The infirmary was dark except for the lamp on the counter, turned so low it was hardly more than a coal. Yusuf Brandt had been sent back to his own ship in the afternoon with his hand in a sling and a note to the factor. The bunks were empty. Ekene sat on the end of the nearest one with Maren beside him, both of them still in their clothes, and the cups of his smoked tea on the deck at their feet, cold.

"Not the Combine. The Combine calls it Sanitation Agent, Pattern H. The men who use it call it the Hush. Because that's what it does." He was looking at the port, where the Lamp hung over Gall's limb. "Tamsin's Rock was an ice-mining station in the Penner lanes. Four thousand bonded. Families, mostly, three generations of them, because the Rock was old. In seventy-nine the price of ice dropped and the Combine cut the air ration, and the Rock struck. They locked out the factor and the wardens and they sat in their warrens and said they would not dig another metre until the ration came back. They were very orderly about it. They kept the plant running. They kept the infirmary open. They sent a petition to Halloran." He paused. "The factor at the Rock had a Schedule H authority."

Maren said nothing.

"It's an aerosol," said Ekene. "That's all it is, in the end. An engineered thing, very small, that likes one sort of tissue and only that sort. Human nerve. Nothing else. Not rats, not plants, not machines. You let it into a habitat's air and the air system does the rest; it goes through every vent in an hour. It doesn't hurt. They told us that, after, the Combine's doctors, as if it were a kindness. You get tired. You get very quiet. People sat down where they were and went to sleep. In their bunks. At their tables. In the crèche." He swallowed. "In six hours there was nobody awake on Tamsin's Rock. In ten there was nobody alive. And the plant ran on. And the lights. And the infirmary's machines went on monitoring the patients in the beds, beeping, for two days, until the power ran down."

"You were there."

"I was a contract medic in the Penner lanes. They sent four of us in, afterward, with a Combine sanitation crew, to certify. That was the word. Certify." He laughed, a terrible small sound. "We had to go through every room. Every bunk. Every child. And write down the time we thought they'd died, so the Combine could close the accounts properly. It took us eleven days."

The Lamp hung in the port.

"The agent stays in the air for a few days and then it settles," Ekene said. "It goes dormant, like a spore. The sanitation crew sealed the Rock and pumped it hard vacuum and baked it, and then they let it sit for a year. And then they shipped in four thousand new bonded from the Kessel yards and opened it up again and started digging." He looked at his hands. "It was turning a profit inside eighteen months. They used it in the Combine's own reports. As an example of what could be achieved."

"Why didn't you stop?" Maren said. "Being a medic for them. After."

"I did. I came here." He turned and looked at her in the dark. "I came to the end of the lanes, Maren, to the most worthless operation the Combine had, so that nothing like that could ever happen around me again. Because who'd waste a Pattern H on Corva? Who'd bother?" His voice cracked. "And now there's one on the other side of the collar. With your factor checking it every day."

"Why bring it?" Maren said. "If they're just leaving. If the Camp's going to die anyway. Why bring that?"

"In case the leaving isn't quiet," said Ekene. "Schedule H. Contingency. You saw the dispatch. *Factor to manage expectations.*" He took her hand. "If we take the ship, Maren, they won't send the *Admonition* to fight us. Why would they? The *Admonition* is expensive. Marines are expensive. They'll just tell the factor to open the box. One cryostat. One key. And then they'll wait a year and send somebody else to dip Gall, if they ever want to dip Gall again. Or not. It doesn't matter to them. That's the point. It doesn't matter."

Maren sat in the dark beside him with his cold hand in hers.

She thought of Quist in the doorway of the cold hold, with his hands in his pockets, looking. Every day. *Humour me, Sergeant.* She thought of the key at his throat.

She thought of the Belly, and the ring of tired faces in the lamplight, and Teska saying *We take her*. She thought of Hale's mild voice in the dark: *I'll be filling sandbags beside you*. She thought of eleven metres of hull with Gall underneath.

She did the arithmetic. It came out differently now. It came out, she realised, sitting there, with the Lamp brightening in the port and the egg asleep in its smoking foam across the collar, the only way it could have come out from the moment she bent over that frosted window and the old animal at the back of her skull told her to back away.

"We don't take the ship first," she said.

Ekene looked at her.

"We take the egg," said Maren Tsolo. "Before anything. Before they know we're coming. We take the egg and the key, and then we see who wants to talk about Sorrel."
