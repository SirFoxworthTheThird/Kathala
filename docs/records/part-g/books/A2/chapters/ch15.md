# The Broker

## Tightbeam

[
  Place: #The Dray
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Cato Marchetti @@Hollis Vey @@Juno Brask
  When: 28 April 2917
]

Juno tells her on the twenty-fifth, in the cockpit, at the change of watch, in a flat clerk's voice, as if reading off a slate. Til listens without turning round. When Juno's finished, Til says, "Thank you," and Juno says, "I'm witnessing. It's my job," and goes aft.

For three days after that Til doesn't do anything at all.

She watches. She flies the *Dray* down the long falling curve toward Gall, and lets Oz plan the sling, and checks the anchors and the cable and the suits, and eats in the galley with Cato Marchetti across the table, and talks to him about the weather on the Lamp and the state of the ice and whether the old man will last; and she watches him. She watches the comms panel in the cargo bay. On the twenty-sixth, at the eleventh bell, the cone icon lights again, for forty seconds. On the twenty-seventh, twice. She doesn't touch it. She doesn't say anything to Oz. She wants to see what he does when he thinks nobody's watching, and what he does, it turns out, is nothing at all, except sit by the old man's cradle with his hands on his knees and send forty seconds of light into the dark astern every night.

On the twenty-eighth, two days from the Lamp, she goes down to the cargo bay at the eleventh bell and is standing beside the comms panel when he comes in.

He stops in the hatch. He doesn't look surprised. He looks, if anything, faintly relieved, like a man who has been carrying something heavy for a long way and has just been told he can put it down.

"Captain," he says.

"Mr Marchetti."

"Your niece saw me. On the twenty-fourth."

"She did."

"I thought she might have." He comes into the bay, slowly, and stops at his courteous distance, and puts his hands in his coat pockets. "She's very quiet. I like that in her. I wasn't sure."

"Who are you talking to?"

"Mother Szabo."

"I know that. I can read a bearing." Til doesn't move from the panel. "Who are you talking to?"

Marchetti considers her for a moment with his quiet attentive face.

"The Office of Supply," he says. "Tarn Directorate. I hold a commission there. Major, as it happens, though I've not worn the uniform in eleven years." He tilts his head slightly. "I was assigned to Mr Vey when he began to buy Concord archives. The Directorate takes an interest in people who buy Concord archives. Most of them are looking for money. Mr Vey was looking for something else, and he was very thorough, and very rich, and the Office decided it would be economical to let him look, and to be beside him when he found it."

"Asset Seven."

"Asset Seven." He nods. "You understand the Reach is coming apart, Captain. You live in it. The Directorate has forty years of emergency powers and a navy it can't pay and six systems that would leave it tomorrow if they had anywhere to go. It needs something that no one can argue with." He says it without any emphasis at all, as if discussing a cargo rate. "A thing that empties a station and leaves it standing. One unit. Kept cold, kept quiet, kept in a vault on Tarn. Nobody would ever need to use it. That's rather the point."

"That's what the Combine said."

"I expect it is." He shrugs slightly. "The Combine was right. It worked for sixty years."

"It worked at Tamsin's Rock."

"It worked best of all at Tamsin's Rock," says Cato Marchetti. "Nobody struck in the Combine's holdings for the next thirty years. That was the point of Tamsin's Rock."

The intercom grille by the hatch clicks.

"Cato," says Hollis Vey's voice. Thin, dry, very tired. "I'm awake."

Marchetti doesn't turn his head. "I know, sir. I left the channel open."

"Eleven years."

"Yes, sir."

"You wiped my mouth." The voice through the grille is shaking, and not with laughter now. "You carried me to the window in the mornings. You read me the Observatory sheets when my eyes went. Eleven years, Cato. You were *mine*."

"I was the Directorate's for twenty, sir," says Marchetti, gently. "Before I was yours. I'm sorry. I've been very fond of you."

The grille is silent.

"What happens now?" says Til.

"Now?" Marchetti takes his hands out of his pockets. "Now nothing very much. Mother Szabo's ship has four turrets and a ram and sixteen of her house men, and the Directorate has paid her a great deal more than Mr Vey ever paid you. She'll come alongside tomorrow and dock. She'll take your niece aboard the *Hymn*, as collateral. Mother Szabo always holds collateral." He says it apologetically. "Then you'll fly us to the Lamp, as agreed. We'll land, as agreed. We'll find the asset, if it's there, and recover it, cold, in its cryostat. Your engineer will keep it cold. And you'll fly it to the *Hymn*, and hand it over, and Mother Szabo will hand your niece back." He spreads his hands a little. "And then the Directorate will clear your debts, Captain. All of them. The *Dray*. Your licence. Your engineer's nine hundred. And your niece's bond, the whole seventeen thousand nine hundred and forty marks of it, in Mother Szabo's stone vault. Burned. I've the authority in writing." He pauses. "And a new ship, if you want one. You'd never need to haul scrap again."

"And if I say no?"

"Then Mother Szabo still holds your niece," says Marchetti. "And sixteen house men. And four turrets." He looks at her almost sadly. "I'd rather you didn't say no, Captain. I asked you a question on the twentieth, in this bay, and I'd very much like to know the answer. But I'd rather not find it out that way."

## Collateral

[
  Place: #The Dray
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Ilona Szabo @@Juno Brask @@Cato Marchetti @@Ozias Mbeki @@Hollis Vey
  Items: ^Juno's bond:@Ilona Szabo
  When: 29 April 2917
]

The *Carrion Hymn* comes alongside at the fourth bell of the twenty-ninth, with Gall filling half the sky and the Lamp a blaze of white and green so close now that its light throws hard shadows across both hulls.

She's three times the *Dray*'s length, long and black and smooth, with her ram bow and her four turrets and the little ledger-and-pen mark on her flank; she slides in on the *Dray*'s starboard side so neatly that Til, at the controls, feels it as nothing more than a faint bump through the deck, and then the clank of grapples, and then the long hiss of a hard dock sealing. She's flown with a good captain. Mother Szabo always pays for the best.

Szabo comes through the lock herself. She's in a quilted coat with a fur collar and good boots and a pair of thin black gloves, and she looks round the *Dray*'s cargo bay, with its ribs and its cable drums and its work-lamps, with exactly the expression she wears in the counting-house when a client opens a strongbox and it's lighter than expected. Four of her house men come through behind her, big quiet men in grey with stun-carbines, and two more after them, and they spread out round the bay without being told.

"Captain Brask," says Szabo pleasantly. "What an ugly ship."

"Mother."

"I've been on the *Hymn* for three weeks. Three weeks, Captain. At my age." She pulls off one glove, finger by finger. "I hope it was worth it."

"Ask your Directorate man."

"I have. He tells me it will be." Szabo smiles at Marchetti, who has come down the bay to stand at a courteous distance, as always. "Major. You look tired."

"I am tired, Mother."

"We'll all rest soon." She turns back to Til. "You've heard the terms."

"I've heard them."

"Generous ones. Very generous. I've never seen the Directorate so generous." She pulls off the other glove. "You'll keep your end, Captain. You'll bring it to me, cold and closed, and I'll give you back what's mine to give, and we'll all go home through the lane and never speak of it, and in a year you'll have a new ship and your niece will be free and you'll wonder what you ever worried about." She folds the gloves together. "Where is she?"

Juno is in the galley hatch.

She's in her new suit, the small one Til bought on Cinderhaven, with her helmet clipped to her shoulder. Oz is behind her, with his big hand on her arm. She looks at Szabo, and at the house men, and at Marchetti, and last at Til, and her face is very still.

"Come along, dear," says Mother Szabo. "You're coming back to the *Hymn* with me."

"I'm witnessing," says Juno. "That's what you sent me for. I'm supposed to witness the recovery."

"Oh, there's been a change of plan." Szabo's voice is kind. "You're collateral now, dear. It's a promotion. You'll have a cabin of your own." She holds out her hand, the bare soft little hand, palm up, the way you hold out a hand for a child to take on a crowded dock. "Come on."

Juno doesn't move. She's looking at Til.

Til has thought about this moment for a day and a night, since Marchetti walked out of the cargo bay. She has thought about the four turrets on the *Hymn*, and the sixteen house men, and the stun-carbines; about the *Dray*'s one cutting lance and her one old flechette pistol, which is in a locker in the cockpit and which Szabo's men will have found within the hour. She has thought about what Marchetti said: *I'd rather not find it out that way.* She has run the arithmetic every way she knows, the way she runs it on every job, and it comes out the same every time.

"Go with her, Juno," she says.

Something happens in Juno's face. It's very small. A flicker, a sort of collapse, there and gone, the way a hull plate flexes once before it holds. And then her face is still again, the fourth-desk-from-the-end face, the counting-house face, without any expression in it at all.

"Of course," she says. "That's what you do."

She takes her arm out of Oz's hand and walks down the bay past Til without looking at her, and past the house men, and puts her hand in Mother Szabo's, and Szabo closes her soft fingers round it and pats it.

"Good girl," says Mother Szabo. "There. That wasn't so hard." She looks up at Til over Juno's shoulder with her rivet eyes. "You see, Captain? I told the Major. I said, she'll cut the line. She always does."

They go through the lock together, the old woman and the girl, and two of the house men behind them. The other four stay. The lock cycles. Til stands in the middle of the cargo bay and listens to it.

From the cradle, through the grille, Hollis Vey says, very quietly: "Captain. You'd give that thing to the Directorate."

"I'd give anything for her."

"Yes," says Hollis. "That's how my family started."

## The weight

[
  Place: #The Dray
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Ozias Mbeki
  When: 29 April 2917
]

In the engine space that night, with the core humming and the hatch shut and two of Szabo's house men asleep in the galley and the other two at the cockpit door with their carbines across their knees, Oz says, "You didn't mean it."

He's sitting on the deck with his back against the coolant manifold, his knees up, his big soft face grey in the work-light. He hasn't said anything since the lock cycled. He's spent five hours in the engine space doing things that didn't need doing.

"Mean what?"

"*Go with her.* You didn't mean it. I saw your face." He looks at her. "She didn't, though. She was looking at you and she didn't see it. Nobody sees your face, Til. You've got it so nobody can."

Til sits down on the deck opposite him. Her legs are very tired.

"She thinks I cut it," she says.

"The line."

"She thinks I cut it on the Harrow and she thinks I cut it today. In the bay. In front of Szabo. *That's what you do.*" Til hears her own voice and it's the weather voice and she can't make it stop. "She's right. That's what I do. It's what I've done every day for eleven years. Every bad job. Every time it comes out wrong. Cut it. Call it. Go home."

"You didn't cut it on the Harrow," says Oz.

Til doesn't say anything.

"I was on the boat," Oz says. "On the other side of the hulk. Minding the winch. I didn't see. I told the girl that and it's true." He folds his delicate hands on his knees. "But I had the winch, Til. I had the line on the drum. I felt it go. I felt it go through my hands on the brake. And I know what a line feels like when it's cut from the near end and what it feels like when it's cut from the far end, and I've known for eleven years which end that one was cut from, and I've never said it, because you never did." He looks at her. "Mags was nearest the hulk."

The core hums. Somewhere aft the air plant coughs.

"Her leg was in the coil," says Til.

She says it so quietly she isn't sure, for a moment, that she's said it aloud. Oz doesn't move.

"The line went round the hulk when it spun. It wound in from the hulk end. And Mags was nearest, and it took her first, and the coil caught her round the leg, round the shin, and pulled tight. She couldn't get it off. I watched her try. She had her hands on it, she was trying to get her boot out, and it was winding in, a metre a second, and I was on the line behind her, between her and the boat, and it was taking me too." Til's hands are flat on the deck. "And I had the knife. It was on my belt. Mine. I had the knife and she didn't, hers had gone, it was on her other side, under the coil. And she was shouting at me. *Cut it, Til. Cut it, cut it, cut it.* And I couldn't. I had my hand on it, I had it out, and I couldn't."

"So she did."

"So she reached back down the line and took it off me." Til's voice is quite steady. "Out of my hand. And cut it. Between her and me. And the hulk took her in, and the line went slack behind her, and I went off backward into the dark with my air on half, and the last thing I saw was her face. She was looking at me. She looked—" Til stops. "She looked annoyed. Like I'd been slow on a job. Like I always was."

Oz says nothing for a long time.

"Then why," he says at last, very gently, "does the whole Ring say you cut it?"

"Because I said I did." Til looks at her hands. "At the inquiry. At Cold Harbour. The Directorate's man asked who cut the line and I said I did. And Szabo was there, buying up Mags's paper, and she heard it, and she told the Ring, and the Ring believed it, because it's the kind of thing that's true about people." She shrugs. "It's true about me. I'd have cut it if she'd been slower. I'd have got there. I always get there."

"That's not why you said it."

"No."

"Then why?"

"Because Juno was six," says Til. "And waiting at the dock. And somebody was going to have to tell her what happened to her mother. And I thought—" She stops. She has never said this, not to anybody, not to herself, not even on the long hauls alone with the core humming. "I thought she could hate me. I'd be there to hate. I'd be alive, and she could hate me as much as she liked, and it would give her something to do with it. But if it was Mags—if she grew up knowing her mother took the knife and cut the line and went in, on purpose, and left her—" Til's voice breaks, finally, just slightly, at the edge. "You can't hate somebody for that, Oz. You can only miss them. For ever. And there's nothing to do with it. It just sits there."

"So you took it."

"It's lighter on me," says Til. "I'm used to carrying things."

Oz sits with his back against the manifold and looks at her with his wet old eyes, and for a long time neither of them says anything.

"She'll ask a third time," Oz says. "She's a Brask."

"She won't. She's done asking."

"Then you'll have to tell her without being asked." He gets up off the deck, slowly, with a hand on the manifold, and stands over her. "On the Lamp. When we've got her back." He holds his hand down to pull her up. "Because we are getting her back, Til. Aren't we. We're not handing that thing to Mother Szabo and her Major."

Til takes his hand and lets him pull her up.

"No," she says. "We're not."

"Good." Oz lets go of her hand. "Then tell me how. Because I've been sitting in here five hours trying to work it out and all I've got is that the *Hymn*'s got to come in close to take the cargo off us. She's got to dock, or near enough. Nobody hands off a cryostat across a kilometre of nothing."

"No."

"And the Lamp's going to be coming apart round us by then."

"Yes."

"And the *Dray*'s a tug." Oz looks at her. "And the *Hymn*'s a ship. And tugs push ships, Til. That's what they're for."

Til looks back at him. In the hum of the core, with the house men at the cockpit door and the Lamp outside blazing on the hull, she feels it for the first time since the Throat: the small cold click at the back of the skull, the one she gets on a hulk when she sees the angle. The way in. The bad job that might, if you're quick and lucky and do everything right and nothing goes wrong at all, come out.

"Tell me about the coil," she says. "On the winch. How much line have we got?"

"Hundred kilometres."

"And how fast can it run out?"

Oz begins, slowly, to smile.
