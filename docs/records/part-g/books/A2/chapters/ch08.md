# Ephemeris

## Under the paint

[
  Place: #The Archive
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Malachy Orr
  Items: ^The Share Plate:#The Archive
  When: 1 July 2654
]

The Archive is the Spine's old cargo deck, four levels down from the nave, and it smells the way I imagine the inside of a very old person's head smells: of dust, and paper, and cold, and something sweetish and faint like rot. There's no proper light. There never has been. Malachy works by lamps, a dozen of them on long arms clamped to his tables, so that the Archive is a great dark cave with little islands of yellow in it, and on every island, books, slates, rolled charts, folders of foil, ledgers bound in plastic gone brown, the whole two hundred and sixty years of Patience written down by people who mostly never expected anyone to read it.

He has the Plate on his biggest table, under three lamps, face down.

"Close the door," he says, when I come in. "And lock it. There's a bar."

I bar the door. When I turn round he's bending over the table with a magnifier strapped round his head on a leather band, like a jeweller, and a tiny brush in one hand and a dish of something in the other that smells sharp enough to make my eyes water.

"Solvent," he says, without looking up. "Old dock solvent. The kind they used to strip hull paint, back when we had hulls to strip. There's a drum of it in the bottom store that's been there since my grandfather's time. It does the job, slowly. Too fast and it would take the scratches with it." He dabs the brush, very lightly, and wipes. "Come and look. Don't breathe on it."

I come and look.

He's cleaned a patch in the top corner of the Plate's back, no bigger than my palm. Inside it, the grey sealant is gone, and the steel underneath is dark and dull and pitted with age, and across it, cut into the metal, there are marks. Not deep. Scratched, rather than cut: thin uncertain lines, as if made with a point dragged slowly and hard, by somebody pressing with all their weight. They're figures. Numbers. A column of them, and beside the column, letters, single letters, like labels.

"Somebody scratched these with an awl," says Malachy. "A rigger's scribe, probably. The kind they used for marking cable. You can see where the point skidded." He touches the air above a line with the end of his brush. "Look at the sevens. They cross their sevens. Nobody's crossed a seven in this city for two hundred years."

"What are they?"

"Numbers."

"Malachy."

"I don't know yet." He straightens up, with a little grunt, and pushes the magnifier up on to his forehead, where it sits like a third eye. "I've done this much in a night. There's the whole back of the Plate still under paint, and the paint's harder in the middle, where it was thicker. A week. Two." He looks at me. "I need you to keep this to yourself, Isaura."

"Who would I tell?"

"Your mother. Your section chief. That rigger woman from South Lock who's been asking about you on the Ninth." He sees my face and smiles thinly. "I hear things. I'm an archivist. Hearing things is most of the job."

"Why does it matter? It's numbers on the back of a plate."

"It matters," says Malachy, "because somebody went to a great deal of trouble to put them there, and then went to a great deal more trouble to make sure nobody could see them." He lays the brush down across the dish. "Think about it. This is the Share Plate. It's the first thing the founders made. It's been hanging in the nave since before there was a nave. Every child in Patience has stood in front of it. And somebody, two hundred and sixty years ago, took it down and cut something into the back of it with an awl, and then painted over it with hull sealant, carefully, edge to edge, so that it would look like nothing but the back of a piece of steel. And hung it up again." He spreads his old spotted hands. "You don't do that with a shopping list."

"Then why not throw it away? If they didn't want it read."

"Because they did want it read." Malachy pulls the magnifier back down over his eye and bends to the Plate again. "Eventually. Just not by whoever was standing in front of it at the time." He dabs with the brush. "Those aren't the same thing, Isaura. Keeping a thing and hiding it. You'll find most of this room is one or the other. The trick is telling which."

## The cells

[
  Place: #The Cells
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Mattias Ebb
  Items: ^Isaura's gauge:@Isaura Venn
  When: 10 July 2654
]

Nine days after South Lock, the Wardens' Office takes me off the Ninth.

I'm called in to see my section chief, a weary shared woman named Paske who has been running the South wardens since before I joined, and who looks at me across her desk with the expression of somebody who has been given a problem she didn't ask for. I'm not being disciplined, she says. Nobody's filed a complaint. The bottle's been paid for out of the Office budget; the militia were very grateful; there's even a commendation, which she pushes across the desk to me on a slip of foil. But there's been talk on the Ninth. About a warden giving away air to a Concord pilot while the Ninth's own children are on the floor. And there have been, Paske says delicately, incidents. A warden on the Ninth North had his ledger taken off him on the stairs and thrown down a shaft. Another had her valve key bent. It would be better for everybody, Paske says, if I were somewhere else for a while.

Somewhere else is the Cells.

The Cells are under the Spine, below the Archive, below the old docks, in what was once the skimmer's keel: a long dim corridor of steel boxes, each with a door and a grille and a meter. In peacetime they hold drunks and thieves and the occasional unshared rioter. Now, eight days into July and twenty-eight days into the siege, they hold a few dozen of each and one Concord lieutenant. Somebody has to read the meters. That's me.

It's quiet work. It's almost restful, after the Ninth. The prisoners are on a fixed allowance set by the Council, a little above the old floor, and nobody pays tithe, so nobody falls into arrears, so nobody has to be cut. I walk the corridor twice a shift with my gauge and read each meter aloud into the ledger and check the seals on the doors, and the prisoners mostly ignore me. Some swear. One old man on the end, a tannery thief from the Fifth, asks me every shift whether the Lamp's still in the sky, and I tell him yes, and he says *good*, and turns over.

The lieutenant's cell is the eleventh.

"Ebb, Mattias," I say to my ledger, on the first morning, outside his grille. "Concord Lane Service. Allowance three-twenty an hour. Draw this cycle, three-oh-five." I look through the grille. "You're breathing light, Lieutenant."

He's sitting on his bunk with his back against the wall and his knees up, in a grey prison smock with the sleeves too short, his cropped fair hair grown out a little into a pale fuzz. The bruising on his face has gone yellow. He looks up when I speak, and I see him recognise me, and something complicated happens in his face.

"I was told to," he says. "In training. *In captivity, conserve.* Every ration. Breathe slow, move slow, sleep a lot." He shrugs. "I'm very good at sleeping."

"You don't have to conserve here. It's your allowance. It doesn't go back in the tank if you don't use it."

"Doesn't it?"

"No. It's just wasted."

He thinks about this with a sort of grave interest. "That's very inefficient."

"Welcome to Patience," I say, and go on to the twelfth.

He's there on the second day, and the third, and every day after, and after the third day we talk. Not much. A few minutes, across the grille, while I take the reading and check the seal. It isn't permitted, exactly. It isn't forbidden either; nobody's thought to forbid it, because nobody's imagined a warden would want to. I'm not sure I want to. But he's the only person in the city who knows less about it than I do, and there's a strange relief in that.

His name is Mattias. He's twenty-four. He comes from a world called Corran, three lanes in, an old Concord holding where everybody works in the canneries. His father owed the Concord a great deal of money for something to do with a fishing boat that sank, and when his father died the debt came down to Mattias, and the Concord, which is efficient, offered him the choice of the canneries for forty years or the Lane Service for twelve. He chose the Lane Service. He's done four years of the twelve. He flies landers. He was flying a lander over Kettle's south ice on the thirtieth of June, on a probe, because his captain wanted to see whether the city had any guns, and the city did.

"That's what it's like everywhere," he says, on the fifth day, when I tell him about the floor. "The Concord. The tithe, the floor. They don't call it that. They call it *allocation*. But it's the same." He looks at me through the grille with his frightened soldier's eyes, which are, I've decided, not frightened at all, only very tired. "You'd think you'd be different here. You threw the Company out. Didn't you? You bought your own air. That's what they told us in the briefings. *The occupiers claim to have purchased the claim.* And then you went and did the same thing to each other."

"We didn't do anything. I wasn't born."

"No. Nor was I." He leans his head back against the wall. "Nobody's ever born when it happens. Have you noticed? It's always already happened."

On the sixth day he asks me why I gave him the bottle.

"It's my job," I say.

"It isn't, though. Is it? Your job's taking it away." He says it without any edge. "The militia officer told me. On the stretcher, when he thought I couldn't hear. He said, *that's the warden from the Ninth, she's cut more air than the plague*. He was laughing."

I look at the meter. Three-oh-eight.

"I don't know," I say, which is the truth.

"Well," says Mattias Ebb, after a while. "Thank you anyway. Whatever it was."

## Numbers

[
  Place: #The Archive
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Malachy Orr @@Lucan Strand
  Items: ^The Share Plate:#The Archive
  When: same day
]

That afternoon Malachy sends for me again, and when I get to the Archive, the First Shareholder is there.

He's standing at the end of Malachy's big table with his hands behind his back, looking down at the Plate under its lamps, and he doesn't turn round when I come in. Two of his stewards wait by the door. Malachy, at the table, gives me a look over the top of his magnifier that I've known since I was eight years old, the look that means *say nothing, you'll only make it worse*.

Ten days of solvent have cleared a long strip down the left side of the Plate's back, about the width of my hand, from the top almost to the bottom. In the strip, the scratched figures go down in a column, with their letters beside them. Six lines. Below the six lines, a gap, and then more scratches, different, less regular, still half under the paint. The rest of the Plate is grey.

"Come in, warden," says Lucan Strand. "The Archivist tells me you were the first to see this. When it fell."

"Yes, First Shareholder."

"And you've said nothing to anyone."

"No, First Shareholder."

"Good." He turns his head and looks at me, and smiles a little, his tired silver smile. "Brother Malachy has been telling me what he thinks it is. I'd like you to hear it. Since you were there at the beginning." He nods to the old man. "Go on, Brother."

Malachy straightens up. He doesn't push the magnifier up this time; he takes it off altogether and lays it on the table, which with Malachy means that he's about to say something he's sure of.

"They're orbital elements," he says. "Six of them. Semi-major axis, eccentricity, inclination, the longitude of the node, the argument of perihelion, and a time." He touches the air above each line with his brush. "That's how dippers wrote down an orbit. They had to know about orbits to do their work, much better than we do; they lived in them. These are in the old dippers' notation, with the old dippers' units. Whoever scratched this knew exactly what they were doing."

"An orbit of what?" says Strand.

"Something long," says Malachy. "And very eccentric. Something that goes a very long way out and comes back." He purses his lips. "I'd need to run the figures to be sure, and I can't run them yet. There's a time on the last line, the time of perihelion, and it's under a flaw in the steel. Somebody's awl slipped."

Strand is quiet for a moment.

"And the rest?" He nods at the half-cleared scratches below the gap. "There's more. Writing?"

"Letters, yes. Words, I think. I haven't got to them." Malachy picks up his magnifier again and turns it over in his fingers. "Another week. Perhaps two. The paint's very hard in the middle."

"Faster, Brother."

"If I go faster I'll take the words off with the paint."

"Then don't go faster," says Strand mildly. "But don't go slower." He comes round the end of the table and looks down at the strip of scratched figures for a long moment, and I see his face in the lamplight and I see something in it I've never seen in it before: not the warmth he wears in the nave or the patience he wears on the speakers, but hunger, plain and sharp, like a man looking at a meal.

"The founders wrote this," he says softly. "On the Plate. And then they hid it."

"Somebody did," says Malachy.

"The founders," Strand says again, as if correcting him. He looks up. "Do you know what reserve stood at this morning, Brother? Fifty-two days. Grieve's slugs took out the third intake on the far ice last night. Fifty-two. A month ago it was ninety-four." He's not raising his voice; he never raises his voice. "The Council meets tomorrow. If the reserve falls below twenty days, we will have to consider measures. Measures nobody in this city wants to consider. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you," says Malachy.

"Then you understand why I want to know what the founders thought was worth hiding." Strand straightens his coat. "Nobody sees this but the three of us. Not the Council. Not my son. When you have the words, you send for me first. Before anyone." He turns to me. "And you, warden. Not a word. Not to your mother. Not to the Ninth. If this city ever needed its founders, it needs them now, and I won't have them turned into a Lamper rumour on the stairs." He smiles. "Do you understand?"

"Yes, First Shareholder."

"Good," he says, and goes out with his stewards.

When the door's barred behind him, Malachy sits down heavily on his stool and puts his face in his hands. He sits like that for a long time.

"*Measures*," he says at last, through his fingers.

"What measures?"

He takes his hands away and looks at me. "You've been a warden twelve years, Isaura," he says. "You know what a city does when it runs short of air. You've done it on a small scale every day of your working life. Think about it on a large one."

I think about it.

I think about the floor, and the doors on the Ninth, and the grandmother under her three blankets with her hands folded. I think about a hundred and seventy thousand unshared and twelve thousand shared, and a reserve going down like a needle on a meter. And I understand what he means, all at once, the way you understand the cold when you step out of a lock, and I sit down too, on the bench by the door, and for a while neither of us says anything at all.

## What's ours

[
  Place: #The Cells
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Mattias Ebb
  When: same day
]

On the last round of my shift, at the tenth bell, I stop at the eleventh cell longer than I should.

The lieutenant's asleep, or pretending. He's lying on his back on the bunk with his arm over his eyes. I read his meter—three-one-nine, he's stopped conserving—and log it, and check the seal, and then I stand at the grille without moving on.

"You never told me," I say. "Why you came."

He doesn't move his arm. "I told you. I was flying a probe."

"Not you. The Concord. Thirty-eight ships, all the way out here to the end of the lanes, for a moon nobody's wanted in two hundred years. For a claim nobody's tried to collect since before your great-grandfather was born." The corridor's dim and cold and quiet, the tannery thief snoring at the far end. "Grieve says the Concord holds the Combine's title. Fine. It's held it for a century. Why now?"

Mattias lies still for a while. Then he takes his arm off his eyes and turns his head on the thin pillow and looks at me through the grille.

"For what's ours," he says. "That's what they told us. *To recover the Concord's property.*"

"The city."

"The city. The claim. The accounts." He hesitates. "And something else."

"What?"

"I don't know." He sits up slowly, wincing at his ribs, and swings his feet down to the floor and sits on the edge of the bunk with his hands between his knees. "I'm a lieutenant. They don't tell lieutenants things. But before we came through the Throat they briefed every lander crew in the fleet, all of us together, in the big bay on the *Steadfast*, and there was an officer from the Commodore's staff there, a commander I'd never seen before, and at the end of the briefing, after the guns and the pickets and the rules about civilians, he said there was one more thing." He looks at his hands. "He said the Combine had lost something here. A long time ago. An item of Company property, never recovered. He said the Concord had reason to believe it was still in the system. He said if any of us, on any probe or landing, came across anything that looked like old Company equipment, anything at all, with the Combine's mark on it, we were to report it and not touch it." He looks up at me. "And then he said, *and if you find it, you are not to open it. Under any circumstances. Not to look inside. Not to break the seal.* And then he said, *you'll know it if you see it.* And then he left."

"What does that mean?"

"I've thought about it a lot," says Mattias Ebb. "In here. I've had time." He's quiet for a moment. "I think it means they're not afraid of you, warden. Not of your guns or your city or your Council. I think they're afraid of something your founders had." He lies back down on the bunk and puts his arm over his eyes again. "Goodnight."

I stand at the grille a while longer. Then I go on down the corridor to the end, where the tannery thief is snoring, and read his meter, and log it, and he wakes up and asks me if the Lamp's still in the sky.

"Yes," I say.

"Good," he says, and turns over.
