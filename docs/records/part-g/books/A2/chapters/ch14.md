# The Rime

## Twenty days

[
  Place: #Patience
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn
  When: 31 July 2654
]

The reserve board on Fourth Central says TWENTY at the start of the morning shift on the thirty-first of July, and by the middle of it, it says nothing at all, because somebody has smashed it.

I'm in the Cells when the order comes. I hear it before I see it: every speaker in the Spine clicking at once, the dry knuckle-on-a-table sound I haven't heard since the night of Lampfast, and then a voice that isn't Lucan's. A steward's voice, reading. *By order of the Council of Shareholders, the provision for relocation adopted on the twentieth of July is now in effect. All air-wardens to report to their section offices. All residents of the tiers listed below to prepare for relocation to the surface shelters. Residents may take one bag. Relocation will proceed tier by tier. Wardens will direct you. Please remain calm. This is for the common good.* Then the list. The Ninth Tier, North and South. The Eighth, upper levels. Parts of the Seventh. *Please remain calm.* Then it starts again.

The night before, a slug came down on the main air plant on the far ice. Not an intake. The plant. I felt it in my bunk on the Fourth, the long deep ring of it, and lay awake listening to the city's fans change their note, everywhere, all at once, as if the whole of Patience had caught its breath. The reserve went from twenty-six to twenty in one night.

At the end of the corridor the tannery thief is sitting up on his bunk.

"Is the Lamp still in the sky?" he says.

"Yes."

"Good," he says, and lies back down. He isn't on the list. The Cells aren't. Prisoners are the Council's property, like Mattias, and the Council doesn't relocate its property to the surface.

I'm not on the list either. The Fourth isn't. I stand in the Cells corridor with my ledger on my wrist and my key on my belt and listen to the steward reading the tiers, and I think: *the Third Tier, on Strand air.* My mother is on the Third Tier. A steward came to my door on the twentieth and told me so, very courteously, at the end of the bell. I've been almost sure I believed him for eleven days.

I go to the eleventh cell. Mattias is standing at the grille.

"The launch is tomorrow," he says. "First bell. They told me this morning. The Council's men." He looks at me. "Where are you going?"

"The Third Tier."

"Why?"

"To see my mother," I say, and I hear my own voice and understand, in the hearing of it, that I already know.

The Third Tier is shared. It's warm, and the corridors are wide and lit with real lamps, and the doors have brass plates on them with family names, and the air tastes of nothing at all, the way air should. I've been up here a dozen times in my life, on errands. The Strand household's guest rooms are at the end of the Third Central gallery, behind a door with a steward on it. I show the steward my warden's ledger and ask for Ama Venn.

He looks at a list. He looks at it for a long time, running his finger down it, frowning, the way people frown when they want you to see they're looking.

"No Venn," he says.

"She was moved here on the twentieth. From the Ninth South. By order of the First Shareholder."

"No Venn," he says again. He looks up. He's young, and shared, and he doesn't know anything, and I can see that he doesn't, and that he's sorry, a little, in the way you're sorry about weather. "I've got no orders about a Venn. Nobody's been moved up here from the Ninth. Nobody at all."

I don't remember the stairs. I remember the lift at Fourth Central being stopped, with a warden I knew standing in front of it saying *no movement between tiers*, and pushing past him, and him letting me, because he saw my face. I remember the stairs from the Fifth to the Sixth, which are the old tannery stairs, steep and wet, and going up them three at a time with the stink of the vats in my throat. I remember the Seventh, where the people on the list were already coming out of their doors with their one bag each, standing in the corridors in their coats, not moving yet, looking at each other.

I remember the Ninth.

## The South Lock queue

[
  Place: #South Lock
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Ama Venn @@Pilar Ocampo @@Dasha Imre:remote
  Items: ^Isaura's gauge:@Ama Venn
  When: same day
]

They're already moving the Ninth South by the time I get there.

The wardens are doing it. My wardens. Ostrander, and Bek, and the Haldane twins, and old Pruitt who trained me, and three dozen others from the North and South sections, in their grey coats with their keys on their belts, walking down the long cold corridors of the Ninth door by door. Shutting the valves. Not to the floor. Off. A quarter-turn past the red, to the stop, the way you'd shut off a household that had died or moved away. Each household's fan coughs and sighs and stops, and the people inside come out with their bags, because there is nothing else to do, into a corridor where the air is already going stale and cold, and the militia in their blue armbands point them toward the stairs. Toward the South Lock ramp. Toward the surface.

Nobody's running. That's what I'll remember most. There's no riot. There's a woman screaming somewhere on the North side, on and on, but here on the South it's very quiet. People walk. They carry their bags and their children and their old ones, and they walk down the corridor toward the stairs in a long slow line, and the wardens shut the valves behind them, and the fans stop one by one, like a row of lamps going out.

Somewhere in the line, the Lampers are singing. Not loudly. An old tune, slow, in a minor key; a dipper's tune, my mother says, from before the city. The same four lines, over and over. *The Lamp comes round, the Lamp comes round. It takes the dead out and brings them back. The Lamp comes round, the debt comes due. It takes the dead out and brings them back.*

The eleventh door on the South is shut. Its fan is silent. I don't look at it.

I find my mother at the bottom of the ramp, in South Lock.

The lock's full. Thousands of people, packed into the long ice chamber under its harsh lamps, with their bags at their feet, waiting for the outer doors to cycle; the Rime is a kilometre out across the surface, and they're taking people out on the big crawlers, a hundred at a time, to the old domes. The surface suits are long gone from their racks. There aren't enough suits in the city for forty thousand people. They'll go out in the crawlers' pressurised beds, and walk from the crawlers into the domes through the old connector tubes, and the Council will supply the domes with what air can be spared.

My mother is sitting on her filter bag against the lock wall, under the racks where the suits used to hang. She's wearing her old grey coat, the one with the patched elbows, and she's got Pilar Ocampo beside her, with her cracked grey gloves, leaning against her arm. Pilar's mother is there too, and her father, and the two little ones, all of them sitting in a row against the wall like birds on a wire.

My mother looks up and sees me, and smiles.

"Isa," she says. "There you are."

"Mama." I'm on my knees in front of her. I don't remember getting there. "Mama, they said you'd been moved. On the twentieth. The steward came to my door, he said the Third Tier, Strand air, he said—"

"Nobody came for me, love." She says it gently, the way you'd tell a child a story it already knows the end of. "I'd have gone if they had. I'd have gone, for you. But nobody came."

"Lucan gave me his word."

"I'm sure he did." My mother puts her hand on my face. Her fingers are cold and rough and smell of soot, the way they've smelled all my life. "He's a man who gives his word very easily, Isa. It's the keeping he's not so good at."

"Come on." I take her arm. "Come on. Get up. I'm a warden. I've got surface rating. I'll take you down to the Fourth, to my room, I'll—"

"No movement between tiers," says a militiaman behind me. He's very young. He has a rifle and a blue armband and he looks as if he wants to cry. "Warden, I'm sorry. Nobody listed leaves the lock except by the crawlers. Council's order."

"She's my mother."

"I'm sorry."

"She's sixty-three. She's got the cough. She won't last a week in the Rime."

"I'm *sorry*," he says, and doesn't move.

"Isa." My mother's hand on my arm. "Isa, stop."

I stop. I'm on my knees on the wet ice of the lock floor with my hands on her arm, and she's looking at me, and her face is very calm.

"You're going on the Lamp," she says. "Aren't you. In the morning."

"How do you know?"

"Dasha Imre told me. She came by last night. She's going too; the Strand boy picked her." My mother smiles. "Everybody on the Ninth knows, Isa. Everybody knows everything on the Ninth. That's the only thing we've got that the Shares haven't."

"I'm not going. I'm staying with you."

"You are going."

"Mama—"

"You're going," says my mother, "to the Lamp. And you're going to find out what's on it. And if it's a gift, you're going to bring it home, and if it's a debt, you're going to pay it. And you're going to stop that man getting his hands on whatever it is." She squeezes my arm. "That's what you're going to do, Isaura Venn. Because you're the only one of us who can."

Behind her, against the wall, Pilar Ocampo is watching us with her coin-flat face.

"I can't leave you here."

"I'm not here," says my mother. "I'm going up to the Rime. With the Ocampos, and the Bekkers from the ninth door, and old Sofi, and the Lampers. Somebody's got to mend their filters up there. You can't breathe through soot." She pats the bag under her. "I've got my picks. I've got my lamp. I've done it for forty years. I can do it for twenty days." She smiles. "The dipper fleet'll fly again. The siege'll end. Lucan says it's a temporary measure. He gave his word."

That's when I start to cry. I haven't cried since my father went. I kneel there on the ice in front of my mother with the Lampers singing on the ramp and the crawlers grinding in through the outer doors, and I cry the way Pilar didn't cry when her grandmother died, all of it at once, and my mother holds my face in her sooty hands and lets me.

When I've stopped, I take my coat off, my warden's coat, which is good wool, lined, the best coat I've ever owned, and put it round her shoulders over the patched grey one. Then I take my gauge off my belt, my flow gauge and valve key on their ring, the warden's tools I've carried for twelve years, and put them in her lap.

"What's this for?"

"The domes have valves," I say. "Old ones. Nobody's turned them in a century. You'll need a key."

My mother looks down at the gauge in her lap for a long moment. Then she closes her hand round it.

"Go on, girl," she says. "Go and get the Lamp."

## The Wick

[
  Place: #The Wick
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Corin Strand @@Dasha Imre @@Mattias Ebb @@Malachy Orr
  Items: ^The Share Plate:@Isaura Venn
  When: 1 August 2654
]

The skiff is called the *Wick*, and she is the smallest thing I have ever seen that was meant to go into the sky.

She sits on her cradle at the bottom of the Spine's old dock shaft, in the dark under the nave, a hundred and twenty metres of vertical ice tunnel going up above her to the surface, where the old skimmer's docking ring used to open on to the stars and where the founders' engineers, two centuries ago, drilled a shaft for the dipper fleet to come and go. She's a dipper skiff: a short blunt-nosed hull like a seed, with a heat shield over her belly scarred black from a hundred runs into Gall's clouds, and stubby fins, and a single big drive bell at the back. Four seats in a cabin the size of a wardrobe. A cargo well behind them, the size of a coffin.

"She'll do," says Corin Strand, slapping her flank. He's in his flight suit, with his helmet under his arm, and he looks happier than I've ever seen anybody look. "She's the fastest thing in the fleet. I've had her eight years. She's been round the giant at sixty kilometres a second and come back with her paint on."

"And the Lamp?"

"The Lamp's just a bigger giant," says Corin, and grins.

Dasha Imre is already in the cabin, stowing gear. She's in a rigger's surface suit, very old, very patched, with comet-tails painted up both sleeves; she looks out of the hatch at me as I come up the cradle ladder and nods, without any surprise at all.

"Warden," she says. "Told you it gets to be a habit."

Mattias comes up last, between two militiamen. He's in a borrowed flight suit, too big for him, and his hands are cuffed in front of him with a length of chain, and his face, still yellowish from the floor, is set and pale. The militiamen walk him to the ladder and stop. Corin takes the chain off him himself, with a key from his pocket, and hands it back to the militiamen.

"He can't navigate with his hands tied," Corin says. "And if he tries anything, we're in a box the size of a cupboard with three people who'll kill him. He knows that." He looks at Mattias. "Don't you?"

"Yes," says Mattias.

"Good man." Corin jerks his head at the hatch. "In you go."

Malachy has come down to see me off. He's in his slippers again, and his old grey coat, and he's carrying the Plate himself, wrapped in a grey blanket, held against his chest in both arms like a child, though it must weigh as much as he can lift. He won't let the stewards take it. He walks it across the dock floor to the foot of the cradle ladder and stops there, breathing hard, and holds it out to me.

"Take it," he says. "Take it up. Clean it as you go. The solvent's in the kit, and the brushes, and my notes, everything I've got." His hands are shaking. "There's more in the middle now. I got the middle started. It's words, Isaura. I can see the shape of them. I haven't had time to read them."

I take the Plate. It's very heavy. I hold it against me in its blanket and look at him.

"Read it before he does," says Malachy, very low. He doesn't look at Corin. "Whatever it says. Read it first."

"I will."

"And Isaura." He puts one spotted old hand on my cheek, the way my mother did, very lightly. "Come back."

They fill the dock shaft with smoke at the first bell, from canisters on the surface, so that the Concord's watchers will see nothing come out but a plume like a vent letting go. That's Corin's idea. We sit strapped into our four seats in the wardrobe of a cabin, Corin and Mattias in front, Dasha and me behind, with the Plate in its blanket in the cargo well behind my seat and my hand reaching back to touch it. I can see nothing out of the little port beside me but the black ice of the shaft wall, and then the smoke.

"Hold on to something," says Corin. "This is the fun bit."

The *Wick*'s drive lights.

It's like being hit by a building. I've never flown. I've never been off Kettle; nobody unshared has. The noise comes up through the seat into my spine and my teeth, a roar so big it's not a sound any more, and something sits on my chest and pushes, pushes me down into the seat, harder and harder, so that I can't lift my hand from the armrest, can't turn my head, can't breathe. The ice wall outside the port goes from black to a grey blur. Then we're out of the shaft and out of the smoke and there's nothing outside the port at all, nothing, and then, slowly, sliding into the corner of it as we turn, a curve of ochre and purple that fills the whole world.

Gall.

"Picket at two-ten," says Mattias. His voice is quite steady. "High. That's the *Vigilant*, she's a frigate, she sits on the Kettle-Gall line. She'll see us when we clear the smoke."

"How long?"

"She's seen us," says Mattias.

The *Wick* turns over. I feel it in my stomach, a long slow roll, and Gall swings past the port and is overhead, and then under us again. Corin is flying with both hands, whistling through his teeth.

"She's coming round," says Mattias. "She'll have a shot at us in four minutes. Go low. Go into the giant. Her captain's a careful man; he won't follow a skiff into the cloud-tops."

"How do you know?"

"I flew landers off her for a year," says Mattias. "He wouldn't let us go below eight hundred kilometres. He said the giant had eaten enough ships."

Corin laughs. "Then he's never had fun," he says, and puts the *Wick*'s nose down.

I don't remember all of what happens next. I remember Gall coming up under us, filling the port, closer and closer, until I can see the storms in the bands, and then the bands themselves, and then the tops of clouds, real clouds, towering, white and ochre and lit from underneath by lightning. I remember the *Wick* starting to shake, a long fine buzzing tremor through the whole hull, and Dasha beside me saying *that's the shield, that's just the shield, she's kissing it*, very calm, as if talking to a frightened animal. I remember something going past the port, very fast, a streak of white light, and then another, and Mattias saying *she's firing, she's firing short, she can't get the angle*, and Corin saying nothing at all, whistling. I remember the push in my chest getting worse and worse, until the edges of the world went grey, and then a long time of grey.

I remember coming back. The push gone. The roar gone. A silence so complete my ears ring. And the port beside me full of black, and stars, and, swinging slowly into it from the left, so bright I have to shut my eyes against it, the Lamp.

"Through," says Corin Strand softly. "We're through. That's the sling." He lets go of the controls for the first time and flexes his hands. "My great-grandfather would have wet himself."

"We're on the Lamp's path?"

"We're on the Lamp's path," says Mattias. He's bent over the little nav screen, his fair head close to it, reading figures. "Six days to match. It'll be close." He looks round at me, over the back of his seat. "The *Vigilant*'s turned back. She won't follow us out here. Nobody will. There's nothing out here but the Lamp."

## The Tether-Woman

[
  Place: #The Wick
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Dasha Imre @@Mattias Ebb @@Corin Strand
  Items: ^The Share Plate:@Isaura Venn
  When: 2 August 2654
]

On the second night, Dasha tells the story.

We're all awake. You can't really sleep in the *Wick*; there's nowhere to lie down, and the seats only tilt so far, and the cabin's so small that every time somebody shifts their weight everybody else feels it. Corin's dozing at the controls with one hand on the stick. Mattias is curled sideways in his seat with his eyes open. I've got the Plate on my knees, unwrapped, face down, with the little work-lamp from the kit clipped to the bulkhead over it, and Malachy's brushes and his dish of solvent wedged between my thighs, and I'm cleaning.

It's slow. It's slower than slow. The middle of the Plate's back is where the paint is thickest, and the scratches under it are faint, and the solvent's so strong the fumes make my eyes stream even with the cabin fans on full. I work a patch the size of my thumbnail at a time. Dab. Wait. Wipe. Look. Dab.

"My gran told it to me," says Dasha, out of nowhere, in the dark beside me. "When I was little. On the Ninth North. She said her gran told it to her."

"Told what?"

"About the Tether-Woman."

I keep cleaning. Dab. Wait.

"In the founding year," says Dasha, "when the Lamp was in the sky, there was a woman on the old ship who ran the rope. The rope they used to let down into the giant. Forty kilometres of it. She was the chief of it. The tether-chief." Her voice is low and slow, a voice for telling things to children in the dark. "And the founders had a debt. A big one. Something they'd done to get their air, or something that had been done to them, my gran didn't know which. Something too heavy to keep in the city. And the Tether-Woman said, *I'll carry it.* And she put on her coat, her tether-chief's coat, with her name on the back, and she took her line knife, and she got in a boat, and they threw her at the Lamp with the rope. The way you'd throw a stone with a sling." Dasha's hand moves in the dark, a slow circle. "And she went out with it. On the Lamp. Into the cold. With the debt. So the city wouldn't have to carry it."

"And then?"

"And then she's there," says Dasha. "Waiting. With the debt. Two hundred and sixty years. And when the Lamp comes round, she comes round with it. And when it comes round, the debt comes due." She laughs softly. "That's what my gran said. The Lamp takes the dead out and brings them back. She's one of the dead. She'll be sitting there on the ice in her coat, waiting to see who comes."

Mattias, curled in his seat, says, "That's a ghost story."

"It's a Lamper story. Same thing." Dasha shrugs in the dark. "The Shares tell you the Purchase. *We bought our air.* We tell the Tether-Woman. One of them's true."

"What was her name?" I say.

"Nobody knows. My gran said she didn't have one any more. She said the Tether-Woman gave her name away, with everything else, when she went out." Dasha turns her head and looks at the Plate on my knees, at the grey back of it under the work-lamp. "Maybe it's on there. On the front. One of the three hundred."

"The first line's blank."

"Is it," says Dasha, and says nothing else.

I go on cleaning. Dab. Wait. Wipe. My eyes are streaming. The *Wick* hums. Outside the port the Lamp is enormous now, so close and so bright that the cabin is full of its light without the work-lamp, a faint green glow on everything, on Dasha's tattooed sleeves and Mattias's open eyes and the back of Corin's sleeping head.

Under my brush, in the middle of the Plate, the paint comes away from a line of letters.

They're scratched like the numbers, with an awl, hard and shaky, pressing. I can see the skid in a T. I bend close. I have to tilt the Plate to the lamp to read them, and my hands are shaking so much I nearly drop the brush, and the letters are faint and half-gone and there aren't many of them, only a few words in the middle of a line whose beginning and end are still under the grey; but I can read them. I read them three times.

*—it sleeps in the cold—*

I don't say anything. I sit with the Plate on my knees and look at the words.

Beside me, Dasha's breathing has gone slow; she's asleep, or nearly. In front, Corin's asleep at the stick. But Mattias isn't. Mattias is curled sideways in his seat with his eyes open in the green light of the Lamp, looking at me, looking at the Plate; and I see him read it, upside down, over the back of his seat, the way I've learned to read meters upside down through frosted glass. I see his lips move. *It sleeps in the cold.*

His face goes very still.

Then he turns his head away and looks out of his port at the Lamp, and doesn't say anything at all; and I put the brush down in the dish and sit there in the hum of the little skiff with my hands flat on the cold steel of the founders' Plate, and think of a grey egg on a laminated card, in a cryostat, in the cold, and of my mother in a dome on the Rime with my coat round her shoulders and my key in her lap, mending filters for people who can't breathe.
