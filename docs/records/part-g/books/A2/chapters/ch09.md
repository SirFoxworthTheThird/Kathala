# Old Iron

## Platform Four

[
  Place: #Platform Four
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Juno Brask @@Ozias Mbeki @@Hollis Vey:phone
  When: 18 April 2917
]

They find the platform on the eighteenth, on the way in, because Oz is looking for it.

"My mother said there were platforms," he says, in the cockpit, with his big soft face close to the scope. "Fuel depots. Where the skimmers topped up between dips. The Combine left them in high orbit when it went. She said her grandmother used to say you could see them from the ice at night, like little moons." He adjusts the gain. "Two hundred years and more. Most of them'll have come down. But high orbit's high orbit."

"We don't need fuel."

"We need reaction mass for the sling, and we need it cheap. And it's old iron, Til." He grins without turning round. "When did you ever pass old iron?"

There it is, on the third sweep: a small hard return in a long slow orbit far above Gall's equator, tumbling very slowly end over end. Til brings the *Dray* up to it in four hours, carefully, the way you come up on any dead thing in the black, and holds her two hundred metres off while Oz puts the lamps on it.

It's ugly. It's uglier than the *Dray*, which takes some doing. A long girder spine with tanks clustered along it like grapes on a stalk, most of them burst open now and peeled back in grey petals; a docking ring at one end, crusted with something white; a squat block of habitat modules at the other, windowless, with a faded number painted on its flank in characters two metres high. A 4. And beside the 4, just visible under five centuries of micrometeorite pitting, a wheel. A green wheel with eight spokes.

"That's us," says Hollis Vey's voice over the intercom, very quietly. He's watching from the cradle through the cabin camera. "That's the family mark."

"It's a fuel depot, Mr Vey."

"Everything we owned had that on it. Everything and everyone." A pause. "Go and look, Captain. Please. Take a camera. I'll pay for the time."

They go across in suits, the three of them, on a line: Til first, Juno in the middle, Oz at the back with the lamps. Til didn't ask Juno to come. Juno asked. She came up to the suit lock while Til and Oz were checking seals and stood in the hatch with her new suit already on, helmet in her hands, and said "I'm witnessing," as if daring anybody to argue, and nobody did.

The habitat block's hatch is jammed. Oz cuts it. Inside, it's black and airless and so cold their suit heaters whine, and their lamps go down a narrow corridor lined with lockers and cable runs and frost, white frost thick as fur on every surface, so that the whole place looks like the inside of a mouth that hasn't been opened for a long time.

It's a small place. A dozen people lived here once, Oz thinks, maybe fifteen, on rotation from the skimmers: depot hands, the people who ran the hoses and kept the tanks from boiling. There's a bunk room with fifteen bunks, the bedding still on them, frozen into stiff grey shapes. There's a galley with a table bolted to the deck and eight chairs, and on the table a cup, standing upright, with a skin of ice in the bottom of it gone hard as stone. There's a washroom. There's a duty office, very small, with a desk and a slate screen gone black and a shelf of foil folders.

On the wall of the galley, under the frost, there's writing.

Juno finds it. She puts her glove to the frost and rubs, and it comes away in a soft white shower, and under it the grey paint of the bulkhead is covered in scratched and pencilled marks: names, mostly, scratched with something sharp, dozens of them, one under another. Some have dates beside them. The dates are old, very old, numbers that don't look like dates at all to Til until she reads them twice: 2361, 2374, 2388. Beside some of the names there are little drawings. A tank. A heart. A bottle. Near the bottom, beside a name nobody can read any more, a small round shape with a tail.

And under that, across the whole width of the panel, in capitals somebody took a long time over:

THE LAMP TAKES THE DEAD OUT AND BRINGS THEM BACK

Oz stands in front of it for a long time with his lamp up.

"That's ours," he says at last. "That's the saying. That's the Roundsmen's." His voice is thick in Til's helmet. "My mother's people wrote that. Here. Right here."

"Somebody did," says Til.

"Somebody did." He puts his big glove on the wall beside the words, flat, the way the Roundsmen touch the chalk loops on the chapel walls on Dock Nine. "Five hundred years, Til."

In the duty office, Juno has opened the shelf of folders.

She's standing at the little desk with one of them open under her lamp, and she's not moving. When Til comes in she doesn't look up. The folder's full of foil slips, thin grey strips printed in a cramped old type, frozen together at the edges. She's peeled the top few apart with her glove.

"Accounts," she says.

Til looks over her shoulder. Each slip has a name and a number at the top, and below them, columns. Dates. Codes. Figures. Some are in black, most in red. She can't read the codes. She doesn't need to.

"*Air, standard ration, thirty days,*" Juno reads, in her clerk's voice, very flat. "*Suit maintenance. Medical, two items. Food, standard. Carried forward from previous: four thousand one hundred and six marks.*" She turns the slip over. "*Balance carried to next of kin.*" She peels off another. "This one's a boy. Look. Sixteen. *Account opened, age of majority, carried from parent.* He'd been working one year and he owed six thousand." Another. "This one's dead. It says. *Deceased, Gall. Balance carried.*" Her glove's shaking. "They're all like that. All of them. Every one."

"Juno."

"I do this," says Juno. "This is my job. This is what I do at the counting-house all day. I carry balances." She turns round in the little frozen office with the folder held against her chest, and through her visor her face is wet. "Five hundred years ago, and it's the same. It's exactly the same. The same words. *Balance carried.*" She looks at Til. "Who are they going to carry it to? They're all dead. They've been dead five hundred years. Who's going to pay it?"

Til doesn't have an answer. She stands in the doorway of the duty office with her lamp, and Juno stands there with the folder, and after a moment Juno puts the folder down very carefully on the desk where she found it, open, with its frozen slips, and closes it.

"Leave it," she says. "Leave it here. They can keep it."

Over the intercom, from the cradle, Hollis Vey says nothing at all.

They pump four hundred tonnes of five-century-old reaction mass out of the depot's last unburst tank before they leave. It's dirty and full of ice and Oz swears at it for two hours while he filters it, but it burns. Til takes the *Dray* away from Platform Four slowly, the way she came, and the old girder spine goes on tumbling end over end behind them in its long slow orbit, with its green wheel and its frozen cup and its wall of names.

## The Roche limit

[
  Place: #The Dray
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Ozias Mbeki @@Juno Brask @@Hollis Vey @@Cato Marchetti
  When: 19 April 2917
]

"It's already breaking," says Oz.

He has the Lamp up on the main screen in the cabin, where the cradle is, so that everyone can see: the comet as it is now, from twenty million kilometres, magnified as far as the *Dray*'s old scope will go. It's not a smear any more. It's a body. A long dark lumpy thing like a potato, or a footprint, or two stones stuck together, wrapped in its glowing coma; and from its surface, here and there, thin bright jets of gas spurt out into the sunlight and curl away into the tail. Along one side of it, near the narrow waist where the two lobes meet, there's a line. A dark line. A crack.

"That's new," says Oz. "That wasn't in the Observatory pictures. That's opened up in the last month." He taps the screen. "And there. And there. Little ones. And it's shedding. See that? That's a chunk the size of the Ledger Ring coming off it."

"How long's it got?"

"Depends what you mean by got." Oz brings up another picture, a diagram, curves on a grid. "This is its path. This is Gall. This dashed line round Gall is the limit, the Roche limit; inside it, the giant's pull on the near side of anything is so much stronger than on the far side that the thing just comes apart. Rubble flies apart. Ice flies apart. Moons fly apart." He traces the comet's curve with his finger. "The Lamp crosses inside the line on the thirtieth of April. By the first of May it'll be in pieces. Twenty, thirty, maybe more. They'll go round Gall in a string, like beads, and they'll come in on the second and they'll hit."

"All of them?"

"Most. The big ones." He shrugs. "Some of the small stuff'll be flung out. Some might go round and come back. Some might go anywhere. Gall's moons are in the way. Kettle's in the way. It's a mess, Til. It'll be the most spectacular mess anyone's seen in five hundred years and there'll be nobody watching it but us." He folds his arms. "If we want to land on it, we land on it before it's pieces. The thirtieth. The first at the latest. After that there's nothing to land on, just a gravel cloud falling into a gas giant at sixty kilometres a second."

"And we can get there."

"We can get there." Oz brings up a third picture, a fat curve swinging round Gall. "This is how. We go in close to the giant, really close, cloud-tops close, and let it swing us round, and we light the drive at the bottom of the swing and we come out of it on the Lamp's path with its speed. It's the old way. The dippers' way. My mother called it the sling." He grins. "Nobody's done it in five hundred years and the *Dray*'s not built for it and if I've got the numbers wrong by a hair we'll go into Gall like one of their dead. But it'll work."

"It'll work," says Til.

"I said it'll work."

"You said it like it might not."

"Everything might not," says Oz comfortably. "That's what makes it interesting."

On his pillows, Hollis Vey has been watching the screen without saying anything. His pale eyes are fixed on the crack along the Lamp's waist, the thin dark line.

"So it's true," he says. "The last time."

"The last time," says Oz.

"Then we'd better be quick." Vey closes his eyes. "Thank you, Mr Mbeki. That was very clear."

Juno, sitting on the deck by the cradle with her knees up, hasn't taken her eyes off the diagram: the comet's path, the dashed circle, the little marks that are Gall's moons, sitting right in the way of whatever doesn't fall.

"What happens," she says, "if something's on one of the bits that gets flung out?"

Everybody looks at her.

"Nothing happens," says Til. "It goes off into the dark and nobody sees it again."

"Or it hits a moon," says Juno.

"Or it hits a moon."

"Kettle's in the way," says Juno, looking at the diagram. "He said."

"Kettle's been in the way of a lot of things," says Oz gently. "It's a big ball of ice, love. It's been hit before."

Juno doesn't say anything else. But Til, looking at her, sees her look across the cabin at Hollis Vey, lying with his eyes shut on his pillows, and sees Cato Marchetti, on his crate by the cradle, look at Juno looking.

## A man with a price

[
  Place: #The Dray
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Cato Marchetti @@Juno Brask @@Hollis Vey @@Ozias Mbeki
  When: 20 April 2917
]

Cato Marchetti comes to find her in the cargo bay on the twentieth, while she's checking the anchors.

She hears him coming. That's unusual in itself; he moves so quietly that Oz has taken to ringing a little bell on the galley hatch when he goes through it, as a joke, and Marchetti, who seems to have no sense of humour at all, has taken to ringing it back. He's let her hear him on purpose. He comes down the bay between the cable drums and stands at a courteous distance with his hands in his coat pockets and watches her work.

"Mr Vey's sleeping," he says. "He has bad nights."

"I know. I hear him."

"He's in a good deal of pain. He doesn't let it show. He's had a long time to practise." Marchetti watches her screw a piton into its rack. "He's very taken with your niece."

"Everybody's taken with my niece. She's very takeable."

"She reads to him. Did you know? In the evenings, while you're on watch. He asked her to. She reads him the ship's manuals, because there's nothing else aboard to read, and he listens with his eyes shut as if it were poetry." Marchetti's voice doesn't change. "He asked her yesterday what she'd do if her bond were paid. She said she didn't know. She said nobody had ever asked her."

Til racks the last piton and straightens up. "Is there something you want, Mr Marchetti?"

"I was curious," he says, "about you."

"I'm not that interesting."

"Everyone's interesting, Captain. That's been my experience. Everyone has a price, and the interesting thing about them is what it is." He takes one hand out of his pocket and runs a finger, idly, along the cold rim of a cable drum. "Mother Szabo said yours was the *Dray*. And then, when I met you, I thought it was your niece. And now I'm not sure."

"What's yours?"

He smiles, for the first time since she's known him. It doesn't change his face much; it's a small courteous movement at the corners of the mouth, like a man acknowledging a good move in a game. "Mr Vey pays me very well."

"That's not a price. That's a wage."

"Yes," says Marchetti. "It is." He takes his hand off the drum and puts it back in his pocket. "You went to prison for four years, Captain, for a salvage your sister planned. You came out owing more than you went in. You've hauled scrap for seven years to pay a debt that isn't yours, for a girl who won't speak to you. You came through a raw lane for a dying man's fairy tale. I've met a great many people who'd do anything for money, and I don't think you're one of them, and it's been puzzling me." He tilts his head. "What would you do, Captain, for nothing? That's what I'd like to know."

"Ask me on the first of May," says Til.

"I will," says Marchetti, and goes, and this time she doesn't hear him at all.

Later, in the galley, Oz says, "He's a bad one."

"I know."

"Not bad like Szabo. Szabo's bad the way weather's bad. You know where you are." Oz is peeling a ration bar with his delicate fingers, very slowly, as if it were a fruit. "He's the other kind. The kind that's polite to you right up to the moment."

"I know, Oz."

"And the old man's lying."

"I know that too."

"And the girl's reading to him in the evenings." Oz eats a piece of the bar. "She asked me about the Harrow."

Til's hands go still on her cup.

"Last night," says Oz. "While you were on watch. She came and sat in the engine space, while I was doing the coolant sleeve, and she asked me if I was there. On the Harrow. When it happened." He chews. "I said no. I was on the boat. I was on the boat on the other side of the hulk, minding the winch, and I didn't see. Which is true."

"What else did she ask?"

"She asked me if it was the way Szabo tells it." Oz looks at her across the little galley table, with his bag-of-stones face and his wet old eyes. "I said it wasn't. I said that was all I'd say, and if she wanted the rest she'd have to ask you. And she said she had, and you wouldn't." He finishes the bar. "That's twice she's asked, Til. She won't ask a third time. She's a Brask."

"I know."

"Then tell her."

"It doesn't help her," Til says. "It doesn't help anybody. It just moves it."

"Moves what?"

"The weight," says Til, and gets up and goes forward to the cockpit.

She has the watch from the eighth bell. Gall fills the lower half of the glass now, enormous, close enough to see the storms moving in the bands, slow as clock hands. Kettle hangs to the left of it, white and cracked, with its night side toward them, a curved black bite out of the giant's glow.

At the tenth bell, on Kettle's night side, a light comes on.

She sees it because she's been watching for it, ever since the radar pulse at the Throat. It's tiny. A pinprick. A single steady point of white light on the black curve of the moon's dark half, where there should be nothing but ice; not a reflection, not a star behind, not a flare of Gall-light off a ridge. A lamp. It burns for nine seconds by the board's clock. Then it goes out.

Til sits for a long time with her hands on her knees, looking at the place where it was.

She doesn't log it. She doesn't wake Oz. She doesn't know why, any more than she knew at the Throat; only that it felt like something private. As if somebody on that dark moon had lit a lamp in a window to see who was out there in the dark, and had seen her looking, and had put it out.
