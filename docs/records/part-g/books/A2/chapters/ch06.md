# The Throat

## The raw lane

[
  Place: #The Dray
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Juno Brask @@Ozias Mbeki @@Hollis Vey @@Cato Marchetti
  When: 10 April 2917
]

A lane is not a road. People who have never been through one think it is, and draw it on their charts as a line between two dots, and talk about *going down the Corva lane* as if it were a corridor with a door at each end. Til has been through forty-one lanes in her life, some of them many times, and she has stopped trying to say what they are. The nearest she has come is this: a lane is a place where the dark has been folded, and a ship in it is a crease.

An anchored lane is a good crease. Somebody, a long time ago, built a station at each end of it that holds the fold flat, and as long as the anchors hold, a ship goes in at one end and comes out at the other with nothing worse than a headache and a strange taste in the mouth, like licking a battery. An unanchored lane is called raw. The fold moves. It ripples like a flag in a wind. Nobody knows what the wind is.

The Corva lane has been raw for longer than anybody alive has been alive, and for most of that time it was closed. Two years ago it opened again, the way a healed bone sometimes aches and opens, and nobody knows why that either.

"Shear coming," says Oz, from the engine space. "Big one. Twelve seconds."

"I see it."

"You don't see it, you feel it. Hold on to something."

Til holds on to the yoke. In the forward seat beside her Juno is strapped in tight, her new suit sealed and her helmet clipped to her shoulder, and she is holding on to both armrests with both gloved hands and staring straight ahead at the cockpit glass, where there is nothing to see. In a lane there is never anything to see. The glass shows a dim grey flicker, like the inside of an eyelid, and occasionally a long slow ripple of brighter grey crossing it from one side to the other, which is the fold moving, and which you learn very fast not to look at.

The shear hits.

It's like being kicked by the whole universe at once. The *Dray* doesn't shake so much as change shape: for one long, sick, impossible moment Til can feel the cockpit getting longer, the yoke in her hands drawing out away from her like taffy, her own arms stretching to follow it; and then it snaps back, and every rivet in the old tug's hull screams at once, a high thin chorus like a choir of kettles, and something aft goes bang.

"Coolant," says Oz, quite calmly, on the intercom. "Number two loop. That was the coupling."

"How bad?"

"Bad enough that I'd like some help, please, Captain."

Til looks at the board. The core temperature is climbing, not fast but steadily, a line going up at the angle of a cheap staircase. She can't leave the yoke. In a raw lane you never leave the yoke; the autopilot doesn't know what the fold is going to do any more than you do, and it doesn't have the sense to be frightened.

"Juno," she says.

Juno doesn't move.

"Juno. I need you to go aft and help Oz."

"I'm not crew." Juno's voice is very small and very flat. She's still staring at the glass. "Mother Szabo said—"

"Mother Szabo isn't in a raw lane with a coolant leak. Mother Szabo is a day behind us in a much bigger ship with a much better engineer. If the core goes, it goes, and you go with it, and then she'll bill me." Til doesn't look at her. She keeps her eyes on the board and her hands on the yoke and her voice exactly the way it would be if she were asking for a cup of tea. "Unstrap. Clip your helmet on. Go aft along the rail, hand over hand, don't let go of the rail for anything. Oz will tell you what to do. He's very good at telling people what to do."

"I am," says Oz on the intercom.

Juno sits for another two seconds. Then she unstraps.

Til doesn't watch her go. She hears her: the click of the harness, the clunk of the helmet seal, the scrape of boots on the deck, the hand-over-hand rattle of the rail. She hears the aft hatch cycle. She keeps her eyes on the board and her hands on the yoke and flies the *Dray* through the dark that isn't dark, feeling for the fold the way you feel for a step in a black stairwell, and she listens to the intercom.

"There," Oz is saying. "That clamp. No, the big one. Get both hands on it. Now push. Harder than that. Pretend it's me."

A grunt.

"Good. Hold it there. Don't let go of it, whatever happens."

"It's hot."

"It's very hot. Your gloves are rated. Hold it. I'm going to put a sleeve on the coupling and it's going to take me forty seconds, and if you let go of that clamp in the next forty seconds the loop'll blow out past my hands and the core'll scram and we'll be sitting dead in a raw lane, so don't."

"I won't."

"Shear coming," says Til. "Eight seconds."

"Wonderful," says Oz.

"Hold on to the clamp, Juno."

"I'm *holding on to the clamp*."

The second shear is worse than the first. The cockpit stretches and snaps and the kettles scream and the board flickers, and Til, flying, feels the fold go under the *Dray* like a wave under a small boat, a long sickening rise and fall, and holds the yoke against it and does not breathe. Aft, on the intercom, she hears Juno shout something wordless and furious, and Oz swearing in the Roundsman cant, a long liquid stream of it, and then silence.

"Oz."

Nothing.

"Oz."

"Sleeve's on," says Oz. He sounds out of breath. "Loop's holding. She held the clamp."

"Juno?"

A pause. Then Juno's voice, shaking, very young. "I held the clamp."

"Good," says Til. "That was good."

She keeps her eyes on the board. She doesn't let her voice change. She flies the *Dray* on through the shifting dark for another nine hours, through four more shears, none of them as bad, and she never once takes her hands off the yoke. And at some point in the fifth hour, without either of them saying anything about it, Juno comes forward again and straps into the second seat and stays there, and when Til asks her to read off the core temperature every ten minutes, she does.

## Corva

[
  Place: #The Throat
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Juno Brask @@Ozias Mbeki @@Hollis Vey @@Cato Marchetti
  When: 12 April 2917
]

They come out of the Corva lane on the twelfth of April at the fourth bell, ship's time, into a silence so complete that for a long moment Til thinks the board has failed.

The board hasn't failed. It's showing her exactly what's there. The glass has gone from grey to black, real black, star-black, with real stars in it, a great spilled field of them; and ahead and a little below, so close and so huge that her eye refuses it at first as a planet and reads it as a wall, there's Gall.

She has seen gas giants before. Every salvager has; the wreck-fields of the outer Reach are full of them, and the dead hulks always fall toward the big ones in the end. She has never seen one like this. Gall is close, the lane mouth hanging in the dark only a few hundred thousand kilometres off its trailing edge, and it fills a third of the sky: a vast striped curve of ochre and rust and old-bruise purple, banded like the grain in a slab of wood, with storms in the bands the size of worlds, turning. At its southern limb there's a white cap. Across its face, very small and very sharp, a moon throws a round black shadow. And above it, high on the left, laid across a quarter of the sky like a scarf thrown on a floor, is the Lamp.

It's enormous. That's what nobody tells you. In the Observatory's pictures it was a bright head and a long dim tail; out here it's a smear of pale green light as wide as Til's hand at arm's length, with a head that hurts to look at and a tail that goes on and on and on, splitting into two, three, four fainter streamers, fanning out across the stars until it's lost in them. It's so bright Gall's night side is faintly lit by it.

Nobody in the cockpit says anything for a while.

"Well," says Oz eventually, from the hatch. "There it is."

Til runs the board. She does it automatically, the way she would coming out of any lane: traffic, beacons, anchor station, comms. There's no traffic. There are no beacons. There's no anchor station where the anchor station should be, at the lane's mouth, only a faint scatter of radar returns drifting in a loose cloud, metal-sized, like the debris from something that once was there and isn't any more. Whatever held this throat open has been gone a long time. Comms are open on every band she's got, and every band is empty: hiss, and the low growling roar of Gall's radio belts, and under that nothing at all. No traffic control. No nav pings. No voices.

"Nobody home," says Juno quietly.

"Lanes go dark for a reason," says Til. "Usually it's because nobody's left to keep them lit."

"It was a city once," says Oz. "Kettle. My mother said. The ice moon. There was a city under the ice."

"Was or is?"

"She didn't know. Nobody's been through in two hundred years and more. Nobody's said a word on the radio."

"Somebody'd say something," says Juno. "If they were there. Wouldn't they? After two hundred years. If a ship came."

Nobody answers her.

The cradle is in the main cabin, aft of the cockpit, where there's room for it, clamped to the deck by its sled and plugged into the *Dray*'s power with a cable Oz rigged out of three adaptors and a prayer. When Til goes back, Hollis Vey is propped up on his pillows with the cradle's canopy slid back and his face turned to the cabin's only port. Cato Marchetti is sitting beside the cradle on a crate, very still, as he always is, with his hands on his knees.

Vey's face is wet.

He doesn't wipe it. He doesn't seem to know it's wet. He's looking at Gall, at the bruised bands and the white cap and the moon's small shadow crossing, and at the Lamp above it, and his face is a long thin yellowish wreck of a face with the tears running down it into the pillow, and he looks happier than Til has seen anybody look in years.

"Captain," he says. "Look at it."

"I've looked."

"Five hundred years," says Hollis Vey. "My family owned this. All of it. That planet. That moon. That sky." The thin voice through the speaker grille is shaking with something like laughter. "Do you know, we never came? Not one of us. Not one Vey ever came to Corva. We owned it for sixty years and sent factors and took the money and never once came to look. And then we lost it, and spent five hundred years wondering how." He turns his head on the pillow. "I'm the first Vey ever to see it. And the last. That's rather funny, isn't it?"

"I wouldn't know, Mr Vey."

"No. You wouldn't." He smiles at her with his perfect too-large teeth. "Sit down, Captain. I promised I'd tell you my business, when I could bear to. I can bear a little of it now."

Til sits on the edge of the bunk across from him. Marchetti doesn't move. Through the hatch she can hear Juno and Oz in the cockpit talking in low voices; she can hear the *Dray*'s air plant coughing, the way it always coughs after a lane.

"My family's company was called Halloran-Vey," says Hollis. "It was a combine of the old sort, in the old lanes, before the Concord swallowed it and before the Concord choked on it in its turn. It dug things out of places and sold them. Gas from giants. Ice from moons. Labour from people." He says it lightly. "It held this system for sixty years. It ran a skimmer that dipped Gall for deuterium, and a camp of bonded workers on Kettle to refine what the skimmer brought up. And then, in twenty-three ninety-one, it lost it."

"Lost it how?"

"That," says Hollis, "is the question I've spent my life on. The records are very bad. The Combine had a habit, when something went wrong, of writing as little about it as possible. There's a register—a loss register, the Combine kept them for every operation—that says Corva was lost in that year, and that certain assets were not recovered. And then nothing. Two hundred and sixty years later the Concord came here to take it back, and the records of that are worse."

"Took it back from who?"

"From whoever was here." He shrugs, a movement of one bony shoulder under the blanket. "The Concord's archives are a midden. Half of them burned when it fell. I have spent a great deal of money buying the other half off the people who looted them, and they tell me almost nothing. That a fleet was sent. Whether it took anything back, or came back at all, they don't say." He looks back at the port, at the Lamp. "And then, in the dock-houses of Tarn, this story. That the founders of Corva hid something on their comet. That it comes back."

"The Hoard."

"The Hoard." He smiles. "You don't believe in it."

"I've bought two maps to it."

"So have I," says Hollis Vey. "Eleven. All different. All forged." He closes his eyes for a moment, as if the talking has tired him. "But my family lost something here, Captain. Something the register calls an asset not recovered. And the Lamp was in the sky the year they lost it. And it has come back only once since, and the Concord's fleet went to Corva that same year. And now it is coming back for the last time, because Gall is going to eat it." He opens his eyes. "If you were me, and had three months to live, where would you look?"

Til doesn't answer. Beside her, very quietly, Cato Marchetti shifts his weight on the crate, the first movement she has seen him make.

"What's the asset?" Til says. "The one not recovered."

"The register doesn't say," says Hollis. "It gives it a number. That's all."

He's lying. Til knows it the way she knows when a hull plate is about to go: by a change in the sound of things. It's not in his face, which is as open and amused and wrecked as before. It's in Marchetti, who has gone still again, still in a different way, the stillness of a man who has just heard his employer say something he was told not to say and is waiting to see whether anybody noticed.

"All right," says Til, and gets up. "Get some rest, Mr Vey. We'll be at Gall in six days."

## Night watch

[
  Place: #The Dray
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Juno Brask
  When: 13 April 2917
]

On the second night out from the Throat, Til has the watch from the eighth bell to the twelfth, and Juno comes up at the ninth and sits in the second seat without being asked.

She doesn't say anything. She's in her undersuit and a borrowed jumper of Oz's that comes down to her knees, with her knees drawn up inside it and her arms round them, and she sits in the dark cockpit looking out at the Lamp. It's on their left now, high and enormous, and Gall is ahead and below, filling the lower glass with its slow bands. The *Dray*'s drive is running at a quarter, a low steady rumble aft. On the board, nothing moves.

Til does the things you do on watch. She checks the core. She checks the air. She checks the course, which is a long falling curve toward Gall that will take them past the giant and use its gravity to swing them out to meet the Lamp's path; Oz's idea, an old dipper's trick he says his mother told him about. She checks the rear scope. Nothing behind them yet. She checks the comms. Hiss and the roar of the belts.

After a long time, Juno says, "Szabo says you cut her line."

Til doesn't look round.

"On the Harrow," Juno says. "My mother's line. She says when the hulk spun up and started dragging you both, you had a choice, and you cut her line to save yourself. She says that's why you went to the prison, after. For the job being illegal and for that." Her voice is very level. "She says everybody on the Ring knows it."

"Everybody on the Ring knows a lot of things."

"Is it true?"

The Lamp hangs in the glass. Til looks at it.

There are things she has said to herself about this moment, over eleven years. She has had a lot of time to say them: four years of it in the Directorate's yard at Cold Harbour, and seven since on the *Dray*, alone mostly, on long hauls. She has rehearsed what she would say if Juno ever asked. She had it worked out, at one point, very carefully. She can't remember any of it now.

"There was a hulk," she says. "A big one. A Directorate ore carrier, dead forty years, out on the edge of the Harrow, where nobody's allowed to go. Your mother found it. She said there was a fortune in its tanks, and there was, if we could get it. It was a bad job. We knew it was a bad job. We went anyway, because we owed money, and because your mother never in her life saw a bad job she didn't want to go and look at." She stops. "We got a line on to it. Both of us, on one line. And the hulk spun up. Something in its old stabilisers woke up, and it spun, and the line went round it, and it started winding us in."

Juno says nothing.

"There was a knife," Til says. "On the line. There's always a knife on the line. Whoever's nearest the hulk can cut. Your mother was nearest the hulk." She takes a breath. "The line got cut. I came off and she didn't. The hulk took her in. I was picked up by a Directorate patrol eleven hours later with my air on red and they charged me with unlicensed salvage and I did four years in Cold Harbour." She looks at the board. "And Szabo bought your mother's debts off the Harrow brokers and bought you with them, and you were six."

"Who cut the line?"

"The line got cut, Juno."

"Who cut it?"

Til is silent.

"You won't say," says Juno.

"I've said what I've got to say."

"You've said *the line got cut*." Juno's voice is shaking now, just slightly, just at the edge. "You've said it like it was weather. Like it just happened. Like nobody did it." She uncurls her legs and puts her feet on the deck. "I was six. I waited for her at the dock for two days. Mother Szabo came and got me and said she'd gone round and she wasn't coming back, and said my aunt had cut the line. And then you came out of the prison and you never once came to see me. Not once. In seven years. You'd walk past the counting-house window. I saw you. I used to watch for you."

"I know."

"Then why?"

Til looks at her at last. In the dark of the cockpit, lit only by the board and the Lamp, Juno's face is her mother's face exactly, broad and stubborn and furious, with the straight black brows drawn down.

"Because you'd have asked me that," Til says. "And I'd have said what I just said."

Juno stares at her. Then she gets up, and goes out of the cockpit, and down the passage to the warm bunk aft of the galley, and Til hears the bunk curtain rattle shut.

She sits in the dark with the board.

At the eleventh bell the rear scope pings. Til looks. Far behind them, back at the Throat, where the lane's mouth is a faint distortion in the stars, something has come out of the fold: a long black hull with a ram bow, running dark, decelerating hard. She doesn't need the transponder. She knows the shape.

"Hello, Mother," she says to the glass.

She logs it, and turns back to the forward board, and that's when she sees the other thing.

It's very small. It's almost nothing. A single pulse on the radar warning, a bright green flick in the corner of the board, there and gone in half a second: somebody, somewhere ahead, has painted the *Dray* with a ranging radar. One sweep. Then nothing. The warning light goes back to dark.

Til sits very still.

She runs the bearing. It's ahead and a little below, toward Gall. Toward the small white moon whose shadow she has been watching cross the giant's face for two days: a cracked white ball, very bright against the bands, that Oz's mother called Kettle.

She watches the board for the rest of her watch. The pulse doesn't come again. Comms stay empty, hiss and the roar of the belts. Nobody calls. Nobody says a word.

It could be automatic. Old systems run for a long time. A radar left on in some dead station under the ice, sweeping the sky for two centuries out of habit, the way a dead man's heart sometimes twitches on a slab.

When Oz comes up at the twelfth bell to relieve her, she tells him about the *Carrion Hymn*. She doesn't tell him about the pulse. She isn't sure why. She lies in her bunk afterward with her eyes open in the dark and thinks about it: one sweep, from a moon where nobody has said a word in two hundred years. One look.

As if somebody down there had raised their head, and seen them, and put it down again.
