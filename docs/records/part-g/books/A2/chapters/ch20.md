# Sent Round Again

## The last of the paint

[
  Place: #The Wick
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Dasha Imre @@Mattias Ebb
  Items: ^The Share Plate:@Isaura Venn ^Maren's hook-knife:@Isaura Venn ^The Hush:#The black ridge
  When: 8 August 2654

We bring Mattias up out of the shaft between us, Dasha and I, on two lines, and get him into the *Wick* and out of his suit, and he bleeds on the cabin floor for an hour while Dasha does what she can with the skiff's kit and her rigger's hands. The shot went in under his ribs on the left side and out again at the back. His patch held. It held well enough that he's alive, and not well enough that he'll be alive for very long without a medic, and the nearest medic is three days and a giant away.

"I'll fly," he says, when he can say anything. He's lying across the two back seats with his head in Dasha's lap and his face the colour of the frost on the cryostat. "Strand's gone. Somebody has to fly. I'm a lander pilot."

"You've got a hole in you."

"Landers don't care," says Mattias, and tries to laugh, and stops.

The cryostat is out on the ice, where I left it, at the foot of the black ridge, clipped to Dasha's surface line beside the anchor. I didn't bring it into the *Wick*. I couldn't. I stood on the ice with it in my hand after Corin went, after his voice went out of my helmet, and I looked at the *Wick*'s open hatch and the little cargo well behind the seats, the size of a coffin, and I couldn't put it in there. It's out on the Lamp in the shadow of the ridge with its new bright scar down its side and its lights dark and its window cracked, and the cold of the Lamp all round it. That's where it lives. That's where it sleeps.

I have the Plate on my knees.

I don't know why I'm doing it now. There's a man bleeding on the floor at my feet and another drifting out into the coma somewhere above us and my mother on the Rime and thirty-eight ships between me and home, and I'm sitting in my seat in the *Wick* with the work-lamp clipped over my head and the founders' Plate face down on my knees, cleaning. Dab. Wait. Wipe. My hands won't stop shaking. I think it's the only thing I can do with them that isn't the other thing, the thing they did on the ice.

There's still paint on the middle of the back, round the line I cleared on the second, *it sleeps in the cold*. Above it and below it. I work outward from the line. I've stopped using Malachy's brush; it's too slow. I'm using the knife. Abel Tsolo's knife. Maren Tsolo's knife. The hook-bladed line knife that hung at the hip of a dead man for two hundred and sixty-three years and cut a living one off the Lamp an hour ago. I hold it by the hilt and use the back of the curved blade to lift the softened paint, and it comes away in long grey curls, like wood under a plane.

Above the line, two words.

*WE SENT IT ROUND*

Below it, four lines, one under another, in the same hard shaking capitals, the awl skidding on every T.

*DO NOT WAKE IT*

*DO NOT USE IT*

*FORGIVE US*

And then, after a gap, smaller, as if whoever cut it had stopped for a long while before they could:

*FORGIVE ABEL*

And under that, two letters and a year.

*M.T. 2391*

I sit with the knife in my hand and look at it.

"What does it say?" says Dasha, from the floor.

I read it to her. All of it. From the numbers at the top down to the initials. My voice is quite steady. When I get to *forgive Abel* she makes a small sound, and Mattias, with his head in her lap and his eyes shut, says, very faintly, "Who's M.T.?"

"His sister," I say. "Maren Tsolo. The tether-chief."

"The one who didn't go."

"The one who threw him."

"She wrote that," says Dasha slowly. "After. She was here. On Kettle. After he went out. She knew where he was and what he'd done and she wrote it down on the back of the Plate." She looks up at me. "And then somebody painted it over."

"Somebody," I say.

I turn the Plate over.

I haven't looked at the front of it since the Archive. I've been looking at its back for six days, inch by inch, under the work-lamp, and I've almost forgotten it has a front. The three hundred names. Fifteen columns, three hundred hands, deep and neat and scratched and shaky. I look at them now in the green light of the Lamp coming in through the port, and I find what I knew I'd find. Halfway down the second column, neat and deep, like the hand on a warden's form: *CORWIN HALE*. Lower down, big and careless and sloping across a whole column's width, so that the next name has had to squeeze itself in underneath: *ABEL TSOLO*. A little drawing of a fish next to a name I can't read. *DANEK SORELL*. *LUSINE*, very small, beside *ODILE ARDA*.

And at the top of the first column, where every child in Patience has been told for two hundred and sixty-three years that the Company kept its share until the Purchase was done, a gouge. A rough, ragged, scraped patch of grey steel, where something was cut once and somebody took a chisel to it.

*It was her plan and her line*, Abel said, *and she was going to come herself*.

I don't know what happened to her. Nobody will ever know, now; it isn't in the Archive and it isn't in the recording and it isn't on the Plate. I know her brother took her coat and her place and went out on the Lamp, and that she threw him straight. I know she lived long enough to cut the Lamp's road and her brother's name into the back of the founders' Plate with an awl. I know that somebody painted it over. I know that somebody scraped her name off the front and called it the Company's, and that the man whose name is halfway down the second column, neat and deep, was a company man, and that his great-great-great-great-great-great-grandson pointed a gun at me an hour ago and said *Dad* as he went out into the dark.

I know that's enough.

"What are you going to do?" says Dasha.

I put the knife down on the Plate. I look out of the port, at the black ridge, at the cryostat sitting in its shadow on the ice. At Gall, filling half the sky, one day from the Lamp's closest pass. At the long bright tail streaming out behind us into the black, toward where, very small and very far, I know Kettle is, white and cracked, with forty thousand people on its surface in the old domes of the Rime.

"I'm going to talk to them," I say.

## The broadcast

[
  Place: #The Wick
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Dasha Imre @@Mattias Ebb @@Anselm Grieve:phone @@Lucan Strand:phone @@Malachy Orr:phone
  Items: ^Abel's recorder:@Isaura Venn

The *Wick* has a dipper's radio, which means it has a very big one. Dipper skiffs go down into Gall's cloud-tops where the giant's radio belts roar like a furnace and anything weaker than a shout gets swallowed; so the skiffs carry transmitters that could be heard, Corin told me on the first night out, from one end of the system to the other, if you aimed them at it. I don't know how to aim them. Mattias does. He tells me, lying across the back seats with his eyes shut and his voice going in and out like a signal, which switches and which dials, and Dasha sets them, and on the ninth of August, at the moment the Lamp makes its closest pass by Gall, at the bottom of its long fall from the dark, I sit in Corin Strand's pilot seat with Abel Tsolo's recorder in my lap and its jack in the board, and open every band the *Wick* has got, and talk.

I don't know who hears it. I find out later. Everybody hears it. Every speaker in Patience, on every tier, the Council's own emergency channel that clicks like a knuckle on a table; every receiver on every one of the Concord's thirty-eight ships, from the *Steadfast*'s command deck to the lander bays to the cooks; the crawlers on the Rime, the domes, the dipper fleet sitting grounded in its docks. Malachy tells me afterward that he heard it in the Archive, alone, at his table, and sat there with his hands flat on the empty place where the Plate had lain, and listened to all of it, and couldn't move.

I start with the recording.

I don't play all of it. Forty minutes is too long; the Lamp doesn't wait. I play the beginning. *My name is Abel Tsolo. I'm a rigger on the skimmer* Patience. *I'm wearing my sister's coat.* I play the part about the jeweller's file, and the part about the bonded taking the ship, and Ferrand with his hands up, and Danek Sorell in the cold hold. I play the part about Tamsin's Rock. I play the part where he says *Hale wanted to use it on the cutter, eighty people, and my sister stood up in the drum hall and said no*. I play the end. *Don't wake it. Whoever you are. It's not a gift. It was never a gift.*

Then I switch it off, and lean forward to the microphone, and say my name.

"This is Isaura Venn. I'm an air-warden of Patience. Ninth South, then the Cells. Unshared." My voice is very level. I've been an air-warden for twelve years. I know how to say a number in a room. "I'm on the Lamp. I'm sitting on its south limb, in a dipper skiff, under a black ridge, two hundred metres from a tug that was put down here in twenty-three ninety-one by the man you just heard. He's forty metres under me in the ice. He's been there two hundred and sixty-three years."

I breathe.

"There's something else here. The thing he brought. It's sitting on the ice outside my port, in a steel cryostat, with a scar down its side where Corin Strand shot at it yesterday. It's a grey egg. It's got four lines stencilled on it and a green wheel. It's called a Sanitation Agent, Pattern H. The bonded called it the Hush. The Concord calls it Asset Seven." I look at it through the port, in the shadow of the ridge. "Commodore Grieve. This is what you came for."

The cabin is silent. Mattias has opened his eyes.

"The men and women on your ships should know what it is," I say. "So I'm going to tell them. Your briefing cards say *do not open*. They don't say why. It's because it kills people. Everyone in a ship or a station or a city. It goes through the air and they go to sleep and they don't wake up. It doesn't touch the machines. That's what sanitation means. The Combine used one on a place called Tamsin's Rock, on four thousand of its own workers, because they struck. They filled the place with new workers inside a year." I wait a moment. "Commodore, you have orders. A man on your own lander deck told me about them. If the city of Patience has Asset Seven, you're to take it back. And if the city won't surrender, you're to *sanitise the claim*. With this. On Kettle. A hundred and eighty thousand people. You'd ship in new ones from Corran."

On the open band, very faint, somebody says something. A voice, a man's, not Grieve's. A babble behind it. Then silence.

"I'm not going to give it to you," I tell the Commodore. "And I'm not going to bring it home."

I take a breath. I look at Dasha, at Mattias.

"First Shareholder," I say. "You'll be listening. Your son tried to take it home. He wanted to put it on the *Steadfast*'s hull and open it. Eleven thousand people. I stopped him. He's gone. He went out into the coma off the Lamp, an hour after we found it, because I cut his line with a dead man's knife. I'm sorry. I'm sorry for it. His last word was for you."

Nothing on the band. Nothing at all.

"And you should know where your family comes from, First Shareholder," I say, "because everybody else in the city knows now. Corwin Hale. Chief warden of the *Patience*. A company informer. He gave a boy a jeweller's file to cut a runner's harness clip, because she'd read the Combine's mail. He wanted to use this thing on eighty sleeping people to win a vote. His name is halfway down the second column of the Share Plate. I'm holding it. His is very neat." I look down at the Plate on the seat beside me. "And at the top of the first column, where you've taught every child in Patience that the Company kept its share, there's a scrape. Somebody took a chisel to a name there. And on the back, under the paint, in the same hand that wrote down where this thing is, there are two letters. M.T. Maren Tsolo. The tether-chief. Her brother went out with the Lamp in her coat, and she threw him, and then somebody took her name off the Plate and told us all we'd bought our air."

I put my hand flat on the Plate.

"We didn't buy anything," I say. "We took it. In the night, with blood. And then they had this thing, the founders. In their hands. They could've used it on the cutter and been free that night, and they nearly did, and a woman stood up in the drum hall and said no. They sent it round instead. On the Lamp. *Do not wake it. Do not use it. Forgive us.* That's what she wrote on the back of the Plate." My voice has gone at last; I let it. "That's the founding of Patience. That's what we come from. Not the Purchase. Her."

In the back of the cabin, Dasha is crying.

"There are forty thousand people on the Rime," I say. "On Council orders. *The few for the many. Cut the line to save the ship.* My mother's one of them. I gave her my coat and my key in South Lock. She's mending filters for the Ocampos." I hear myself say it and I can't stop. "Open the valves. Shared, unshared, anybody who can hear me. Go up to the Rime and bring them down. The siege is about this, and only this, and this isn't coming home. There's nothing left to cut the line for." I look at the egg on the ice. "Commodore, your asset is going back where it came from. Into the cold. You can't follow it. Nobody can. I'm going to put it forty metres down in the ice beside the man who brought it, and the Lamp's going to take it out past the cold for another two hundred and sixty years, and you can go home and tell the Concord it's gone. That's the truth. You can check. Look at the Lamp in a month."

I take my hand off the Plate.

"That's all," I say. "This is Isaura Venn. On the Lamp. Out."

I switch it off.

Nobody in the *Wick* says anything for a long time.

## Come for it, then

]

Grieve answers four minutes later.

He sounds exactly the way he sounded on the speakers at Lampfast: dry, tired, educated, as if he's reading. He doesn't deny anything. He says that he has heard the transmission from the comet and that he notes its contents. He says that the Concord's orders regarding Asset Seven are not a matter he is able to discuss on an open band. He says that if the person speaking has the Asset in her possession, she is in possession of Concord property, and that she is required to surrender it, unopened, to the Concord's officers, who will come for it. He says that if she does not, he will be obliged to take such steps as are necessary.

"Come for it, then," says Mattias, from the back seats, very faintly, though Grieve can't hear him. "You'll never match it now. Not with the *Steadfast*. Not with anything. It's past the bottom. It's going out."

He's right. I find out later that the Commodore tried. That two of his frigates lit their drives and turned toward the Lamp, the *Vigilant* and the *Resolute*, and burned hard for an hour, and fell back, the way Maren Tsolo said in the drum hall two hundred and sixty-three years ago that the *Admonition* would.

What I don't know, sitting in the *Wick*, is what's happening on the *Steadfast*'s lander deck. I learn that later too. From Malachy, who heard it on the Concord's own fleet band, which some lander crew had left open in the confusion, so that half of Patience heard it as well. A man's voice, a junior officer, and then another, and then a lot of them, all at once, in the big bay where the lander crews eat, where a commander from the Commodore's staff once told them *you'll know it if you see it*. Questions. Shouting. Somebody asking whether it was true. Somebody saying *Tamsin's Rock, I know Tamsin's Rock, my grandmother was from the Penner lanes*. Somebody saying the Concord could go and sanitise itself. And then the *Steadfast*'s lander deck going off the fleet band altogether, and the *Constant*'s, and the *Fortitude*'s, and three of the Concord's thirty-eight ships not answering the Commodore's orders for the rest of the day.

"They're bonded," Mattias says, much later, when I tell him. "Most of them. Like me. Somebody's fishing boat." He closes his eyes. "Nobody tells them anything. It's always already happened."

## Forty metres again

The first slug hits the Lamp three hours later.

We're in the shaft. Dasha and I, with the cryostat between us on two lines, lowering it back down. It goes down easier than it came up; nothing weighs anything on the Lamp, and the shaft is clean now, and the blue light comes down through the ice to meet us. I've got the Plate strapped on my back in its blanket. I've got the knife at my hip, in its old hard sheath, on a loop I've cut from my own harness. Mattias is in the *Wick*, alone, at the controls, with the hole in his side and his hand on the stick, because somebody has to be.

The slug hits a kilometre off, on the far side of the ridge. I don't hear it. Nobody hears anything on the Lamp. I feel it. The whole shaft jumps, a hard sharp jolt like the Spine being kicked, and the blue walls ring, and ice comes down past us in a glittering sheet, and the cryostat swings on its lines and bangs the wall.

"He's shooting at it," says Dasha. Her voice is very calm. "Your commodore. He can't catch it, so he's going to break it."

"He can't break it. Not the egg. Not with slugs."

"No," says Dasha. "But he can break us."

"Isaura," says Mattias, in my helmet. Faint. "That was a slug. Off the *Steadfast*, from a long way out. They're walking them in. Ten minutes between. They'll be close in thirty." A breath. "You've got thirty."

"Thirty," I say.

We get the cryostat down.

We put it back where it was. Beside him. In the little round room cut in the blue ice with a pick, against the curve of the wall, where it sat for two hundred and sixty-three years. Its lights are still dark. The crack across its window has spread a little. Inside, through the crack, in the light of my helmet, the egg lies in its brittle foam, grey and smooth and indifferent, as if it's never been moved at all. Abel sits beside it in his frosted coat, with his knees up and his head on his arms, as if he never moved either.

The second slug hits. Closer. The room rings.

"Twenty," says Mattias.

I unstrap the Plate from my back and take it out of its blanket and kneel in the little room with it face up on my knees, three hundred names in steel, and the gouge at the top of the first column. I take the knife off my hip. It's sharp. It's the best knife I ever held. It's not meant for cutting steel; nothing that size is. But the Lamp's cold makes the old steel of the Plate brittle, and the edge is hard, and I'm an air-warden, I've spent twelve years turning frozen valves with my whole weight on them, and I put my weight on it.

I cut her back in.

It takes me twelve minutes. Dasha holds the lamp and counts the slugs. The letters are bad. Worse than hers; worse than the awl. They go across the gouge and through it, ragged, deep, the knife skidding on the scars Hale's chisel left, so that the name sits on top of its own erasure like a word written over a word. But you can read it. Anybody could read it.

*MAREN TSOLO*

"Ten," says Mattias.

I turn the Plate over. Under her lines on the back, under *FORGIVE ABEL* and *M.T. 2391*, there's a hand's width of bare grey steel, where the paint's come off and nothing was ever written. I cut there too. Faster. Smaller. I don't think about the words. I've been thinking about them for six days without knowing it.

*SENT ROUND AGAIN 2654*

*WE DID NOT USE IT EITHER*

*ISAURA VENN AIR-WARDEN*

*FOR THE UNSHARED*

The third slug hits. It's very close. The room jumps and goes dark for a second, all the blue gone out of the walls, as if something's passed over the sun; and then it comes back, flickering, and Dasha has her hand on my shoulder.

"Isaura. Now."

I stand the Plate against him. Against his knees, where they're drawn up, the way you'd lean a picture against a wall, face out, so the names show. So whoever comes will see it first. Hers at the top, cut over the scar.

I put the knife back in its sheath on his hip. It took me a long time to decide about that, in the *Wick*, with Mattias bleeding at my feet. I don't know who'll need it. Somebody might. Somebody always does.

Last, I take my own recorder out of my suit, the little warden's log I've kept every round of every shift for twelve years, and that I talked into for an hour last night while Dasha slept, in the *Wick*, with the Plate on my knees. All of it. The Ocampos and the floor and the grandmother. The bottle at South Lock. Mattias at the grille. The Rime. My mother. Corin. The knife. All of it. I don't know who it's for. Whoever comes. I put it in his hand, his gloved hand, folded round his shin, beside his own.

"Forty metres again," I tell him. "Sorry. We tried."

"Isaura," says Dasha.

We go up the shaft as fast as I've ever gone up anything, hand over hand, with the blue walls ringing round us and the ice coming down. At the top the sky is full of fire. There's a new crater a hundred metres along the ridge, black and steaming, a great raw wound in the Lamp's south limb with a jet roaring out of the bottom of it straight up into the dark; and the old *Gull* has been knocked over on to her side by the shock, her grapple arm torn half out of the ice, but she's there, still, at the foot of the ridge, where she's sat for two hundred and sixty-three years. The *Wick* is lifting already, on her thrusters, rocking, her hatch open, a metre off the ground.

We go in through the hatch and Dasha slams it and Mattias puts the nose up.

## The lieutenant

We lift off the Lamp at the bottom of its pass, as the fourth slug comes down where we were.

I watch it hit. I'm strapped into Corin's seat beside Mattias, with my helmet still on, and I watch the place on the open ice where the *Wick* was sitting a minute ago, two hundred metres from the ridge, jump and flash and vanish in a great slow fountain of black and white, ice and soot and fire going up together into the sky. The *Wick* lurches. Something hits her, a lump of ice, a stone, something; I feel it go through the hull behind my seat like a fist, and the cabin fills for a moment with a high thin shriek of air going out, and then the patches Dasha has had ready for three days go on, and hold.

"Hit," says Mattias. His voice is very quiet. "Not bad. We're going."

We go. Up and away from the Lamp's south limb, out of its shadow into the sun, with the Lamp falling away behind us, its black body breathing fire from a dozen new wounds and its tails fanned across half the sky; and then Gall, swinging into the forward port, vast and banded and close, and Mattias puts the *Wick*'s nose down toward it, toward the sling.

"I'm going to take us round," he says. "Same way we came. The giant'll throw us home. Three days." He breathes. "You'll have to land her. At the other end. I'll show you the switches."

"You'll land her."

"I'll show you the switches," says Mattias.

He shows me on the way down into Gall's sky, with the cloud-tops rising under us and the shield beginning to buzz. He shows me with one hand, the other pressed to his side, where the blood has come through the patch and through the flight suit and is shining on his fingers. He tells me what each one is for and when, and makes me say it back to him, the way Malachy made me say my sums back when I was eight. He says it all very carefully. He doesn't hurry.

We go round the giant. I don't remember it. I remember the push in my chest, and the grey at the edges, and his voice in my helmet through all of it, saying the switches.

When we come up out of the sling on the other side, on our long curve home toward Kettle, I look back. The Lamp is behind us now, a great white blaze going out, beginning already to dwindle. And far beyond it, back toward the Throat, there's a flash.

It's very small. A spark of blue-white light at the edge of the dark, where the lane mouth hangs behind Gall; there and gone. Then another, smaller, beside it. Then nothing.

"What's that?" says Dasha.

Mattias has his eyes on the board. He doesn't need to look.

"The anchor," he says. "The Throat's anchor station. That was the *Steadfast*'s guns." He breathes out, slowly. "He's going. The Commodore. He's taking the fleet home. And he's shutting the door behind him."

"Why?"

"Because he thinks you've got it," says Mattias. "Or that you could get it. A city with Asset Seven in its back pocket, at the end of a lane, that just told his whole fleet what he was ordered to do with it. He can't take it back. So he can't let it out." He smiles, very faintly. "He's quarantined you. The lane'll go raw now, without its anchor. It'll close. Ten years. Twenty. Nobody'll come through it for a long time." He closes his eyes. "Nobody'll go out, either. You'll be on your own."

"We were always on our own," says Dasha.

"Yes," says Mattias. "That's what I thought."

He dies on the second day out, in the morning, in the pilot's seat, with his hand on the stick.

He's been quiet a long time. He's stopped talking about the switches; I know them now. I've been sitting beside him in Corin's seat watching his face go greyer and his breathing go slower, and Dasha's been behind us with her hand on his shoulder, and nobody's been saying anything. Gall's behind us. Kettle's ahead, white and small and growing.

"Warden," he says, near the end. His eyes are open, looking at the port.

"I'm here."

"What's my allowance?"

I look at the board. At the cabin air. At him.

"Three-twenty," I say.

"Good," says Mattias Ebb. "I won't conserve."

## Breathe

[
  Place: #South Lock
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Malachy Orr @@Dasha Imre @@Mattias Ebb @@Ama Venn:dead elsewhere
  Items: ^Isaura's gauge:@Pilar Ocampo

I land the *Wick* on Kettle on the fourteenth of August, badly, on the surface ice a kilometre from South Lock, because I can't find the dock shaft and I'm too frightened to try. She comes down hard on her belly in the snow and slews round and stops, and Dasha and I sit in our seats with our hands shaking and laugh until we cry, and then just cry.

They come out for us on a crawler. Riggers, in orange, and wardens, in grey, and Malachy, in a surface suit three sizes too big for him with his old grey coat over the top of it, sitting on the crawler's bed with his slippered feet dangling. He's the first one through the *Wick*'s hatch. He doesn't say anything. He looks at me, and at Dasha, and at the pilot's seat, where Mattias is still sitting with his hands folded in his lap because we didn't have the heart to move him; and then he puts his arms round me, in his too-big suit, and holds on.

He tells me on the crawler, on the way in. All of it. He tells it the way he told me my history lessons on the Fourth when I was nine, in order, very clearly, leaving nothing out.

He tells me that everybody heard. He tells me that the nave filled, that afternoon, the ninth, with people who hadn't been inside it in weeks: shared and unshared together, standing on the benches and the old tether gallery and the drum's own scarred flank, under the Oculus, with the Lamp burning over them; that they stood there and listened to it all again, because somebody in the Council's radio room had recorded it and played it on every speaker in the city, every hour, until the First Shareholder's stewards stopped trying to stop them. He tells me that the forty heads of the Shares met in the old bridge that night and did not vote for anything, because the old bridge was full of people who weren't Shares, standing between the dead consoles, waiting to see what they would do; and that Lucan Strand stood at the helm with his hand on the rail and his face like paper and didn't say a word, not one, for the whole of the meeting, and at the end of it walked out of the bridge and down to the Strand house on the Third Tier and shut the door, and hasn't opened it since.

He tells me the crawlers went up to the Rime at dawn on the tenth. Every crawler in the city. Driven by dippers and wardens and refinery men and anybody who could drive one, with the South Lock doors standing open and nobody, Malachy says, nobody at all, standing in front of them saying *no movement between tiers*. He tells me they brought them down all day and all night, forty thousand people, out of the old domes and down the ramp and into the warm, and that the shared tiers opened their doors, some of them, a lot of them, more than he'd have believed, and took people in.

He tells me the Concord's ships went back through the Throat on the eleventh, all of them, the three that weren't answering and the thirty-five that were, and that on the twelfth the dipper fleet flew for the first time since June, eighty skiffs going up out of the dock shaft one after another into Gall's sky in a long glittering line, and came back on the thirteenth with their tanks full; and that the reserve board on Fourth Central, which somebody's put back up, said twenty-two this morning, and climbing.

Then he stops.

"Isaura," he says.

I know. I've known since he put his arms round me in the *Wick*. I've known, I think, since South Lock.

"The sixth," says Malachy. "Before you'd even reached the Lamp. In the Ocampos' dome, on the Rime. She'd been mending their filters. The Bekkers' and the Ocampos' and old Sofi's. Nine days without a stop. Dasha's people up there said she wouldn't sleep." His voice is very gentle. "The cough went into her chest, they said. Like the grandmother. She just got tired."

I don't say anything.

"She'd given your coat to the girl," says Malachy. "The Ocampo girl. The night before. The girl was cold. She put it round her and did the buttons up. They said the girl wouldn't take it off after. Not even in the crawler. She's still got it on." He looks at me. "And your key. She was still holding your key."

## The eleventh door

[
  Place: #The Ninth Tier
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Pilar Ocampo
  Items: ^Isaura's gauge:@Isaura Venn
  When: 15 August 2654
]

I go up to the Ninth South at the end of the next day, after the medics have finished with me, after Dasha's gone home to the Ninth North to sleep for a week, after I've stood for an hour in the Archive with Malachy looking at the empty place on his big table where the Plate used to lie under the lamps.

The Ninth's warm. That's the first thing. I've never in my life felt the Ninth warm. The fans are running full, every one, all along the corridor, so loud you have to raise your voice; and the doors are open, half of them, people coming and going between them, carrying things, talking. The frost on the ceiling's dripping. Somebody's got a pan of something on a heater in the corridor outside the sixth door, and the smell of it is all down the tier.

The eleventh door's open.

Pilar Ocampo is sitting in it, on the threshold, with her knees drawn up. She's wearing a grey wool coat that comes down past her boots and has its sleeves rolled up six times, a warden's coat, the best coat I ever owned. In her lap, in her cracked grey gloves, she's holding a flow gauge and a valve key on a ring.

She looks up at me with her coin-flat face, and doesn't say anything.

I sit down on the floor of the corridor beside her, with my back against the ice-brick wall, and for a long time we just sit there, the two of us, listening to the fans.

"Mama's at the plant," she says at last. "Papa's at the plant. Tomás and Juli are asleep." She looks down at the gauge in her lap. "She gave me this. Your mama. She said give it back to the warden when she comes." She holds it out. "She said you'd need it."

I take it. I turn it over in my hands, the old brass gauge, the key worn bright where my fingers have held it for twelve years. Then I get up, and go to the eleventh door's frame, to the meter under its frosted window, and wipe the window with my glove.

The needle's on the red. Nobody's had the time to turn it back. Two hundred litres an hour for five. The floor.

"Ocampo, Ninth South eleven," I say aloud, to nobody, to the corridor, to the whole warm loud dripping tier. There's no ledger on my wrist. I left it in the *Wick*. "Household of five. Tithe owing—"

I stop.

I fit the key. The valve's stiff. They're all stiff, up here. I put my shoulder into it, my whole weight, the way I've done ten thousand times, and it turns, and the needle comes up off the red, and up, and past the old floor and the older one and every line on the dial I've ever read aloud into a ledger; and up, to the stop. And the fan behind the eleventh door changes its note, the way they always do, from a sigh to a roar.

Pilar's watching me.

"What's that?" she says.

"That's the stop," I say. "That's as far as it goes."

"Is that allowed?"

"I don't know," I tell her, and go on to the twelfth.

I do them all. Every door on the Ninth South, all sixty-one, from the eleventh to the end and then back round to the first. Some of them are open and some of them aren't, and I don't knock, and I don't say *air-warden*, and I don't read anything aloud. I wipe each window with my glove, and fit the key, and put my weight on it, and turn every valve on the tier as far as it will go, until the whole long cold corridor under the surface ice is roaring with air like the inside of a giant's lung, and the frost on the ceiling is coming down in drops all along it like rain.

Pilar Ocampo follows me the whole way, in my coat, with her cracked grey gloves, watching. At every door, after I've turned it, she reaches up and wipes the window again, the way I do, and looks at the needle on the stop, and says, quite seriously, to the meter, to the door, to whoever's on the other side of it, the word I've been trying to say for twelve years and never had the right to.

"Breathe," she says.
