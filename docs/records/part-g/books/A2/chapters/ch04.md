# Consolidation

## Lusine's locker

[
  Place: #The Patience
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Odile Arda
  Items: ^Lusine's clip:@Maren Tsolo
  When: 5 November 2391
]

The runners lived aft of the drum, in a long low compartment the crew called the Shelf, where the bunks were stacked four high along both walls and the air came in through a single overworked fan that had been rattling since before any of them were born. Each runner had a bunk and a locker. When a runner went into Gall, the locker was emptied by the warden's office, and anything of value was sold against the account, and the rest went into the waste cycler. It had been three days. The warden's office had not got round to Lusine's yet. Maren had made sure of that, by giving Bram Kovač two dips' worth of overtime on the drum.

Odile was sitting on her sister's bunk when Maren came in, with the locker open beside her and its contents laid out on the blanket in rows, as if for an inventory. A spare undersuit. A comb. A packet of the cheap dried fruit they sold in the Camp at Midwinter. A cloth bag of hair ties. A stack of letters from somebody in the Camp, tied with string. A small battered slate with a cracked screen. A carved wooden fish, no bigger than a thumb, very old.

"That was our mother's," Odile said, without looking up. "The fish. She carved it. She used to carve things. She said she'd teach me but she didn't."

Maren sat down on the bunk opposite. "I'm sorry, Odile."

"You keep saying that."

"I keep meaning it."

Odile picked up the fish and turned it over in her fingers. Her face was swollen and blotched and fierce, and she had not slept, by the look of her, since the dip; she had done her shifts anyway, both of them, on the line, at six kilometres, and run them clean, and Maren had watched her dot on the board and said nothing, because there was nothing to say to a runner who wanted to be on the line except *clamps on*.

"The warden came round," Odile said. "Hale. Yesterday. With the form." She put the fish down in its place in the row. "Said I'd got her account now. Said it was standard. Two hundred and eighty days, carried forward, at the standard rate. For her suit and her bottle and her beacon and the inspection on the crawler. He was very sorry."

"He's always very sorry."

"I said I'd pay it." Odile looked up at last. "I said I'd pay it, Chief, because what else do you say? And he said that was very good of me and wrote it down. And then he said, was there anything of hers I'd found, any papers, any messages. For the inquiry." She was watching Maren's face. "I said no. Because I hadn't looked yet."

"And now you have."

"And now I have."

Odile reached under the pillow and brought out a strip of grey foil, the kind the relay printers used for hardcopy, folded small and soft with handling. She held it out.

"It was in her boot," she said. "Her deck boot. The left one. Inside the liner, under the heel. She always kept her money there when we were kids, in case somebody went through her pockets. I knew where to look." Her mouth moved. "Nobody else would."

Maren unfolded the foil.

It was a relay transcript, printed in the cramped type the old relay printers used, and it had been printed by hand, one line at a time, as if somebody had been feeding the printer a little at a time so it wouldn't log a full job. It was headed with the Halloran-Vey wheel and a string of routing codes, and below the codes, a single block of text.

*FROM: Combine Operations, Halloran. TO: Factor-designate A. QUIST, by beacon ahead of CLEMENT. SUBJECT: Consolidation of Corva Operation.*

*1. Corva Operation to close effective arrival of factor-designate. Operation no longer commercially viable following Sorrel price adjustment. Board resolution 2391/114 refers.*

*2. Skimmer PATIENCE to complete current season dips to maximise final load. Vessel to be decommissioned and scuttled in Gall at season end. Salvage value does not justify transit.*

*3. Skilled bonded (est. 300, PATIENCE complement) to be transferred with accounts aboard CLEMENT to Levy Office, Sorrel Station, for assignment under Combine-Levy labour agreement 2390/22. Transfer to be presented as relocation.*

*4. Kettle Camp installations and bonded accounts (est. 2,100 households) to remain in place. Supply to cease on departure of CLEMENT. Accounts to be offered at discount to secondary holders.*

*5. Factor to manage expectations. Contingency authority (Schedule H) confirmed. ADMONITION to follow as escort.*

Maren read it once, slowly, and then again. The fan rattled. Somewhere forward of the Shelf the drum was beginning its spin-up for the second dip, a long rising groan that came through the deck like a tide.

"What's the Levy," said Odile.

"The Levy Office is how the Combine rents bonded to the Sorrel lane forts," Maren said. Her own voice sounded strange to her. "For the war. Diggers. Loaders. People to stand on gun-decks and fill sandbags with ice. They pay the Combine by the head. The Combine pays the bonded the standard rate against their account. Most of them never come back up the lane."

"And the Camp."

"*Supply to cease.*"

"What does that mean?"

Maren looked at her. Odile was nineteen. She had been born on Kettle, in the Camp, in the warrens of the third shaft, where her mother had carved fish and her father had died of the ice cough when she was two; she had never been anywhere else. She knew what supply meant. She was asking because she wanted somebody to say it so she would not have to.

"It means nobody's coming back," said Maren. "It means the *Clement* leaves and doesn't come again, and the Camp has what's in its tanks and its stores, and when that's gone, it's gone. The refinery runs on what the *Patience* brings it, and the *Patience* will be at the bottom of Gall. They'll have three months, maybe. Four, if they're careful. Then the air plant stops."

"Two thousand households."

"Twenty-one hundred."

"And then somebody buys their accounts," said Odile, very quietly. "At a discount. Somebody buys the debts of dead people."

"Somebody always does."

Odile put her face in her hands. She didn't cry. She sat there with her hands over her face and her shoulders very still, breathing, and after a while she said through her fingers, "She told someone."

"She said so. On the line."

"She'd have told me. She told me everything. She didn't tell me this." Odile took her hands away. Her eyes were dry and terrible. "She'd only not tell me if she was scared for me. If she'd told somebody else first and it had gone wrong. If she thought they'd—" She stopped. "Who'd she tell, Chief?"

"I don't know."

"She'd have told someone she trusted. Someone she—" Odile's mouth twisted. "She was seeing somebody. The last couple of months. She wouldn't say who. She went all quiet and pleased about it, like when we were kids and she'd stolen something. I thought it was one of the dock boys."

"Was it?"

"I don't know. She said—she said he was funny. She said he made her laugh in the lock." Odile looked at the rows of her sister's things on the blanket: the comb, the fruit, the fish. "She said he was always in debt and he didn't care. She said it was restful."

Maren's hands had gone very cold. She folded the foil up again along its old creases, small and soft, and held it.

"Keep this between us," she said. "Not Hale. Not anyone. Not even the runners."

"Why?"

"Because the last person who read it told somebody," said Maren, "and she's in Gall."

## Relocation

[
  Place: #The Drum
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Aurelian Quist @@Corwin Hale @@Abel Tsolo @@Danek Sorell @@Grandam Teska @@Bram Kovac @@Odile Arda
  When: same day
]

Quist told them that evening, at the change of shift, in the drum hall, under the names.

He did it well. Maren had to give him that, afterward, when she had the stomach to give him anything. He stood on the gallery with Hale beside him and the marine sergeant at his back, in his plain dark coat with the wheel on the lapel, and he did not read from a slate. He told them that the Combine had reviewed its operations in the Corva system and had concluded, with regret, that the market for Corva deuterium could no longer sustain them. He told them that the *Patience* was an old ship, older than most of them, and that she would finish her season and then be retired. He told them that the Combine valued its skilled bonded very highly, and that every one of the *Patience*'s complement would be relocated, with their accounts intact and in good standing, to new postings in the Sorrel lanes, where the work was steady and the air was cheap. He told them the *Clement* would carry them. He told them it would sail at the end of the month.

He told them nothing about the Levy, or the war, or the Camp.

He did not lie about the Camp. That was the clever thing. When old Pirro from the Bucket crew shouted up from the floor, *What about our families? What about the Camp?*, Quist turned to look at him with his long grave lined face and said that the Kettle installations would be transferred to a successor operator under the usual terms, and that the Combine's obligations to the Camp's residents would pass with them, and that family members of relocated bonded who wished to follow on were welcome to apply for passage at their own account. Every word of it was true. Maren, standing at the back of the hall under the drum with the foil in her pocket, could hear exactly how true each word was, and exactly how much it weighed.

*At their own account.* A passage on the *Clement* to Sorrel cost more than a rigger earned against his debt in eight years.

The hall took it the way bonded halls took things. There was a long silence, and then a noise, a sort of collective exhalation like a fan spinning down, and then the arguments began, small ones, everywhere at once, people turning to their neighbours. A woman was crying near the slot. Somebody was laughing. Danek Sorell, beside Maren, said "Sorrel," three or four times, as if he were trying to remember where he had heard the word.

"I've got kids in the Camp, Chief," he said. "Three. Tamar's in the third shaft. What's he mean, apply?"

"He means you can't."

"So we go to Sorrel and they stay."

"That's what he means."

"For how long?"

Maren didn't answer him.

Up on the gallery, Quist had stepped back, and Hale had stepped forward with his slate under his arm and his soft patient voice raised just enough. Order, the warden said. Questions to be raised with section chiefs, who would be meeting with the factor individually over the next days. Dips to continue as rostered. Air rations unchanged. Any bonded found organising, or refusing duty, or damaging Combine property, would have their accounts reviewed, with all that that implied, and their families' accounts likewise. He said it gently. Bram Kovač and two other trusties stood at the foot of the gallery stair with their shock-sticks loose in their hands, looking at nobody in particular.

"Organising," said Teska, loudly, from the bench near the drum where she always sat. "What's that, organising? Is that talking? Is it organising if I talk?"

"Grandam," said Hale, with great kindness, "nobody's going to stop you talking."

"No," said Teska. "You never have." She turned her head toward the hall, with her one good eye. "I've been on this ship sixty-one years. I came out on her in pieces. I've buried two husbands and a daughter on that line. Now they say they're going to sink her." She spat on the deck. "Gall can have her. Gall's had everything else."

Some of the hall laughed. Most of it didn't.

Maren found her brother at the edge of the crowd near the riggers' stair, where he always was, where the talk was. He was flushed and bright-eyed and talking fast to a knot of young riggers, his hands moving, and when he saw her coming he broke off and came to meet her with his arms open like a man at a party.

"Sorrel," he said. "Did you hear him? Sorrel, Maren."

"I heard him."

"Off this rock. Off this ship. Do you know what they've got on Sorrel? Real gravity, on the forts. They've got markets. They've got—God, I don't know what they've got, I've never been anywhere. Neither have you." He caught her by the shoulders. His breath smelled of the illegal still in the dock section, which meant he had been celebrating since before the hall. "We're getting out. Both of us. Thirty years they've had us hanging off this planet on a string and now they're letting us go."

"They're not letting us go."

"Same thing."

"It isn't, Abel."

He looked at her face, and some of the brightness went out of his. He let go of her shoulders.

"You've got that look," he said. "Your chief's look. Like something's about to walk."

"Something is."

"What?"

She looked at him. Her little brother, twenty-nine years old, the same height as her and twice as handsome, with their mother's eyes and their father's absence and a laugh that had been getting him out of trouble since he was four. Everyone on the *Patience* liked Abel. Everyone on the *Patience* was owed money by Abel, or owed him a favour, or both. He had never kept a credit in his account for longer than a week in his life. It was, Lusine had said to her sister, restful.

"Not here," Maren said.

## The factor's cabin

[
  Place: #The factor's cabin
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Aurelian Quist
  When: same day
]

The factor sent for her two hours after the hall.

He had taken Moberly's old cabin, forward of the bridge, and done nothing to it; it was still Moberly's, with the fat man's soft chairs and his hideous picture of a sailing ship on a green sea, and his stains on the carpet. Quist sat behind Moberly's desk with a single slate in front of him and a cup of real coffee, which Maren could smell from the door, and he got up when she came in, which no factor had ever done.

"Chief Tsolo. Thank you. Sit down, please."

She stood.

He looked at her for a moment, and then sat down again himself, without fuss. "As you like. Would you like coffee? It's real. I'm told there hasn't been any on this ship in ten years."

"No."

"No." He turned the cup a little on its saucer. "I'll come to the point, then. I need a tether-chief to manage the end of the season. You're the senior chief on this ship and the crew listen to you, which is not true of the others, or of the warden, though he'd like to think so. I need the last dips run clean, the last loads brought up, and the *Patience* ready for decommission on the twenty-ninth. I need the crew calm while it happens. I can offer you something for it."

"What?"

"An assessment," said Quist. "When you reach Sorrel. As supervisory rather than general labour. It would mean a different rate against your account, a different kind of work. You'd be off the gun-decks." He paused. "You'd keep your air."

"And Abel?"

"Your brother? I could put him on the same assessment. I'd have to bend it."

"And Danek Sorell's three children in the third shaft?"

Quist did not answer at once. He looked down at the slate in front of him, and then up at her again, and his long lined face did not change, but something in it went still, the way a man goes still when he hears a sound in the dark.

"You've read the dispatch," he said.

Maren said nothing.

"The advance dispatch. On the beacon. The one that came ahead of me." He nodded slowly. "There was a runner on the relay. The one who died on the line. I'm told she worked the relay on her off-shifts."

"Lusine Arda."

"Lusine Arda. And now you've read it." He sat back. "I wondered how long it would take. I wondered who it would be. I'd have said you, if you'd asked me. I've been in this work a long time, Chief Tsolo, and there's always one."

"Then you know."

"I know what I was sent to do." He picked up the coffee and drank some of it and put it down. "The Corva operation is finished. It's been finished for two years; it just hasn't been told. The Sorrel yards went over to catalysed helium last spring, and the price of deuterium in the outer lanes fell by two-thirds in a quarter, and there is no longer any way to bring Corva gas up the lane and sell it for more than it costs to get it out of the sky. So the Board has done what boards do. It has cut its losses. The ship is worth nothing; it goes into Gall. The crew are worth something, to the Levy; they go to Sorrel. The Camp is worth less than nothing. The Camp is a liability. So the Camp is left in place, and its accounts are sold to a discount house in Halloran for a fraction of a mark on the hundred, and the discount house will never send anybody to collect, because there will be nobody to collect from."

"Two thousand households."

"Twenty-one hundred."

"You're going to leave them to die."

"I'm going to leave them," said Quist, "to be somebody else's problem. That's the form of words. I didn't write it." He looked at her steadily. "I want you to understand that I'm not a stupid man, Chief Tsolo. Nor a cruel one, I think. I've done this before. I closed Bellamy Deep in eighty-four and the Hollow Moons in eighty-seven. Bellamy went quietly; most of them got out on the last ore boats, as stowaways, and I looked the other way. The Hollow Moons didn't go quietly, and it didn't help them. It's always worse when it isn't quiet."

"And Tamsin's Rock?" said Maren.

It was a guess, a name she had heard in the infirmary once, late, from Ekene, and never asked about. She saw it land. He did not flinch; he was too old and too practised to flinch. But he put the cup down very carefully, so that it made no sound on the saucer, and he took his hand away from it.

"I wasn't the factor at Tamsin's Rock," he said.

"But you know what happened there."

"Everybody in the Combine knows what happened there. That's what Tamsin's Rock is for." He looked at her for a long moment. "If you know what happened at Tamsin's Rock, then you know why I'm telling you the truth. Because you're going to do something, Chief Tsolo. I can see it in you. You're going to talk to your crew, and the crew are going to talk to the Camp, and somebody is going to have an idea. And I'd rather you did it knowing exactly what you're up against than not." He spread his hands on the desk. "The *Admonition* is two weeks behind me. She has sixty marines and a torpedo bay. And I have contingency authority. Whatever you do, you can't make it end any better than it's going to end now. You can only make it worse."

"For who?"

"For everyone," said Quist. "For you. For them. For me." He paused. "Most of all for them."

Maren looked at him across Moberly's desk, at his plain coat and his good coffee and his long sorrowful face. She thought of Lusine's voice going faint on channel four. *I don't think it was the clip.*

"Was it you?" she said. "Lusine. Did you have her line cut?"

For the first time Quist looked genuinely surprised.

"Her line was cut?"

"Her clip. Somebody took the spring two-thirds through."

He was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "No. It wasn't me. I didn't know her name until this morning." He looked down at the slate. "But I'd look closer to home, if I were you. I'd look at who on this ship would have wanted the Combine to be grateful to them. There's always somebody. There's always somebody who'd like to be on the right list." He looked up. "The offer stands, Chief. Supervisory. You and your brother. Think about it."

"I've thought about it," said Maren, and walked out.

## The blister

[
  Place: #The blister
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Abel Tsolo
  When: same day
]

She found Abel at the third bell of the night, in the blister.

The blister was a dome of thick old glass on the *Patience*'s dorsal hull, a relic of the days when skimmers had carried passengers who wanted to look at things. It was cold, and badly lit, and the crew used it for courting and drinking and the kind of conversations that couldn't be had anywhere else, and nobody ever cleaned it. Abel was sitting on the floor with his back against the frame and a bottle of dock-still between his knees, looking up. He didn't turn round when she came in. He held the bottle up over his shoulder.

She took it, and drank, and gave it back, and sat down beside him.

Above them, through the scratched glass, Gall filled half the sky, ochre and purple, a storm the size of a world turning slowly on its southern limb. Above the storm, in the black, the Lamp hung like a thumbprint of light. It was brighter than it had been three days ago. You could see the tail properly now, a long faint fan of pale green going away from the sun, so delicate that if you looked straight at it it disappeared, and came back when you looked to the side.

"Teska says it takes the dead out," said Abel.

"Teska says a lot of things."

"She says it'll be back when our grandchildren are dead." He drank. "That's a long time to be a comet."

"It's not a long time for a comet."

"No." He laughed a little. "I suppose not." He held the bottle up to the glass and looked at the Lamp through it, through the cloudy brown spirit, so that it wobbled. "I keep thinking about her. Lusine. Out there. Going in."

"So do I."

"You talked to her. The whole way. I heard you." He lowered the bottle. "That was a good thing you did, Mar."

"I didn't do anything."

"You did, though." He turned his head and looked at her for the first time. His face was grey and his eyes were wet and red-rimmed, and she could not tell how much of it was the still. "You always think you didn't do anything. You do more than anybody."

She looked at him for a long moment, at his handsome tired face in the Gall-light, her brother, whom she had carried on her hip through the third shaft when their mother went into the cloud.

"Were you seeing her, Abel?"

He didn't move. He didn't look away. But she had known him all his life, and she saw the small thing happen behind his eyes, the thing that happened when he was eight and she asked him who had got into the dried fruit, the half-second of arithmetic.

"Seeing her?"

"Lusine. Odile says she was seeing somebody. Somebody funny. Somebody always in debt who didn't care."

Abel laughed. It was almost his real laugh. "That's half the riggers on the ship."

"Were you?"

"We were friendly." He shrugged. "A bit. Now and then. She was—she was good fun, Mar. She liked a joke. We'd have a drink in the lock after shift. It wasn't serious. She wasn't serious about anybody." He drank. "Why?"

"Did she tell you anything? Before she died? About the *Clement*? About a dispatch?"

"A dispatch?" He frowned. "What dispatch?"

"Did she?"

"No." He shook his head. "No. Why would she tell me about a dispatch? I'm a rigger. I can barely read the duty board." He smiled at her, the old smile, *don't worry, it's me*. "What's this about?"

Maren looked at the Lamp.

"Nothing," she said. "It's late. I'm tired."

"You're always tired."

"I'm always tired."

He put his arm around her shoulders, the way he used to when he was sixteen and had just grown taller than her and wanted her to know it, and she let him, and they sat together in the cold blister and looked at the comet. His arm was warm and heavy and familiar. His hand, where it hung over her shoulder, was shaking, very slightly, with a small fine tremor, the kind of tremor that a man gets when he has been drinking for three days because he cannot stop seeing something.

She did not say anything about it. She sat there in her brother's arm and looked at the Lamp and thought, very clearly, *he's lying to me*, and then, because she was the tether-chief and that was what tether-chiefs did, she began to do the arithmetic.
