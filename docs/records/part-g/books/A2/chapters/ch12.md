# The Register

## What the Vey family lost

[
  Place: #The Dray
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Hollis Vey @@Juno Brask
  Items: ^The Vey register:@Hollis Vey
  When: 24 April 2917
]

On the twenty-fourth, six days from the Lamp, Hollis Vey sends Cato Marchetti forward to sit with Oz in the engine space, and asks Til to come aft.

She finds Juno already there. She's sitting cross-legged on the deck beside the cradle with one of the *Dray*'s old maintenance manuals open on her knees, the thick foil one for the winch assembly, and she looks up when Til comes in and then down again, and doesn't get up. Hollis is propped higher on his pillows than usual. His face is worse. In the last two days it's gone from yellow-grey to grey, and the skin over his cheekbones has gone tight and shiny, and his breathing, through the cradle's speaker grille, has a catch in it now on every inbreath, like a hinge.

"I asked Juno to stay," he says. "I hope you don't mind. I find I'd rather say this to two people than one. It's less like confession."

Til sits on the end of the bunk.

"There's a case," says Hollis, "in the rack under the cradle. A black one. Would you?"

She finds it. It's an old document case of black hide, scuffed white at the corners, with a brass catch. Inside, sealed in clear sleeves, there are papers. Real paper, some of it, brown and soft as cloth; and foil, old foil, the grey printed kind she saw on Platform Four, in Juno's hands, in the frozen duty office.

"The top one," says Hollis.

The top one is a single sheet of foil in its sleeve, printed in the same cramped old type. A heading, with the green wheel. Columns. Most of it is codes. Near the bottom of the page, a line has been marked, very long ago, with a pen, in the margin.

*CORVA OPERATION. Status: LOST, 2391. Assets not recovered:*

*Asset 7. Sanitation Agent, Pattern H (one unit). Seized by bonded mutineers 17.xi.2391. Not recovered. Disposition unknown.*

Til reads it twice and hands it to Juno without a word. Juno reads it, her lips moving very slightly, the way her mother's used to.

"That's a loss register," says Hollis. "The Combine kept one for every operation. That's the page for Corva. It's the only page for Corva that survived the Concord's archives, and I paid more for it than I paid for my house." He breathes, with the hinge in it. "The next one down is a memorandum. From the Combine's operations board to its factors, about twelve years earlier. You should read it. Both of you. I've read it a thousand times and it doesn't get easier, so there's no point in waiting for it to."

Til reads it. It's longer, four pages. It's about a place called Tamsin's Rock, and a strike, and a factor who exercised something called Schedule H, and the results, which are given in a table. There are four thousand and eleven people in the table. There is a column headed *Time of death (est.)*, filled in by hand, by four different hands, for every one of them. At the end there's a paragraph about resettlement, and a figure for the operation's profit eighteen months later, and a sentence recommending the episode to all factors *as an example of what may be achieved by decisive use of contingency authority*.

Juno reads it after her. She reads it very slowly. When she gets to the table she stops, and Til watches her look at it, the long columns of names and times in faded ink, and sees her face change, sees her understand what kind of ledger it is.

"That's what Asset Seven is," says Hollis Vey. "That's what my family made, or bought, or had made for them; I've never found out which. That's what my family took to Corva, in twenty-three ninety-one, to use on its own workers if they didn't go quietly when they were sold. They didn't go quietly. They took it." His wet blue eyes go to the port, where the Lamp is a blaze. "And then nobody knows."

"And you think it's on the Lamp," says Til.

"I think it's on the Lamp."

"Why?"

"Because it isn't anywhere else." He smiles his too-large smile, but there's no amusement in it now. "Because the mutineers took it, and the Combine never got it back, and the Concord came for it two hundred and sixty years later in the next year the Lamp was in the sky, and the Concord's books on that year are a wall of ash. Because the dippers who came out of Corva carried a saying about the Lamp that's on the wall of a fuel depot in Gall orbit and on the wall of every Roundsman's chapel in Tarn: *what goes out comes back*. Because the Tarn docks tell a story about a hoard on a comet, and dock stories are always about something, even when they're wrong about what. Because if I'd been a dipper in twenty-three ninety-one with a thing like that in my hands and nothing I could do to kill it, and a comet going out into the cold for two hundred and sixty years passing close enough to touch, I'd have put it there." He closes his eyes. "I've had thirty years to think about it, Captain. That's where I'd have put it."

Nobody says anything. The *Dray*'s air plant coughs.

"You lied to us," says Juno. Her voice is very quiet. "You said it was your family's. Something your family lost. You let us think it was money."

"It is my family's," says Hollis. "And I did lose it. I told you no lies, child. I told you very little truth, which isn't the same." He opens his eyes and looks at her, and his face is suddenly very old and very tired. "Would you have come? Would your aunt? Through a raw lane, to fetch the thing in that table, for a dying man who wants to see it before he goes?"

"Why do you want to see it?" says Til.

"Because it's ours. Because it's the last thing of ours in the world." His hand moves on the blanket, the long bony fingers, as if feeling for something. "I don't want to sell it, Captain, if that's what you're thinking. I don't want to use it. I'm seventy-one years old and I'm dying of a disease the doctors in Tarn can name and can't spell, and I have no children and nobody to leave anything to. I want to find it. And I want it gone. Gone where nobody can ever find it again. Into the giant, with the rest of the Lamp, where it should have gone five hundred years ago." He takes a breath, the hinge catching. "My family has a long debt, Captain. I'd like to pay one line of it before I die. Just one."

## Six days

[
  Place: #The Dray
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Juno Brask @@Ozias Mbeki @@Cato Marchetti @@Hollis Vey
  When: same day
]

She tells them in the galley. All of them, Oz and Juno and Marchetti, crowded round the little table with the cups of Oz's terrible coffee; Hollis on the intercom from the cradle, listening, saying nothing. She tells them plainly, the way she'd brief a salvage: what Asset Seven is, what it did at Tamsin's Rock, what the Combine's register says, what Hollis thinks. She shows them the memorandum. Oz reads it with his lips pressed tight together and puts it down very carefully, face down, on the table.

Marchetti doesn't read it. He sits with his hands round his cup and looks at Til with his quiet attentive face and says nothing at all, and his face doesn't change, and that's the thing Til notices, afterward, when she has time to notice things: that his face doesn't change, not even a little, not even the way a face changes when it hears about four thousand dead in their bunks. As if it had already heard.

"You'd have taken us to a plague," says Juno.

She's standing against the galley hatch with her arms folded. She hasn't sat down.

"I didn't know," says Til.

"He did." Juno jerks her chin at the intercom grille. "He knew the whole time. Thirty years, he says. He brought us all this way, through that lane, with the coolant going and the shears, to fetch *that*, and he let us think it was some old company's money." She looks round the galley. "And now what? Now we land on a comet that's coming apart, and dig up a thing that kills people in their sleep, and do what with it? Carry it home? In here?" She slaps the bulkhead. "Next to the galley?"

"We don't know it's there," says Oz mildly.

"We don't know it isn't."

"No."

"Juno," says Til.

"Don't." Juno turns on her. "Don't do your voice. Your weather voice. *The line got cut.* Don't."

Til stops.

"Here's what I know," she says, after a moment, in a different voice, an ordinary one. "Oz?"

Oz brings up the Lamp on the galley screen, the long cracked body in its coma, the diagram with the dashed circle round Gall. He doesn't need to explain it again. Everybody's seen it.

"If Hollis is right," Til says, "and it's on the Lamp, then on the thirtieth the Lamp goes inside Gall's limit and comes apart. Thirty pieces, Oz says. Maybe more. Most of them go into Gall on the second. Some don't. Some get flung. Some go round and out. Some go across the moons." She looks at Juno. "You asked about that. On the nineteenth. What happens if something's on one of the bits that gets flung."

Juno says nothing.

"If it's on a piece that goes into Gall, deep, it's gone. Hollis says that's the only thing that kills it, the deep of a giant. Fine. If it's on a piece that gets flung, it goes off into the dark, and the sun warms that piece, and the ice opens, and in a week or a month it's lying out in sunlight on a lump of gravel, warming. And then it vents." She taps the screen, where Gall's moons are little marks across the comet's path. "Into a debris stream that's crossing every one of those orbits. And there are people on one of them."

The galley goes very quiet.

"What people?" says Marchetti. It's the first thing he's said.

"I don't know. I've seen a light. On Kettle's night side, on the twentieth. Nine seconds and gone. And at the Throat, the night we came out, somebody painted us with a ranging radar. One sweep." Til looks round the table. "Somebody's alive down there. Somebody's been sitting under that ice for two hundred years not saying a word on the radio, and they saw us come in, and they looked, and they put their heads down again."

"You didn't tell me," says Oz.

"I wasn't sure."

"You're sure now."

"I'm sure now."

Juno has unfolded her arms. She's looking at the diagram on the screen, at the dashed circle and the little white mark that is Kettle, sitting right across the comet's road.

"So we turn round," says Til.

They all look at her.

"That's the sensible thing," she says. "That's what I'd do on any job. We've been paid ten thousand. The client lied about the cargo. The cargo's lethal. The site's coming apart under us in six days, and there's a repossession cutter sitting off our stern waiting to see what we bring up. Any salvager on the Ring would turn round, and call it a bad job, and nobody'd blame her." She looks at Juno. "And you'd go back to the counting-house, and I'd go back to hauling scrap, and the *Dray* would be paid off, and we'd be where we were. And whatever's on that comet goes where it goes."

"And the people on Kettle," says Juno.

"And the people on Kettle breathe what they breathe."

Juno looks at her for a long moment. It's the look from the counting-house, from the fourth desk from the end, black and level and without any expression at all; only it isn't, quite, now. There's something under it. Til can't read it. She's never been able to read it.

"Is that what you want to do?" says Juno.

Til opens her mouth to say yes.

It's what she always says. It's what she's said for eleven years, on every job, every time the money and the risk came out on the wrong sides of the line. *Cut it. Call it. Go home.* She's good at it. She's alive because she's good at it. And she sits at the little galley table with her niece standing against the hatch and Oz's big soft face beside her and the dying man breathing through the grille, and she opens her mouth to say yes, and what comes out is: "No."

Juno blinks.

"No," says Til again, as if trying it out. "No. I don't." She looks at the screen. "I'm tired of things going where they go."

Oz lets out a long breath through his nose, and folds his delicate hands on the table, and says, very quietly, in the Roundsman cant, a word Til knows: the one you say at the chapel brazier when you put the salt in. It means something like *so be it*, or *on its way*.

Marchetti says nothing. He's looking at his coffee.

"We go on," says Til. "We land. We find it, if it's there. And then we work out how to put it in Gall, deep, where it can't come back." She looks round. "Anybody who wants off can have the cutter. I'll signal Mother."

Nobody moves.

"Thank you," says the intercom grille, in Hollis Vey's thin dry voice. "Thank you, Captain."

## A falling mountain

[
  Place: #The Dray
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Ozias Mbeki @@Juno Brask @@Cato Marchetti
  When: same day
]

In the engine space afterward, Oz says, "How?"

He's lying on his back under the coolant manifold with a lamp in his teeth, re-seating the sleeve he put on in the lane, which has been weeping. Til's sitting on the deck by his feet passing him tools. It's the only place on the *Dray* where two people can talk without being heard: the core's hum covers everything, and the hatch is heavy.

"How what?"

"How do you put a thing like that in Gall. Deep. Hollis says deep. Deep's a thousand kilometres. Two. We can't go a thousand kilometres into Gall, Til. The *Dray*'d be a crushed can at fifty. And if it wakes up on the way down, in the top of the clouds, it vents there, and it's sitting in Gall's upper air for a thousand years for anybody who ever dips the giant again."

"Hollis said that?"

"His register said it. One of the papers in the case. A factor's note. I read it while you were telling them." Oz takes the lamp out of his mouth. "*You'd have to get it there cold. Faster than it can wake.*" He puts the lamp back. "Somebody knew. Five hundred years ago. They just didn't have a way."

Til sits with a spanner in her hand.

"We've got a way," she says.

Oz stops working.

"The Lamp," says Til. "The pieces. Thirty of them, you said. Going into Gall on the second, at sixty kilometres a second. What happens to a mountain of ice that hits a gas giant at sixty kilometres a second?"

Oz is silent for a long moment, under the manifold.

"It goes in," he says slowly. "It goes in very deep, very fast. A big piece—a kilometre—it'd go down hundreds of kilometres before it even started to break up. Before it even felt warm, inside. It'd be a fireball the size of Tarn on the cloud-tops and the core of it'd be down in the deep before the flash was over." He slides out from under the manifold and sits up, the lamp in his hand, and looks at her. "A falling mountain."

"A falling mountain," says Til. "We don't have to take it anywhere. The Lamp's taking it. We just have to make sure it's on the right piece. The big one. The one that goes straight in, not the ones that graze and skip and go out across the moons." She turns the spanner over in her hands. "If it's buried in a little bit, we move it. Dig it out, cold, and carry it, cold, to the big bit, and bury it again. If it's on a bit that's going to graze, we push."

"Push."

"The *Dray*'s a tug, Oz. That's what she does. She pushes things."

Oz looks at her for a long time. Then he starts to laugh. It's a big soft wheezing laugh, his real one, the one she hasn't heard since Cinderhaven, and it goes on for a while and makes him cough.

"You're mad," he says.

"I know."

"Mags'd have liked it."

"I know that too," says Til.

She goes forward afterward, toward the cockpit, past the galley, past the cargo bay. The bay's dark except for the work-lamps on the ribs, and the comms panel by the forward hatch, which is the *Dray*'s secondary, the one Oz uses to talk to the winch when he's working the boom. Its screen is lit. She doesn't notice that at the time. She notices it afterward, when Juno tells her.

Juno's in the bay. She's in the shadow behind the third cable drum, sitting on the deck with her knees up, in Oz's old jumper; she came down here to be by herself, she says later, after the galley, because the warm bunk was too close to the cradle and she didn't want to hear the old man breathe. She's been sitting there in the dark for twenty minutes when Cato Marchetti comes into the bay from the aft hatch, without a sound, and goes straight to the comms panel by the forward hatch, and stands in front of it, and types.

She can't see what he types. She can see the screen, over his shoulder. She can see the little icon in its corner light up, the one shaped like a narrow cone: a tightbeam, the *Dray*'s laser link, which only talks to one ship at a time, and only to a ship you aim it at. She can see the bearing it takes, swinging round, settling. Astern. Two hundred thousand kilometres astern, where the long black hull with the ram bow sits in the comet's light, running dark, waiting.

Marchetti types for about a minute. Then he waits. A line of text comes back, short. He reads it, and clears the screen, and the cone icon goes out, and he turns away from the panel and walks out of the bay the way he came, and as he passes the third cable drum, Juno says afterward, he doesn't look down at the shadow behind it, not even a glance; and she's never been sure, then or ever, whether that means he didn't know she was there, or that he did.
