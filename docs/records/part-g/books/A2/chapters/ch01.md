# The Dip

## The line

[
  Place: #The tether deck
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Lusine Arda:dead @@Odile Arda @@Abel Tsolo
  Items: ^Maren's hook-knife:@Maren Tsolo ^Tether-chief's coat:@Maren Tsolo
  When: 2 November 2391

The line was singing in E-flat when Lusine Arda called the ice.

Maren Tsolo heard it before the board showed it: a thickening in the hum that came up through the gallery floor and into the soles of her boots, the note the tether made when something on it had gone heavy. Forty kilometres of carbon braid ran down from the drum beneath her feet, out through the keel slot of the *Patience* and down, always down, the tether deck had no other direction, into the ochre murk of Gall, where the Bucket rode the cloud-tops at seven kilometres a second and swallowed weather.

"Runner Two," Lusine said on the line channel. "I've got rime at eleven-four. Thick. It's growing while I watch it."

"How thick?"

"Thumb and a half. Feathering on the windward side."

Maren put two fingers on the tension trace. It was climbing, slowly, the way a fever climbs. Rime on the line meant weight, and weight in the wrong place meant the line would start to oscillate, and oscillation on a forty-kilometre tether was the kind of thing that turned up in the Combine's quarterly loss summaries as a single line of figures. Equipment, one Bucket. Personnel, as listed.

"Clear it," she said. "Go slow. Runner One, hold at six and watch your sister."

"Holding," said Odile, who was nineteen and had never in her life held at anything. Her crawler sat on the line six kilometres down, a red dot on the board, and Maren watched the dot and did not trust it.

The tether gallery was a long room with a long window, and the window looked at nothing useful. You saw the drum below you, the size of a cathedral, turning; you saw the slot it fed into; and past the slot, if you leaned, a curve of Gall so close and so large that it was not a planet at all but a weather, a floor of ochre and old-bruise purple that went on until it bent away into black. Nobody leaned. The board was where the line was. The board was where you saw it breathe.

Maren had been tether-chief for nine years and on the line for twenty-six before that, and she had stopped thinking of the board as a picture of anything. It was a body. The long green trace of the tension was its pulse. The fat amber band of the Bucket's intake was its belly filling. The little coloured dots that crawled along its spine were her runners, and the numbers beside them were their air.

"Starting on it," Lusine said. Her voice had the slight breathless flatness of someone working hard in a suit. "Pick's biting. It's coming off in plates."

"Don't let them hit the crawler."

"I'm not new, Chief."

"You're not old either."

A laugh on the channel. It was a good channel, the line channel. Everyone on shift could hear everyone else, and in a dip, which was six hours of nothing followed by four seconds of everything, the voices were company. Down on the drum floor, Maren could see her riggers at their stations under the floodlights, little figures in grey coveralls, each one bent over a brake housing or a sensor head. Her brother was one of them. She could pick him out without trying, the way you pick out your own handwriting: Abel at the number three brake, leaning on the housing with the elbow that wasn't meant for leaning, his helmet pushed back off his forehead against regulations, talking to somebody. Abel was always talking to somebody.

"Abel," she said on the floor channel. "Helmet."

He looked up at the gallery window, though he couldn't have seen her through the glare, and pulled the helmet down with an elaborate, injured slowness that got a laugh from the rigger beside him.

The tension trace climbed another hair.

"Eleven-four's clear," said Lusine. "Moving down to eleven-six. There's more. It's in bands, Chief, like the line's been dipped in it."

"Weather," said Maren. She looked at the met strip along the top of the board. There was a storm coming up out of the south under the Bucket's track, a bruise the riggers had been calling Old Purple since the morning, and it had been throwing moisture into the high cloud for an hour. She had been watching it the way you watch a man at the next table who might be drunk. "It's the Purple. We'll be out of its shadow in forty minutes."

"Forty minutes is a lot of ice."

"Then work faster."

That was what a tether-chief said. You said it with a little edge on it so they'd know you meant it and a little warmth under it so they'd know you were there. Maren had learned the voice from her own chief, Hesketh, who had gone into Gall the year Abel was born; and Hesketh had learned it from the woman before her, and so on back up the line to the first tether-chiefs of the first skimmers, who had probably said it to people who were also going to die.

The *Patience* was sixty-one years old. She had been built for the Combine in a yard four lanes away and towed to Corva in pieces, and she had been dipping Gall ever since: two dips a day, ten months of the year, with the drum shut down only in the dead season, when Gall's southern storms made the cloud-tops too violent even for the Bucket. She carried three hundred bonded crew. On the ice of Kettle, Gall's white moon, the Combine kept two thousand more families in the warrens of Kettle Camp, refining what the *Patience* brought up and breathing what the refinery left over. Every one of them was in debt to the Combine for the air in their lungs. Every one of them had been born in debt, or would die in it, or both. Maren had been born in the Camp. Her mother had been a runner. Her mother had gone into Gall from fourteen kilometres down when Maren was fifteen and Abel was three, and the Combine had added the cost of the lost crawler to the family account, and Maren had paid it off at nineteen, and then started on her own.

You did not think about that in a dip. You thought about the trace.

"Eleven-six clear," Lusine said. "Eleven-eight is—oh, that's ugly. That's a fist of it, Chief."

"Can you take it?"

"I can take it."

The trace jumped.

It was not much of a jump. A bump. The kind of thing the line did ten times a dip when the Bucket hit a pocket of thicker air and bit. But the note under Maren's boots changed with it, dropped half a tone and wavered, and she had her hand on the damper before she knew why.

"Shear," said the met tech from the end of the gallery, too late. "Shear under the Bucket, the Purple's throwing—"

"I see it." Maren rolled the damper on. Down in the slot the brakes bit and the drum shuddered and every rigger on the floor grabbed something. The trace kinked, jumped again, and began, very slowly, to make a wave. "Runners, lock down. Lock down now. Line's going to walk."

"Locked," said Odile at once.

"Locking," said Lusine. "I'm—Chief, the crawler's bucking, I can't get the—"

"Get your clamps on and get flat."

"Clamps on. Clamps are on."

The wave went down the line. You could not see it from the gallery. You could only watch it on the trace, a long green swell running out from the drum, and know that forty kilometres away the Bucket was yawing in the cloud like a fish on a hook, and that at every point between, the braid was flexing in a long, slow, enormous S that would come back up again, and then go down again, until the dampers ate it or it ate the dampers. Maren had seen a line walk once that killed seven people and the Bucket. She had been a runner then. She had been on the line. She had held on.

"Second pulse coming up," she said, as calm as the floor under a bed. "Lusine. Odile. It'll be worse at you than at me. Hold on and count. It goes by."

"Counting," said Odile, and began to count aloud, which was what Maren had taught her.

Lusine said nothing. On the board her dot was steady, her air steady, her clamps green.

The swell came back up the line and passed eleven-eight, and Maren felt it pass in the drum, a heave like a ship going over a wave. The trace spiked. Then there was a noise on the line channel that was not a word, a small hard sound, like a cough or a stone dropped on a deck.

"Lusine."

"Chief." Lusine's voice was quite clear. "Chief, I'm off."

Maren looked at the board. Lusine's dot was where it had been. The crawler's dot. The crawler was still on the line, clamped and green. Beside it, separating so slowly that for a moment it seemed to be a fault in the display, was a second, smaller mark, the personal beacon in Lusine's suit, and it was moving away.

"Clip," said Lusine. "My clip went. Harness clip. I'm off the crawler. I'm—I've got nothing to grab, Chief, the line's gone past me."

"Stay on this channel."

"I'm on it."

The thing about a tether is that it hangs. It is not in orbit, except at one point; every part of it below the *Patience* moves more slowly than an orbit at that height would need, and is held up only by the line itself. A runner who comes off the line comes off moving too slowly to stay up. She does not fall the way a stone falls on a world. She falls the way a dream falls: gently, a few metres a second at first, and then more, and then more, away and down along a long curve that ends in Gall.

Maren knew all of this the way she knew her own teeth.

"Gull," she said on the command channel. "Status on the Gull."

"In the cradle, Chief." The dock officer sounded sick. "Port thruster's still stripped, we've got it open, it's—forty minutes to button it, an hour—"

"Then button it."

"Chief, by the time—"

"Button it."

"Chief," said Lusine. "Don't."

Maren closed her eyes, once, briefly, and opened them again on the trace. It was still walking. The second pulse was going back down toward the Bucket and there would be a third, smaller, and a fourth, and Odile was at six kilometres counting aloud in a voice going higher with every number.

"Odile," Maren said. "Keep counting."

"That's my sister. That's my—"

"I know. Keep counting. You stay clamped. If you come off that crawler I will put you on report and then I will kill you myself. Count."

"Twenty-two," said Odile, and choked, and said, "twenty-three."

On the floor below the gallery the riggers had stopped working. They were standing at their stations with their faces turned up toward the window as if Maren could do something, as if she were a god in a glass box with her hands on the weather; and she could do nothing, so she did the only thing there was to do, which was run the line. She fed the damper in, and fed it out again, and listened to the drum, and kept the line from killing anyone else.

"It's quite nice actually," said Lusine, a little while later. "Out here. You can see the whole of the Purple. It's got a hole in the middle like an eye. Did you know that? You never see it from the line."

"I didn't know that."

"It's looking at me."

"It's not looking at you. It's weather."

"Everything's weather to you, Chief." A pause. A breath. "My air's good. Six hours on the bottle."

"Good."

"Not that I'll need six hours."

Maren didn't answer that. The dock officer came back on the command channel to say that the port thruster was out of its housing and the replacement was being walked down from stores, and Maren said, "Good," to him as well, and the word went dead in her mouth.

Lusine Arda was twenty-four years old. She had come up from the Camp at sixteen with her sister on her hip, practically, and been a rigger for three years and a runner for five. She did the comms relay on the off-shifts for the extra air-credit. She had a laugh like a dropped tray. She had been Maren's best runner for two years, and Maren had been meaning to tell her so and had not, because you didn't, because a runner who thought she was the best was a runner who went down the line too fast.

"Chief," Lusine said, after a time. Her voice had gone quieter. The channel was beginning to fuzz at the edges, the way it did when somebody was getting far from the antenna. "Chief, I want to tell you something. Not on this channel."

"Switch to four."

A click. Maren switched. Channel four was the chief's private, unlogged, a habit from the old days when tether-chiefs had needed somewhere to swear.

"Go."

"I read something," said Lusine. "On the relay. I wasn't meant to. There's a dispatch coming in on the *Clement*, it came ahead of her on the beacon, and it's—Chief, it's about all of us. I told someone. I shouldn't have told. And now my clip—"

The fuzz rose up and swallowed a sentence.

"Say again. Lusine, say again."

"—don't think it was the clip, I think it was—"

"Lusine."

"It's getting warm, Chief." Very faint now. "That's funny. I thought it'd be cold."

The beacon mark on the board touched the upper edge of the cloud.

"I can see the Lamp," said Lusine Arda, from very far away. "Did you know? It's coming round. It's just above the Purple. Teska says it takes the dead—"

And the channel was empty, and stayed empty, and Maren sat with her hand on the damper and her eyes on the trace for another four hours and fifty minutes, until the Bucket was full and the dip was done.

## Given to Gall

[
  Place: #The Drum
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Odile Arda @@Abel Tsolo @@Corwin Hale @@Grandam Teska @@Danek Sorell
  Items: ^Lusine's clip:@Maren Tsolo

They reeled Lusine's crawler up with the line.

It came in through the slot at the end of the dip, riding the braid into the drum like a beetle on a string, and the riggers stood back from it as if it were hot. It was not hot. It was rimed white from its long ride in the shadow, and its clamps were still locked, and its little green lights were still green, because nothing had ever been wrong with it. The harness strap that had held Lusine to it was still clipped to its saddle ring. The other end of the strap, where her harness should have been, hung loose.

Maren went down the gallery ladder and across the floor and stood in front of it.

Nobody said anything. The floor was full of people. The off-shift had come in, and the galley hands, and riggers from the Bucket crews, and half of the dock section, so that the drum hall, which was the only room on the *Patience* big enough to hold the whole crew, was holding most of it. The drum itself had stopped turning. Without its noise the hall sounded wrong, too large, full of small sounds: boots, breathing, the tick of the frost coming off the crawler as the warm air reached it.

"Get me a light," Maren said.

Danek Sorell gave her his hand-lamp. Danek was the biggest rigger on the line, a man with hands like shovels who sang in the shower block and was not singing now. Maren crouched and put the light on the saddle ring.

The clip was a dog clip, the kind every runner wore: a hooked steel jaw with a spring-loaded gate, so that it could be snapped on to a ring one-handed in gloves. The gate was missing. The jaw was there, and the hinge pin, and the stub of the spring, but the gate itself had gone, and the spring stub ended in a face that was not torn. Steel that fails under shock tears. It goes ragged and grey and granular, like the inside of a broken biscuit. This was bright for two-thirds of its width, a clean flat shine like the face of a coin, and only the last third was grey.

Maren looked at it for a long moment. Then she unhooked the clip from the ring, closed her hand over it, and stood up.

"Gate failure," she said. "Spring went."

"It was the shear," somebody said behind her. "That shear was a bastard."

"It was the shear."

Odile Arda had come in off the line twenty minutes after the end of the dip, stripped her suit in the lock without waiting for the dresser and walked into the drum hall in her undersuit, sweat-black down the spine, and now she was standing at the edge of the crowd with her arms wrapped around herself, staring at the crawler as if it might at any moment open and let her sister out. Grandam Teska was beside her. Teska was eighty-three and had been on the *Patience* since the year it was towed in; she had one good eye and one milk-white one and she held Odile's elbow in a claw of a hand that was steadier than it looked.

"She's gone into Gall," said Teska, to no one in particular. "Gall has her."

"Gall has her," the hall said back, the low ragged chorus, like a congregation that has forgotten what church it's in.

That was all the funeral a dipper got when the line took them. When you died on the ship there was a proper giving: the body was wrapped and walked down the line in a crawler and released at forty kilometres into the cloud, so that the Deep could have it. When the line took you, Gall had already done the taking. There was nothing to walk down. There was only the crawler coming home empty, and somebody chalking the name on the drum.

Teska did the chalking. She had to be helped up the drum ladder to do it. The drum's outer skin was covered in names, layer on layer of them, the old ones rubbed back to ghosts by sixty years of hands and shoulders and the new ones bright: dates, names, sometimes a joke. GALL TOOK MY HUSBAND AND HE WASN'T EVEN GOOD. Teska wrote LUSINE ARDA 2391 in her slow shaking capitals and underneath it, after a moment's thought, a small round shape with a line trailing off it, like a seed with a tail.

"What's that?" said one of the young riggers.

"That's the Lamp," said Teska. "It's coming round. You'll see it in a week, if you go up the blister. It came round when my grandmother's grandmother was a girl and it'll come round when your grandchildren are dead. It goes out past the cold and it comes back. It takes the dead out with it and it brings them back."

"That's a story," said the rigger.

"Everything's a story," said Teska, climbing down. "You're a story. You're a short one."

Somebody laughed, the way you laugh at funerals. The hall began to break up into its small groups and its quiet talk. Maren stood by the crawler with the clip in her fist and watched the room.

She watched the riggers, who were looking at the floor. She watched the dock crew, who were looking at each other. She watched Odile, who was looking at nothing. And she watched her brother Abel, who had come across from the number three brake and was standing a little apart with a bulb of tea he was not drinking, his face greyish under the floodlights, and who, when he felt her looking, lifted the tea in a small salute and gave her the grin he had been giving her since he was four, the one that said *don't worry, it's me*; and then looked away, a fraction too soon.

"Chief Tsolo."

The voice came from behind her, and the hall, in its small way, went quiet again.

Corwin Hale was the chief warden of the *Patience*. He was a bonded man like everyone else, born in the Camp, carrying a family account like everyone else; but he was a trusty, which meant that he had been given a shock-stick, a cabin to himself, a double air ration and the job of keeping the other bonded in order on the Combine's behalf, and in return the Combine took a little off his account every year and nobody liked him very much. He did not seem to need to be liked. He was a big, slow, patient man with a soft voice and a shaven head, and he carried a slate under his arm the way a priest carries a book.

"Warden."

"I'm sorry for your loss." He meant the crew. He said it to her because she was the chief. "Arda was a good runner."

"She was."

"I'll need the incident report from you. And the dip log. I've filled in what I can." He held out the slate. "Sign where it's marked."

Maren took it. The form was already completed in Hale's neat hand. *Personnel loss, tether operations. Runner L. Arda. Cause: harness detachment during line oscillation; runner did not secure secondary lanyard per Standing Order 14.* Below that, the line items. *Personal beacon, lost. Suit, runner's, lost. Air, six hours, lost. Crawler, recovered, inspection and recertification.* Each with its figure. The total was transferred to Account ARDA, L., and since Account ARDA, L. had no further earning capacity, it had been carried forward, as the Combine's standing terms provided, to the next of kin. ARDA, O.

Maren read it twice.

"She didn't have a secondary," she said. "Nobody has a secondary. We haven't been issued secondary lanyards since before you came aboard. You know that."

"Standing Order 14 says a runner will secure a secondary lanyard."

"Standing Order 14 says a runner will be issued one."

"Then the shortfall is the Combine's," said Hale reasonably, "and the factor can strike the line. He'll be aboard in an hour. You can raise it with him. In the meantime the form has to be filed with the dip, and the dip has to be filed before the *Clement* docks, or the load goes unregistered and the whole crew gets docked a day." He paused. "That would be a pity. Today."

Maren looked at him. He looked back with his patient, mild, slightly sorrowful face, as if he too were a victim of the form.

She signed. Of course she signed. You signed, or the crew got docked a day, and a day's air-credit to a rigger with two children in the Camp was a week of thin meals. Hale took the slate back and tucked it under his arm.

"And the clip," he said. "For the inspection."

"What clip?"

"The harness clip. Arda's. It'll need to go to the factor's engineer with the crawler. For the report."

"Spring failed," Maren said. "Gate's gone. It's in Gall with her."

Hale's eyes rested on her closed fist for a moment, and on her face, and then he nodded. "I'll note that," he said, and went away across the hall with his slate, and Bram Kovač, the youngest and hardest of his trusties, peeled himself off the wall by the door and went after him.

Maren put her fist in her pocket and left it there.

## The factor

[
  Place: #The Drum

  When: same day
]

The *Clement* came in at the end of second shift, out of the black on the Kettle side, a long dull hull with the Halloran-Vey wheel painted on her flank in green, and mated to the *Patience*'s forward collar with a bang that went through every deck. She was the supply ship. Four times a year she came up from the Throat, the lane mouth that hung in the dark behind Gall, with spare parts and medicines and new bonded and the Combine's paper, and went back with deuterium. She was eleven days early. Everyone knew she was eleven days early. Nobody knew why.

The factor came aboard at the change of shift, when he could address both watches at once, and he addressed them in the drum hall, under Teska's chalk.

He was not what Maren had expected. The last factor had been a fat cheerful thief called Moberly who had sold the infirmary's painkillers to the Camp and been recalled for it. This one was tall and spare and grey, fifty or a little past it, in a plain dark coat with the green wheel on the lapel and nothing else to say he was anyone. He had a long face, lined in the wrong places for a man who smiled. He stood on the drum gallery with Hale on one side of him and a marine sergeant on the other, a stocky woman in Combine grey with a sidearm, and he looked down at three hundred tired bonded and waited for them to stop talking. It took a while. He waited without impatience.

"My name is Aurelian Quist," he said, when they had. "I'm the Combine's factor for Corva, from today. I've been told there was a death on the line this morning. I'm sorry for it."

He said it as if he were. That was the first thing Maren noticed about him: that he said things as if he meant them, and it made the hall uneasy, because no factor in living memory had meant anything.

"I won't keep you," Quist said. "You've had a long day and a bad one. I'll say only this. The Combine has been reviewing the Corva operation, as it reviews all its operations from time to time. That review is now complete. There will be changes. In the next few days I'll be speaking to the chiefs and the wardens about what those changes mean for each of you, and I'll speak to all of you again before the end of the month. Until then, the dips go on as before. Thank you."

That was all. He turned and went down the gallery steps with the sergeant behind him, and Hale stood a moment longer looking out at the hall, and then followed.

The hall was very quiet.

"Changes," said Danek Sorell, at last, beside Maren. "What's that mean, changes?"

"It means changes," Maren said.

"Good changes or bad changes?"

She looked at him. Danek had been on the line eleven years. He had a wife in the Camp and three children and a laugh you could hear from the slot, and he was asking her what kind of changes the Combine made, as if there had ever been more than one kind.

"Go and get some sleep, Danek," she said.

## The Lamp in the port

[
  Place: #The infirmary
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Ekene Adu
  Items: ^Lusine's clip:@Maren Tsolo
  When: same day
]

The infirmary was the one place on the *Patience* that was ever warm. Ekene kept it that way on purpose, cooking the heat exchangers past their rating and fiddling the logs, because, he said, sick people healed better warm and well people talked more truthfully; and it was true that you heard things in the infirmary at night that you did not hear anywhere else on the ship. It was a narrow white room with eight bunks, a dispensary cage, a surgical bay behind a curtain, and a single round port the size of a dinner plate that looked out along the hull toward Gall's limb. Ekene had hung a dried flower in a bag over the port. Nobody knew where he had got a flower.

He was sitting at the dispensary counter when she came in, writing up the day by the light of a single lamp. He looked up, and took off his reading lenses, and looked at her for a long moment without saying anything. Then he got up and came round the counter and put his arms round her, and she stood in them with her face against the stiff cotton of his coat and her fist in her pocket and did not cry, because she had not cried in nine years and was not about to start now in front of a dispensary cage.

"I heard it," he said into her hair. "Half the ship was on the line channel. I heard you talking to her."

"I didn't do anything."

"You talked to her."

"I couldn't do anything, Ekene. The Gull's thruster was stripped. It's been stripped three weeks. I put in for the part three times." She let go of him and stepped back. "I ran the line. That's what I did. I sat there and ran the dip and I listened to her go."

"If you'd stopped the dip—"

"If I'd stopped the dip the line would have walked and we'd have lost Odile as well. And the Bucket. And the drum, maybe. And they'd have docked the whole crew for the load." She sat down on the end of the nearest bunk. "That's the arithmetic. I did the arithmetic."

Ekene went back behind the counter and filled two cups from the urn and brought them over. It was the infirmary's tea, which was not tea at all but something he grew in a tray under the ultraviolet lamp in the surgical bay and dried and smoked, and which tasted like a wet fire. She drank it. He sat beside her on the bunk. He was fifty-two, eleven years older than she was, a big soft-spoken man with grey in his beard and the long patient hands of somebody who had spent his life doing small things carefully to people in pain. He had come to Corva on a medical contract six years ago, not bonded, which made him a strange thing on the *Patience*, a free man who could have left on any *Clement*. He had not left. Maren had asked him why, once, and he had said that he was a coward and the Combine's other postings were worse; and then, later, in the dark, that it was her.

"The factor," he said.

"You saw him?"

"He came by. Before the hall. He wanted to see the infirmary." Ekene turned his cup round in his hands. "He asked about our stock of painkillers. How many beds. Whether we had a cold store and what was in it. He was polite. He asked very good questions."

"What kind of questions?"

"The kind an auditor asks. What things cost. What could be moved." He looked at her sideways. "What do you think *changes* means, Maren?"

"I think the *Clement*'s eleven days early," Maren said. "I think nobody sends a new factor to a skimmer that's making its numbers just to say hello." She drank the rest of her cup in one swallow, burning her throat. "And I think somebody cut Lusine's line."

She took her fist out of her pocket and opened it.

Ekene looked at the clip for a long time. Then he took it from her palm, gently, the way he took everything, and turned it under the lamp, and found the stub of the gate spring and the bright clean face of the cut, two-thirds of the way through.

"A blade," he said.

"A fine one. Not a cutter. Cutters crush. That's a saw cut, or a knife that's been honed. Somebody took that spring two-thirds through, so it'd hold in calm and go in the first hard shock." She heard her own voice, very level, the tether-chief's voice. "Every runner checks her clip at the lock. You snap it, you load it, you look at the gate. You don't look at the spring. Nobody looks at the spring."

"Who could do this?"

"Anybody who could get to her harness. Anybody in the runners' lock, between her last check yesterday and the dip. Riggers. Runners. Dock crew. Wardens." She stopped. "Me."

"Not you."

"No. Not me." She took the clip back. "She said something on the way down. On four, so it's not on the log. She said she'd read a dispatch on the relay, something that came ahead of the *Clement* on the beacon. She said it was about all of us. She said she'd told someone, and she shouldn't have."

"Told who?"

"She didn't say. She went into the static." Maren closed her hand on the clip again. "She said, *I don't think it was the clip.*"

They sat for a while without talking. Through the port, over Ekene's shoulder, she could see the long curve of the *Patience*'s hull lit by Gall-shine, rust-coloured, and beyond it the edge of Gall itself, and beyond that the black. It took her a moment to see what she was looking at. There was a smudge in the black, just above the planet's limb: a faint pale patch no bigger than a thumbprint, like a breath on glass, with the barest suggestion of a tail.

"There it is," said Ekene, following her eyes. "Teska's comet."
