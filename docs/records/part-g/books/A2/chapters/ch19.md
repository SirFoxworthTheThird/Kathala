# The Purchase

## Static

[
  Place: #The tether deck
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Ekene Adu @@Odile Arda @@Grandam Teska
  When: 2 December 2391
]

For two days after the throw, Maren kept the board.

She didn't sleep on it, exactly. She sat at the tether-chief's station in the gallery with channel four open and the static hissing out of the speaker, and when she was too tired to sit she lay down on the gallery floor beside the chair, and when she woke she sat up again. Ekene brought her food. She ate some of it. Odile brought her tea. She drank it cold. The crew dipped Gall around her, twice a day, because Teska said they must, and Maren ran the dips without thinking, the way you breathe, her hands on the damper and her eyes on the trace; and between the dips she sat with the static and listened to it.

There was nothing in it. She knew there was nothing in it. The Gull's relay at the top of the shaft would have gone out of the *Patience*'s range a few hours after Abel's last words, as the Lamp went out round Gall's far side and away; and Abel himself would have stopped talking long before that, forty-one metres down in the cold, in her coat, with his knees drawn up. She knew all of that. She listened anyway.

On the second day, Ekene sat down on the floor beside her chair, with his bad shoulder strapped up where Bram Kovač's shock-stick had caught it, and said, "Maren. You have to come down."

"I'm down."

"You're in a chair in the gallery with your hand on a speaker. Come down to the drum hall. They need to see you."

"For what?"

"Because the *Admonition* is still out there," said Ekene, "and Hale's men still have the bridge and the carbines, and the crew are frightened, and they keep coming up to the gallery door and looking at you and going away again." He paused. "And because you made a promise. To Odile. In the Belly."

Maren took her hand off the speaker.

She had made it. She remembered every word. *After the Lamp. I'll put him in front of the whole drum hall and tell them what he did and what Hale did.* She had sworn it on the line, on Lusine's name chalked on the drum. Abel was gone; she could not put Abel in front of anybody. But Hale was not gone. Hale was on the bridge with his slate under his arm, mild and patient and reasonable, and nobody in the drum hall but her and Odile and Ekene and, perhaps, Teska knew what he was.

"Tomorrow," she said. "At the change of shift. Call them for tomorrow. All of them. The Camp too."

Odile, at the next station, looked round. Her face was very white.

"I'll tell them," she said.

"Tell them everything," said Maren. "I'll tell them the rest."

That night, while the crew slept and the *Patience* swung on her long orbit over the giant with the drum still and the line reeled in, the *Clement*'s little cargo boat cast off from the forward collar without anybody on the bridge logging it, and crossed eighty thousand kilometres of dark to the *Admonition*, and docked. There were two people aboard. One of them was Aurelian Quist, with his wrists still tied in front of him in a length of medical tubing. The other was Corwin Hale.

## Terms

[
  Place: #The Drum
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Corwin Hale @@Ekene Adu @@Odile Arda @@Grandam Teska @@Bram Kovac @@Aurelian Quist @@Severin Lachance:phone
  When: 3 December 2391
]

He came back at the second bell of the morning and called the drum hall himself, an hour before Maren's.

She heard it from the gallery: the warden's whistle in the corridors, three long notes, the old summons that had called the crew to the duty roster every shift for twenty years. *All hands to the drum. All hands.* By the time she came down the gallery ladder the hall was full. The crew. The Camp's delegates. The children again, on their parents' shoulders. They stood packed on the deck under the still drum and the chalked names and the Plate on its end, three hundred names cut in steel, and they were very quiet, the way a hall is quiet when it has been called by someone it is afraid of and does not yet know why.

Hale was on the gallery stair, three steps up, with his slate under his arm. Quist stood beside him, no longer tied. Bram Kovač and four other trusties stood at the foot of the stair with the two carbines.

Maren stopped at the edge of the crowd.

"I've been to see Captain Lachance," said Hale.

He didn't raise his voice. He never had to.

"I went last night, with the factor, because somebody had to, and because the *Admonition* has been sitting off our bow for nine days with twelve torpedoes and nobody has been talking to her. I've talked to her." He looked round the hall. "The Sanitation Agent is gone. You all know that. You were all there, or you heard. It's on the Lamp, and the Lamp is going out, and nobody will ever get it back. Not the Combine. Not us." He paused. "The tether-chief did that. And her brother. And I want to say before anything else that it was a brave thing, and that it was done, and that it can't be undone."

A murmur. Maren stood very still.

"Captain Lachance," said Hale, "has had orders from Halloran overnight, on the long beacon. New orders. Because there's nothing left here for the Combine to take back." He lifted the slate and read from it, without looking at the hall, the way he read the duty roster. "*The Corva operation is written off. The Combine has no further interest in the vessel* Patience, *in the Kettle installations, or in the accounts of their personnel, beyond the following. The Combine will transfer its title to the vessel and to the Corva claim, and to all installations and accounts therein, to a body representing the bonded complement of the* Patience, *for consideration of one share. The Combine will retain that share, nominally, until the transfer is complete, and will then relinquish it. No personnel will be relocated. No reprisals will be taken. The escort will withdraw.*"

The hall didn't move.

"It means," said Hale gently, "that we've bought her. The ship. The claim. The Camp. Our air." He lowered the slate. "For one share. Nobody goes to Sorrel. Nobody's account is sold. The *Admonition* goes home, and doesn't come back, and nobody comes after her. We're the Shareholders of the *Patience*. All three hundred of us. Every name on that Plate."

For a moment the hall said nothing at all. Then somebody at the back made a sound, a sort of gasp, and somebody else laughed, high and cracked, and then it went through the hall like a wave through a tether, a great ragged roar, three hundred people and twice that many from the Camp all shouting at once, hugging, weeping, children shrieking on their parents' shoulders without knowing why. Teska, on her bench by the drum, put her face in her claw of a hand.

Maren stood at the edge of the crowd and didn't move.

She was watching Hale. Hale was standing on the stair with the slate under his arm and his mild face lifted to the noise, letting it go on, letting it wash up against him; and when at last it began, slowly, to die, he lifted his hand.

"There's one more thing," he said.

The hall went quiet again in waves.

"The Combine has lost a Sanitation Agent," said Hale. "One unit. Pattern H. It's never lost one before. Somebody at Halloran has to write that down, in a register, and somebody has to answer for it. The Combine's courts require it. That's the condition." He looked down at the slate. "*The person or persons responsible for the mutiny aboard the* Patience *and for the loss of the Asset will be surrendered to the escort for trial at Halloran.*" He looked up. "That's all. One name. One person, to stand for all of us. And then it's done."

Nobody said anything.

"I didn't ask for this," said Hale. "I want you all to know that. I argued. The factor will tell you I argued." Quist, beside him, said nothing at all, and didn't look at anybody. "But Captain Lachance has his orders, and I've seen them, and they're very clear. The Combine needs a name in its register. And there's only one name it'll accept."

He turned his head and looked across the hall at Maren.

They all looked at her. Six hundred faces, more, turning in the light from the gallery windows, the way the riggers had turned their faces up to the glass on the morning Lusine went into Gall, as if she were a god in a box with her hands on the weather.

"It was her plan," Hale said softly. "Her line. Her throw. Everyone heard her say so. Everyone in Corva knows who took the key out of the cold hold."

She understood it then. All of it. The whole shape of it, at once, the way you see the whole shape of a line walking on the board when the trace finally kinks. She understood that Hale had known, on the gallery, with Odile's knife at his neck, that she would stand up in the drum hall and tell them all what he was; and that he had gone to the *Admonition* in the night and come back with this, so that he could stand on the stair first. So that if she spoke now, if she told them about Lusine and the jeweller's file and the line in the warden's ledger that said *contain*, it would not be the truth about Corwin Hale. It would be the ringleader, the one they wanted, trying to save herself by pulling the deal down on everybody else. On the Camp. On the children on their parents' shoulders.

And she understood that he was right. That it would. That half of them would believe her and half of them wouldn't, and the half that did would go for him, and his trusties had the carbines, and the *Admonition* was still off the bow with her twelve tubes; and Lachance, watching a mutiny tear itself apart over the terms of its own surrender, would do what he was told.

She had done that arithmetic before. On the line, at the board, with Lusine going faint on channel four. You ran the dip, or you lost them all.

"Maren," said Ekene, beside her, very low. "Don't."

Odile had come up on her other side. She didn't say anything. She had her line knife in her fist, at her side, where nobody could see it but Maren.

"No," said Teska, from her bench, loudly, to the whole hall. "No. Not her. Give them him." She pointed a shaking claw at Hale. "Give them the warden. He's the Company's dog. Twenty years. Everybody knows it."

"Grandam," said Hale gently, "the Combine doesn't want me."

"I'll go," said Maren.

The hall was silent.

"I'll go," she said again, a little louder, in the tether-chief's voice. "It was my plan. It was my line. I threw it." She looked round at them, at all of them. "Nobody else goes. Nobody else's name. Mine. That's the share. That's the one share." She looked up at Hale on the stair. "Done."

Corwin Hale looked down at her for a long moment, with his mild patient face.

"Thank you, Chief," he said.

Afterward, when the hall had broken up and the crew had gone off in its small stunned groups, and the trusties had come for her, quite politely, with the carbines held low, he came and walked beside her down the corridor toward the *Clement*'s cargo bay, where she would wait for the boat. He walked slowly, with his slate under his arm. He didn't look at her.

"They'll love you for ever," he said. "You know that. I'll see to it."

She said nothing.

"We'll say you went with it," said Hale. "On the Lamp. The Camp's already saying it; half of them think it was you on the Gull, they heard *Tsolo* on the channel. We'll let them. The Tether-Woman, who went out with the Lamp to carry it. That's a story they can tell their children." He paused at the cargo bay hatch. "It's better than a rope, Maren. It's better than the Kessel mines. It's better than the truth, which is that their tether-chief was sold to the Combine for their air. Nobody wants to tell their children that."

"You sold me."

"I bought them," said Corwin Hale. "With the only coin I had." He opened the hatch for her. "That's all a purchase is."

## The factor's garden

[
  Place: #The Clement
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Aurelian Quist
  When: same day
]

Quist came to see her in the cargo bay that evening.

They'd put her in a corner of it, behind a stack of crates, with a bunk and a blanket and a bucket, the same as the *Clement*'s crew had had for the two weeks they'd been locked in there; the crew themselves had been let out that morning and were back on their own decks, sullen and astonished, getting their ship ready to go home. Nobody guarded her. There was nowhere to go. The bay doors were locked from the outside and the only other way out was the maintenance hatch to the cold hold, and the cold hold was empty now.

Quist sat down on a crate across from her bunk. His wrists were free. He looked older than he had looked on the day he came aboard, by a good deal more than a month.

"I didn't argue," he said. "In the hall. He said I'd tell you he argued. He didn't. He went to Lachance and offered him you before Lachance had asked for anybody. I was there."

"I know."

"I thought you'd want to know for certain."

"I knew for certain," said Maren, "when he said *one name*."

Quist nodded slowly. "He'll do well," he said. "Your warden. He's the kind who does. I've met a great many of them. Every operation has one." He looked at his hands. "The Combine will give him a very good reference."

They sat for a while without speaking. Somewhere aft, through the bulkheads, the *Clement*'s engines were being warmed, a deep rising hum.

"I'll be on the *Admonition* with you," said Quist. "Going home to be disciplined. Lachance will put me in a cabin and you in the brig. When we get to Halloran there'll be an inquiry, and a court." He looked up at her. "I'm going to tell them the loss of the Asset was mine. That I had the key, and the authority, and the opportunity, and didn't use it. That's true. That's in my log, every night for fifteen nights. They'll want to know why I didn't, and I'll tell them I didn't know. They won't like that." He paused. "It won't save you. But it'll muddy the register. With a factor in it, the court will want to be careful. It'll probably mean Kessel rather than the rope."

"What's Kessel?"

"A mine. A long way in. The air's bad and the work's worse and people last, on average, about eleven years." He said it plainly, the way he'd said everything to her since the first day. "I'm sorry. It's what I've got."

"Why?" said Maren.

Quist was quiet for a long time.

"I've been thinking about gardening," he said at last. "About my friend in his greenhouse, with the roses. I used to think he went into the greenhouse because of what he'd done at Tamsin's Rock. Because of the four thousand." He turned his hands over and looked at the palms. "I think now he went in there because it was the only place he could make something grow. Instead of the other thing. I think it took him too long to find it, and when he did, it was too late, and that's why he—" He stopped. "I'd like to find mine before it's too late. That's all. That's what this is. It's very small. It's a rose."

"It's a rose," said Maren.

"It's a rose," said Aurelian Quist, and got up off the crate and went to the bay door and knocked to be let out.

## The back of the Plate

[
  Place: #The Drum
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Ekene Adu @@Odile Arda
  Items: ^The Share Plate:#The Drum
  When: 4 December 2391
]

Odile came for her at the fourth bell of the night, the dead hour between dips, when the *Patience* was asleep.

She came through the cold hold's maintenance hatch, the way Maren and Danek and she had come the night of the seizure, outside along the hull in a runner's suit with a spare one over her arm; and she opened the hatch into the cargo bay without a word, and threw Maren the suit, and waited while she put it on. Then they went out together on to the hull, hand over hand along Odile's old running line, under the stars, under the long pale streamer of the Lamp's tail, which was so far out now that the head of it had shrunk to a point, and in along the collar fairing, and down through the forward suit lock into the dark of the *Patience*.

Ekene was waiting in the drum hall.

He was alone, with one lamp turned low on the deck by the drum's end, and his arm in its sling. The Plate was off its bolts. He'd taken it down himself, one-handed, and laid it on the deck face down on a folded blanket, its grey back up. Beside it, on the blanket, there was a rigger's scribe, a hardened steel awl for marking cable. And a pot of hull sealant, and a brush.

Maren stood and looked at it.

"Odile told me," said Ekene. "What you'd want. Before the boat."

"I didn't tell her."

"You didn't have to," said Odile, at the hatch, keeping watch. "You've been doing sums with your fingers for two days, Chief. On the gallery. Against the rail. Like you do on the board when you're working out a throw." She shrugged. "I watched you. I've watched you since I was sixteen. You were writing down where it went."

Maren knelt down by the Plate.

She'd watched the throw on the board. She'd watched the Gull's track and the Lamp's, and she'd sat at the tether-chief's station for two days with nothing to do but listen to static and look at the old projection the riggers had drawn for the throw, the long falling curve of the Lamp's orbit coming in past Gall and going out, with its numbers down the side. A tether-chief remembers numbers. It's most of the job. She had them all.

She picked up the scribe and began to cut.

It was slow. The back of the Plate was raw steel, rough and pitted, and the scribe was meant for marking cable, not cutting plate; she had to press with all her weight on every stroke and drag it, slowly, so that the point would bite and not skid. It skidded anyway. On the sevens. On the T. She did the numbers first, in a column down the left-hand side, six of them, with their old dippers' labels, the elements of the Lamp's orbit, the way Hesketh had taught her to write an orbit down when she was a runner of twenty and had asked how the old chiefs found things in the sky. At the bottom of the column, the time of the Lamp's closest pass. Her hand slipped on the last line, and the awl skated across the year and left a long bright gouge through it. She left it. Anybody who could read the rest could work it out.

Then, underneath, after a gap, she cut where.

*SOUTH LIMB*

*BENEATH THE BLACK RIDGE*

*FORTY METRES*

Then she sat back on her heels for a while, with the scribe in her hand, and looked at the rest of the bare grey steel.

"What else?" said Ekene.

"I don't know."

"Yes, you do."

She did. She bent over the Plate again and cut it, in the middle, in her shaking capitals, line by line, pressing until her wrist ached and her fingers bled inside her gloves.

*WE SENT IT ROUND*

*IT SLEEPS IN THE COLD*

*DO NOT WAKE IT*

*DO NOT USE IT*

*FORGIVE US*

And then, after a long time, smaller:

*FORGIVE ABEL*

And under that, her initials, and the year.

When she'd finished she put the scribe down on the blanket and sat with her hands in her lap. Her hands were shaking. Ekene put his good hand over them.

"Hale will see it," said Odile from the hatch. "When he puts it back up. Or someone will. And tell him. And he'll grind it off."

"Not if he can't see it," said Maren.

She picked up the pot of hull sealant. It was the thick grey paint the dock crews used on the outside of the ship, over seams, against the cold: a paint that went on like cream and dried as hard as the steel under it and lasted, the dock crews said, for ever. She opened it and dipped the brush.

"Paint it over," she said. "All of it. Edge to edge. Every line. So it looks like the back of a plate." She held the brush out to Ekene. "And let it keep. Under the paint. Till somebody needs it."

"When will that be?"

"When the Lamp comes round," said Maren. "Two hundred and sixty years. When somebody's standing in front of this Plate and the Lamp's in the sky and they need to know where it is and what it is and what we did." She looked at him. "Somebody will. Somebody always does. There's always one."

Ekene took the brush.

He painted it one-handed, slowly, carefully, the way he did everything, edge to edge, while Maren held the pot and Odile kept watch at the hatch. The grey went on thick and smooth over the bare steel and the scratched lines, the numbers and the words and the initials, and they disappeared under it one by one, like names under resin. When he'd finished, the back of the Plate was a plain grey rectangle, featureless, gleaming wet in the low light of the lamp. You would never have known.

They waited for it to dry. It took two hours. They sat together on the deck of the drum hall, the three of them, under the still drum and the chalked names of the dead, LUSINE ARDA 2391 and Teska's little drawing of the Lamp beside it, and didn't say much. At some point Ekene put his good arm round Maren and she leaned against him, against his stiff cotton coat that smelled of the infirmary's smoked tea, and closed her eyes.

"I'll come," he said into her hair. "To Halloran. I'm not bonded. I can pay my own passage on the *Clement*. I'll find out where they send you. Kessel. Wherever. They always need medics in the mines."

"Don't."

"I'm going to."

"They need a medic here," said Maren. "The Camp. The ship. There's two thousand households on this moon that are going to need somebody who can set a bone and grow tea in a tray, now there's nobody coming." She opened her eyes. "Stay. Please. That's the one thing I'll ask you. Stay, and look after them, and look after the Plate." She paused. "And when Teska tells the children I went out with the Lamp, don't tell them it isn't true."

He was silent for a long time.

"Why?" he said at last.

"Because it isn't a lie," said Maren. "Not really. Somebody went out with it. Somebody called Tsolo, in my coat, with my knife." She looked at the drum, at the names. "Let them have him. Let them have the Tether-Woman. He'd have liked that. He always wanted to be somebody people told stories about."

When the paint was dry they hung the Plate back on its bolts, face out, three hundred names in steel, and Odile took her back along the hull to the cargo bay before the change of shift, and nobody ever knew.

## The Blank Share

[
  Place: #The Drum
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Corwin Hale @@Odile Arda @@Grandam Teska @@Ekene Adu @@Bram Kovac
  When: 5 December 2391
]

They came for her at the first bell of the morning.

Bram Kovač and two trusties, with the carbines held low and their eyes on the deck. They took her out of the cargo bay and through the collar and along the *Patience*'s forward corridors toward the boat dock, the long way, through the drum hall, because the short way was blocked by the dock crews loading the *Clement*'s last cargo for home. She didn't ask them why. She knew why. Hale would want them to see.

The drum hall was full. Not the way it had been full for the vote, or for the terms, packed and shouting. Full the way a room is full at a funeral: people standing along the walls, and on the gallery stair, and round the drum, quite quiet, in their work coveralls, the dippers and the riggers and the runners of the *Patience*. They didn't say anything when she came in. They didn't look at her, mostly. Some of them did. Teska did, from her bench, with her one good eye, and her face was wet. Odile did, from the gallery rail, very straight, with her hands at her sides. Ekene did, from the infirmary hatch, with his arm in its sling.

Hale was at the Plate.

He was standing at the end of the drum with his back to the hall, in front of the three hundred names, with a cold chisel in one hand and a mallet in the other. He was working at the top of the first column. At the first name. MAREN TSOLO, in shaky capitals, with the long scratch where the awl had skidded on the T. He was cutting it off. Not cleanly. He didn't know how to cut steel any better than she had; he was just scraping, gouging, with the chisel's edge, back and forth, so that the letters went ragged and then blurred and then were only a roughened, gouged, scarred patch of grey metal at the top of the column, where something had been written once and somebody had taken it off again.

He heard her come in. He didn't turn round until he'd finished. Then he did, with the chisel still in his hand, and looked at her across the drum hall with his mild patient face; and then he looked past her, at the crew, at all of them, standing along the walls.

"The Company's share," said Corwin Hale, quite gently, and tapped the gouged patch with the chisel's end. "Struck off. As agreed. The Purchase is complete."

Nobody said anything.

Nobody stopped him. She hadn't expected anybody to. She walked through the hall between Bram and the trusties, past the drum, past Hale, past the Plate with its three hundred names and its blank first line, and nobody stopped her either. At the gallery rail, as she passed under it, Odile Arda put her hand flat on the rail, palm down, the way runners put their hands on the drum before a dip, for luck. That was all. And then she was through the hall and out of the far hatch and along the corridor to the boat dock, and the *Clement*'s little cargo boat was waiting with its hatch open, and she got in.

## The port

[
  Place: #The Admonition
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Severin Lachance:remote
  When: same day
]

The *Admonition* was very clean.

That was what she noticed. After sixty years of the *Patience*, of grease and frost and old cable and the smell of three hundred bonded living in each other's breath, the cutter was clean and quiet and smelled of nothing, and its corridors were grey and narrow and lit very evenly, and the marines who walked her from the dock to the brig did it without speaking and without touching her more than they had to. Captain Lachance did not come to see her. She heard his voice, once, on a speaker in the corridor, pleasant and unhurried, giving an order she didn't understand.

The brig had a port. She hadn't expected that. A small round port the size of a dinner plate, very thick, set in the outer wall at head height, so that a prisoner could look out at what she'd left.

She stood at it as the *Admonition* turned for the Throat.

Kettle went by first. Small and white and cracked, with the long dark smudge of the Camp's warrens along its equator, two thousand households under the ice, breathing. Then Gall, filling the port, enormous, banded ochre and purple, with Old Purple turning on its southern limb and its single dark eye looking up at her. And the *Patience*, very small against it, a long rust-coloured whale hanging over the giant on her orbit, with her drum still and her line reeled in, and somewhere inside her, on the end of the drum in the hall, a steel plate with three hundred names on it and a blank where the first one had been.

And beyond them all, very far out now, so far that she had to press her face to the cold thick glass and look for it, the Lamp.

It was going. It was nothing but a faint smear now, a thumbprint of pale light low in the black beyond Gall's limb, with the barest suggestion of a tail, like a breath on glass. The way it had been on the day Lusine went into the cloud. Going out. Going out past the cold, where the sun was only a star and the ice was so cold it rang, for two hundred and sixty years, carrying in its black south limb, forty-one metres down under a broken ridge, a grey egg asleep in its cradle and a man in her coat beside it with his knees drawn up, keeping it company.

*Teska says it brings them back.*

Maren Tsolo stood at the port of the *Admonition*'s brig with her palm flat on the glass, the way you put your hand on the drum before a dip, and watched the Lamp until she couldn't tell any longer whether it was there or whether she was only remembering where it had been.

Then she sat down on the bunk, and folded her hands, and began, because she was the tether-chief and that was what tether-chiefs did, to count.
