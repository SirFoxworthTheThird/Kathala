# The Long Round

## Three hundred names

[
  Place: #The chamber
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Juno Brask @@Abel Tsolo:dead @@Hollis Vey:phone

There's a plate against his knees.

That's the first thing Til sees, after the coat. A rough grey rectangle of steel the size of a door panel, its edges wavy and black from an old torch, leaning against the drawn-up knees of the figure in the coat the way you'd lean a picture against a wall, face out, so that whoever came in through the arch would see it first. In the blue light of the ice it looks like a gravestone. It's covered in names.

Juno goes past her on her knees and kneels in front of it.

She doesn't touch it. She bends close, with her helmet lamp, and reads, the way she reads a column in the counting-house: top to bottom, left to right, her lips moving very slightly behind her visor. Fifteen columns. Names cut into steel by a lot of different hands: some deep and neat, some barely scratches, some only a mark, a cross or a star or a little drawing of a fish, with a name cut beside it by somebody else.

"Three hundred," says Juno, after a long while. "About. I lost count in the ninth column." She puts her lamp on the top of the first column. "Look at this one."

Til looks. At the top of the first column, where the first name ought to be, the steel is scarred: gouged and scraped and roughened, a patch of ragged grey where something was cut once and somebody went at it with a chisel. And over the scar, through it, across it, there's a name. Cut later. Cut badly, with something that wasn't meant for cutting steel, the letters deep and ragged and skidding, so that the name sits right on top of its own erasure like a word written over a word.

*MAREN TSOLO*

"Somebody took her off," says Juno. "And somebody put her back."

Til looks at the coat. At the back of it, at the stencil across the shoulders, white gone grey. *TSOLO.*

"Is that her?" says Juno.

"I don't know."

On the channel, very faint, through the relay Til clipped to the old anchor at the top of the shaft, Hollis Vey is breathing. He's been quiet since Oz. He's been listening to all of it.

"Captain," he says now. "What is it? What's down there?"

"A man," says Til. "In a coat. Sitting. He's been here a long time. There's a plate with names on." She takes a breath. "And there's something else."

It's beside him. Against the curve of the wall on his left, where she didn't see it at first behind the plate and the drawn-up knees. A cylinder of grey brushed steel, waist-high, on a squat base, with a ring of little dead lights round its rim and a window in its lid, frosted white and cracked right across. Down its side there's a long bright scar through the steel, newer than anything else in the room, as if something hit it once and skidded.

Til goes to it on her knees. She puts her lamp on the cracked window, and bends, and looks through the crack.

Inside, in a nest of old grey foam gone brittle with cold, there's an egg.

It's grey. A matte grey with no shine at all, so that her lamp sinks into it instead of lying on it. Smooth. Longer than her forearm. A fine seam round its widest part. And on its upper face, stencilled in black, in old capitals, three lines she has read before, in a dead man's register, in a dying man's black hide case, on the *Dray*'s galley table.

  *SANITATION AGENT*
  *PATTERN H*
  *FACTOR'S AUTHORITY ONLY*

Under them, small, a green wheel with eight spokes.

"It's here," says Til. "Hollis. It's here."

On the channel there's a long silence. Then a sound she's never heard Hollis Vey make, and doesn't want to hear again: a long, thin, broken breath, coming out of him through the cradle's grille as if it had been inside him for thirty years.

"Thank you," he says. "Thank you, Captain."

Til takes the probe off her belt. It's a salvager's probe, the kind you use to check whether a dead ship's tanks are still pressurised or a reactor's still hot; it reads temperature through a hull to a tenth of a degree. She sets it against the cryostat's flank, beside the scar, and holds it there, and watches the number come up.

Thirty-one kelvin.

She watches it for a full minute. It doesn't move. Then it does: thirty-one point one.

"It's warming," she says.

"How fast?" says Juno.

"Slow. A tenth in a minute. Maybe less." Til looks up at the arch, at the blue walls, at the ice dust still drifting down past the opening into the shaft. The Lamp shudders round them, the long grinding tremor coming up from somewhere far below. "The lobe's coming apart. The cold's coming out of it. There's sun getting in, down the cracks. It was colder than this, once. It's been colder than this for a long time." She does the arithmetic. "Hollis's register said forty."

"Nine kelvin," says Juno.

"Nine kelvin," says Til. "A tenth a minute, if it doesn't get faster. Ninety minutes. If it does get faster, less."

Juno doesn't say anything. She's still kneeling in front of the plate. She reaches out, slowly, and puts both gloved hands on its edges, and lifts it away from the knees of the man in the coat; it weighs almost nothing here; and turns it round.

The back of the plate is written on too.

Juno reads it aloud.

She reads it in her clerk's voice, flat, steady, the voice she used to read the frozen accounts on Platform Four; and Til, kneeling by the cryostat with the probe against its flank, listens, and watches the number. Thirty-one point three.

"There are numbers at the top," says Juno. "Six lines. Letters beside them. I don't know what they are."

"An orbit," says Hollis on the channel. "That'll be an orbit. The Lamp's. That's how they'd have written it."

"Then words." Juno bends close with her lamp. "*South limb. Beneath the black ridge. Forty metres.*" A pause. "Then a gap. Then—" She stops. She starts again. "*We sent it round. It sleeps in the cold. Do not wake it. Do not use it. Forgive us.*" Another stop. Longer. "*Forgive Abel.* And two letters. *M.T.* And a year. Twenty-three ninety-one."

"M.T.," says Til.

"Maren Tsolo." Juno's lamp goes to the front of the plate and back. "The name at the top. The one somebody cut back in."

"Then who's Abel?"

Nobody answers. Juno's looking at the figure in the coat. So is Til.

"There's more," says Juno. "Underneath. Newer. Cut with something else, not the same tool as the rest. Deeper and rougher." She reads it. "*Sent round again, twenty-six fifty-four. We did not use it either. Isaura Venn, air-warden. For the unshared.*"

The Lamp shudders. Ice dust falls past the arch.

"Twenty-six fifty-four," says Hollis Vey, very quietly. "The Concord's year. The year the fleet came." A breath. "They found it. Somebody in that year came out here and found it, and had it in their hands, with the Concord's fleet sitting off their moon. And put it back." Another breath. "*We did not use it either.*"

Thirty-one point five.

There are two recorders in the man's gloved hand. Til saw them when she first came in and didn't let herself look: two small dark boxes, held loosely in the fingers that are folded round his shin, one older and blacker than the other, both with jacks for a suit. She works them free, one at a time, gently, the way you'd take something from the hand of a sleeping child without waking it. She puts them inside her suit, against the chest heater, and holds them there with her arm.

"We haven't got time to listen," says Juno.

"We've got ninety minutes."

"We've got ninety minutes to do something about *that*."

"I know," says Til. "And I don't know what yet. And while I'm working it out, these are warming." She looks at Juno over the cryostat. "Somebody left them here for whoever came. Twice. Two hundred and sixty years apart. They went to a lot of trouble." She holds them against her. "I'd like to know what they had to say. Before we decide what to do with what they left."

Juno looks at her for a long moment through the visor. Then she sits back against the blue wall, beside the man in the coat, with the plate across her knees, and waits.

The older recorder warms first. Til plugs its jack into her suit and opens the channel so that Juno can hear, and Hollis, forty metres up and two hundred metres across the ice in his cradle in the *Dray*; and a man's voice comes into her helmet out of five hundred and twenty-six years, tired, ordinary, with an accent she's never heard and almost understands.

"*My name is Abel Tsolo. I'm a rigger on the skimmer* Patience. *I'm wearing my sister's coat.*"

They don't listen to all of it. There isn't time. Til lets it run while she watches the probe, ten minutes of it, and then the second recorder is warm, and she switches. But ten minutes is enough. It's enough to hear a man say that he killed a girl called Lusine Arda with a jeweller's file, and say her name three times. It's enough to hear him say what the thing in the cryostat is, and what it did at Tamsin's Rock, and who brought it, and how the bonded took it, in the night, with blood. It's enough to hear him say that his sister stood up in a drum hall and said no, and that it was her plan and her line, and that he put her to sleep with the factor's brandy and took her coat and came instead.

"*She threw me,*" says Abel Tsolo. "*Straightest throw anyone ever threw.*"

Juno has her hand flat on the plate across her knees. On the front of it. On the top of the first column. On the name.

The second recorder is a woman's voice. Younger. Quick, precise, a little hoarse, with the same accent but changed, the way an accent changes in two hundred and sixty years. She sounds very tired. She sounds like somebody talking in a small space at night while somebody else sleeps.

"*This is Isaura Venn. I'm an air-warden of Patience. I don't know who you are. I don't know when you are. I'm on the Lamp, in a dipper skiff, and in the morning I'm going to put it back. I'd like to tell you why.*"

She talks for an hour, on the recording. Til lets it run for twenty minutes, watching the probe. Thirty-three point one.

The woman talks about a city under the ice. About air. About an air-tithe, and a floor, and a girl in cracked grey gloves wiping the frost off a meter, and a grandmother with her hands folded. About a fleet of thirty-eight ships, and a council that voted, with thirty-four hands, to put forty thousand of its poorest people out on the surface in old domes to save the air for the rest. *Cut the line to save the ship*, she says they called it. About her mother, who mended filters, and who went up to the domes with the rest, with her daughter's coat round her shoulders and her daughter's key in her lap.

She talks about a pilot called Corin Strand, who wanted to take the thing in the cryostat home and open it on eleven thousand people, and about a knife.

"*I cut his line,*" says Isaura Venn, in Til's helmet. "*With the knife on the dead man's hip. He went out into the coma. I watched him go. I don't know if I'm sorry. I think I'll find out later. I think I'll be finding out for a long time.*" A pause. Breathing. "*I don't know what's going to happen. I don't know if the city's going to be there when I get back, or if I'm going to get back. I don't know if my mother's alive. I'm going to talk to them all, in the morning, on the big radio, the city and the fleet both, and tell them what this is and what we come from, and then I'm going to put it back where he put it and go home. And then it goes out. Round again. Two hundred and sixty years.*"

Another pause. Longer.

"*So if you're hearing this, you're there. When it's come round. I'm sorry. I'm sorry it's you. We couldn't kill it. Nobody could. We just sent it round, both of us, him and me, and hoped that whoever was there when it came back would be wiser than us.*" A small sound that might be a laugh. "*You'd better be. Or luckier. One or the other.*"

And then, very quietly, as if to herself:

"*If anybody still breathes on Kettle, when you hear this, tell them it was the floor. Tell them nobody dies on the floor. It's a lie. Tell them I said.*"

Til switches it off.

The little room is very quiet. The Lamp shudders, and ice dust falls past the arch, and the blue light trembles on the walls; and in the corner, against the curve of the wall, the cryostat sits with its cracked window and its long bright scar, and the probe on its flank says thirty-four point zero.

Juno is crying.

She's sitting with her back against the blue wall beside the man in the coat with the plate across her knees and her hand flat on the first name, and she's crying, without any sound, the way she must have cried at the dock at six, waiting for two days for somebody who wasn't coming back. Til has never seen her cry. Not once. Not at the counting-house, not on the *Dray*, not on Platform Four with the frozen accounts.

"Juno," she says.

"Don't," says Juno.

"I'm going to anyway."

Juno looks at her through her visor.

"Your mother cut her own line," says Til.

## The knife

She tells it the way she told Oz, in the engine space, two nights ago. She doesn't use the weather voice. She can't find it; it's gone, somewhere on the ice by the *Dray*'s flank, where Oz is sitting with his back against the armour and his visor turned up to the sky. She tells it in an ordinary voice, the one she has when she's tired.

The hulk spinning up. The line going round it. Mags nearest, and the coil catching her by the leg, and Mags with her hands on it, trying, and it winding in. The knife on Til's belt. Til's hand on it. Til's hand not doing anything.

"She was shouting at me," Til says. "*Cut it. Cut it, cut it.* And I had it out. I had it in my hand. And I couldn't." She looks at her hands in their gloves. "So she reached back down the line and took it off me. Out of my hand. And she cut it. Between her and me. And the hulk took her in."

Juno is very still.

"I said I did it," says Til. "At Cold Harbour. To the Directorate's man. And Szabo heard, and she told the Ring, and the Ring believed it. And I let it." She looks up. "You were six. You were at the dock. Somebody had to tell you something. I thought you could hate me. I'd be there. I'd be alive. You could hate me as much as you liked. But if it was her—if you grew up knowing she took the knife and went in on purpose—" Her voice goes. She lets it. "You can't hate somebody for that. You can only miss them. And there's nothing to do with it. It just sits there. For ever."

"So you took it," says Juno.

"I'm used to carrying things."

Juno looks at her for a long time.

"What did she look like?" she says at last. "At the end. When she cut it."

Til thinks about it. She's thought about it every night for eleven years, and she still has to think.

"Annoyed," she says. "She looked annoyed. Like I'd been slow on a job. Like I always was."

Juno makes a sound. It takes Til a moment to understand it. It's a laugh. A small, wet, cracked laugh, behind the visor, with the tears still running down under it, the laugh of somebody who's been hit very hard and has just found something funny in it.

"She always looked like that," says Juno. "When you were slow. I remember. You'd be late for dinner and she'd stand at the hatch with her arms folded and that face." She wipes at her visor with her glove, uselessly. "That's her. That's exactly her."

"It is."

"You should've told me."

"I know."

"You should've *told* me, Til." Juno's voice cracks. "Eleven years. I could've—I'd have—" She stops. She doesn't know what she'd have. Neither does Til. "You should've told me."

"Yes," says Til.

The Lamp shudders, harder than before. A long grinding roar comes up through the floor of the little room, through their knees, and the blue light flickers and dims and comes back, and a crack opens in the curved wall over the man in the coat, a thin black line, and light comes through it. White light. The sun, getting in.

The probe on the cryostat says thirty-five point two.

"It's going faster," says Juno.

"It's going faster."

Juno takes her hand off the name on the plate. She looks at the cryostat, and at the crack in the wall with the sun coming through, and at the man in the coat beside her with his knees drawn up and his head on his arms. At his hip, on its loop, in a sheath of leather gone hard as wood, there's a knife. A short hooked blade. A line knife.

"Two of them," says Juno. "Two of them sent it round. Him and her. Abel and Isaura. Two hundred and sixty years apart." She looks at Til. "And it's come round to us. And it can't go round again. Can it."

"No," says Til. "It can't."

"Then what do we do?"

Til looks at the knife.

She's been thinking about it since the galley, since Oz under the coolant manifold in the engine space, since the twenty-fourth. *A falling mountain.* She's been thinking about it on the ice by the *Dray*, with Oz telling her the south lobe was on the worst path of all. *The north lobe's going straight down. Dead centre. Gone.*

"Hollis," she says.

"I'm here, Captain." His voice is very faint now. The hinge in every breath.

"Oz said the north lobe goes in straight. Dead centre. Is that still true?"

A long pause. She can hear the cradle humming, the faint click of its instruments. She can hear him using his last strength to bring up Oz's numbers on the cabin screen, the curves on the grid, and read them.

"It's still true," he says. "Mr Mbeki was very good. The north lobe goes in at nine bells tomorrow, on the second. Straight. Steep. A mountain of ice two kilometres across, at sixty kilometres a second." A breath. "*You'd have to get it there cold. Faster than it can wake.*" Another. "Oh, Captain. Oh, yes."

"How far is it from us?"

"Four kilometres. Across the gap. It's drifting away. Every hour, further."

Til reaches across the man in the coat and takes the knife from his hip.

She does it gently. The sheath comes off the loop as if it's been waiting to. It's light in her hand; the blade, when she draws it an inch to look, is old and dark and very sharp still, with a hook at the end of it like a beak. A tether-chief's knife. The kind she's seen in the Roundsmen's chapel on Dock Nine, painted on the walls under the oldest names, next to the loops.

"We take it across," she says. "In the *Dray*. Cold. We put it down a crack in the north lobe, as deep as the winch'll go. And we let it go." She looks at Juno. "And Gall takes it. All the way down."

"And him?" says Juno.

Til looks at the man in the coat.

"Dippers gave their dead to the giant," she says. "Oz said. They lowered them down on the line and let them go." She puts the knife on her own belt. "He's a dipper. He's been sitting here waiting for somebody to give him to Gall for five hundred years."

"And Oz," says Juno.

"And Oz."

The probe says thirty-five point nine.

"Then we'd better," says Juno, and gets up off the floor with the plate in her arms.

## Cold

It takes them an hour to get everything up the shaft and across the ice and into the *Dray*, and afterward Til will remember almost none of it, only the probe.

She remembers cutting ice. That was Juno's idea. They're kneeling in the little blue room beside the cryostat, with the crack in the wall widening and the sun coming in white, and Juno says *the walls are colder than the room*, and takes the thermal lance off Til's belt, which is no use, it's heat, and then the pick, and starts cutting blocks out of the deep blue ice at the back of the room, where the sun hasn't got to. Slabs. Each one the size of a book. The probe reads them at twenty-eight. Til packs them round the cryostat on the sled, against its scar and its cracked window and its base, a little igloo of blue ice, and the number on the probe stops climbing, and sits at thirty-six point one, and stays there.

She remembers bringing him up. They lift him together, the man in the coat, out of the place where he's sat for five hundred and twenty-six years, and he comes up off the floor of the little room all of a piece, his knees still drawn up, his head still bowed on his arms, as light as a bundle of sticks, and they carry him between them out through the arch into the shaft and up on the line, hand over hand, forty metres, into the sun. Juno carries the plate on her back. Til tows the sled.

She remembers the ice. The south lobe is breaking up round them as they go, slowly, beautifully, the way it's been breaking for two days: long black cracks opening across the crust, with white fire coming out of them, and the horizon tilting, and the ground under the surface line rising and falling like the deck of a boat. The old tug lies on its side at the foot of the black ridge with its grapple arm half torn out of the ice, the way it's lain, she supposes, for a very long time. There's a crater along the ridge, old and black and huge, that she didn't notice before; and another; and another. Somebody, a long time ago, shot at this place. She doesn't stop to think about it.

She remembers Oz.

He's sitting where they left him, against the *Dray*'s flank, with his visor turned up to the sky. They bring him in last. Juno takes his arms and Til takes his legs, and he's heavy, even here, heavier than the man in the coat, and they get him up the *Dray*'s cargo ramp and lay him down on the deck of the bay beside the other one, side by side, two dippers, five hundred years apart. Juno takes off her own gloves, inside the bay, when the ramp's sealed and the air's up, and closes Oz's eyes through his opened visor with her bare fingers, the way you're meant to.

"On its way," she says, the Roundsman word, very quietly, and puts his hands on his chest.

The *Dray*'s cargo bay is open to the black for the crossing, its ramp down and its inner doors sealed, so that the bay is airless and dark and as cold as space can make it, out of the sun, with the cryostat on its sled in the middle of the deck in its igloo of blue ice. Til flies with the bay turned away from the sun the whole way. Four kilometres. It takes forty minutes, on thrusters, slow, through the falling dust and the drifting fragments of the Lamp, with Juno in the second seat calling out the probe's number every minute in her clerk's voice.

"Thirty-six point one."

"Thirty-six point two."

"Thirty-six point two."

Hollis Vey lies in his cradle in the cabin with his face turned to the port and says nothing at all.

The north lobe of the Lamp comes up in front of them like a black mountain hung upside down in the sky. It's breathing fire out of its broken face, the raw white side where it tore away from the south lobe two days ago, a cliff two kilometres high of new ice glittering in the sun with jets coming out of it everywhere. Across the middle of the cliff, running up from its base into the dark, there's a crack. Not a fissure. A canyon. A black split in the white ice, twenty metres wide, going back into the heart of the lobe further than the *Dray*'s lamps can reach.

"Oz would've liked that," says Juno.

"Oz would've wanted to go down it."

"We're going down it," says Juno. "Aren't we."

Til sets the *Dray* down on the lip of the canyon, on her grapple, as close to the edge as she dares. The ice here is new, freshly broken, and the probe, when Juno goes out and pushes it into a crack with her glove, says twenty-two.

"Colder," she says. "It's colder in there. It's the inside. Nothing's ever got to it."

They lower it on the winch.

The *Dray*'s winch, Oz's winch, the big salvage winch with its hundred kilometres of cable, most of it gone now, trailing from the bow of the *Carrion Hymn* somewhere out toward the Throat. What's left on the drum is enough. Three kilometres. Til swings the boom out over the lip of the canyon and Juno clips the cryostat's sled to the hook, with its igloo of blue ice still packed round it, and Til runs it out.

It goes down slowly into the dark. The *Dray*'s lamps follow it for a while, a little grey box in a nest of blue going down between walls of white, smaller and smaller, until it's a spark, and then not even that. The cable runs out off the drum, smooth and steady. Five hundred metres. A thousand. Juno, at the canyon's lip, reads the probe off the cryostat's own sensor through the cable's line, the number going the right way at last.

"Thirty-four. Thirty. Twenty-seven."

At two thousand metres, Til stops the winch.

Then they go back up the ramp into the bay and bring out the others.

They lower Oz first. That's Juno's idea too. She says he'd want to go first, to see what's down there, and Til thinks she's right. They clip him to the cable above the cryostat with his own lanyard, the one Til unclipped from the *Dray*'s hull on the ice and clipped to her own and hasn't let go of since, and they lower him down into the dark, sitting up, with his hands on his chest and his visor turned up toward the light. Til runs the winch. Juno watches him all the way.

Then the man in the coat.

They take him to the lip of the canyon between them, still folded the way he sat, with his knees drawn up and his head on his arms, and his coat stiff and white with frost, and the stencil across his shoulders, *TSOLO*, going grey. Juno clips him to the cable. Then she stops, and lays her glove flat on the back of the old coat, between the shoulders, where the name is, and leaves it there a moment.

"Abel," she says. "Abel Tsolo. Rigger. Of the *Patience*." She takes her hand away. "Gall has him."

"Gall has him," says Til.

They lower him down after Oz.

The last thing is Hollis.

He asked for it on the crossing. Not in words, at first; Til saw him looking at the canyon on the cabin screen, and at the probe's numbers, and at the two dead men on the deck of the bay through the cabin hatch, and knew. When she went back to him he was propped high on his pillows with his face turned to the port and his long bony hands folded on the blanket, and he said, quite calmly, through the grille, with the hinge in it: *Captain, I'd like to go down with it.*

She said she knew.

*My family brought it here*, said Hollis Vey. *I'd like to take it down. I haven't got long, you know. A day or two. Perhaps not that. The cradle's got power for a week. I'd like to spend what I've got watching Gall come up.* He smiled with his too-large teeth. *I came all this way to see Corva. I'd like to see the inside of it.*

So they take him out in his cradle, on its sled, down the ramp and across the ice to the lip of the canyon, and set him there, at the edge, on the north lobe's broken face, with the canopy turned toward Gall. The giant fills half the sky. It's very close now. You can see the storms moving in the bands. In nine hours this lobe will hit it at sixty kilometres a second, and the cloud-tops will light up from end to end, and the lobe will go down into the deep a thousand kilometres before it even begins to break, carrying everything in it; and there's nobody on it to see that but Hollis Vey.

"Thank you," he says, through the grille. His face under the canopy is grey and shining and very happy. "Thank you, Captain. Thank you, Juno. For reading to me."

"Mr Vey," says Juno.

"Go on. Both of you. You've a long way to go." His eyes are on the giant. "It's very beautiful, isn't it? My family never saw it. Not once. Sixty years." A breath. "Go on."

Til goes back to the winch.

The cable runs out over the lip of the canyon, taut, two thousand metres of it, straight down into the dark, with the cryostat on the end in its nest of blue ice, and Oz, and the man in the coat, all going down into the heart of the falling mountain together. Til stands at the boom with her hand on the drum brake. She could cut it with the winch's own shear, the big hydraulic jaws on the boom. That's what they're for.

She doesn't. She takes the knife off her belt.

It's a tether-chief's knife. A short hooked blade, old and dark and very sharp, made for one thing. She doesn't know who carried it first. A woman whose name somebody cut off a plate, and somebody else cut back. Her brother, who wore it out to the Lamp in her coat. An air-warden two hundred and sixty years later, who used it once, on a man, and put it back. Til doesn't know any of them. She knows what the knife's for.

She hooks the blade round the cable, where it runs off the drum and over the lip of the canyon.

"Til," says Juno, beside her.

"I know."

"You've got it in your hand."

"I know, Juno."

"Can you?"

Til looks down at her own hand on the knife's hilt. Her glove's not shaking. She'd have expected it to. It isn't.

"Yes," she says, and pulls.

The knife goes through the cable like it isn't there.

The end of the cable whips away over the lip and down, and is gone. It's very quiet. There's no sound on the Lamp. There's only the cut end of the cable, sliding away into the dark of the canyon, down, down, after everything it was carrying, until the *Dray*'s lamps lose it; and then there's only the canyon, black, going back into the heart of the north lobe, and the cold inside it, and the giant waiting below.

Til stands at the lip with the knife in her hand.

"Cut the line," she says, to nobody, "to save the ship."

"What?"

"Something somebody said once." She puts the knife back in its sheath, on her own belt. "On the recording. The air-warden. It's what they said on her moon, when they put her mother out on the ice." She looks at the canyon. "I think it's the first time anybody's said it right."

## The long round

[
  Place: #The Dray
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Juno Brask @@Hollis Vey:dead elsewhere
  Items: ^The Hush:#The Deep ^The Share Plate:#The Dray ^Maren's hook-knife:@Til Brask
  When: 2 May 2917
]

The *Dray* gets off the north lobe with seven hours to spare, and needs all of them.

Til flies. Juno does the engines. She does them the way Oz taught her in the lane, without being taught: in the engine space aft, with the core running hot and the coolant sleeve weeping and the board red in three places, on her knees under the manifold with a lamp in her teeth, holding the clamp. She holds it for six hours. Til can hear her on the intercom, swearing at it in Oz's Roundsman cant, which she must have picked up from him in the evenings without Til ever hearing.

They have to climb. The Lamp's pieces are falling toward Gall, and the *Dray*, sitting on their path, is falling with them; to get out of the way of the giant she has to turn and burn and build her speed in a new direction with an old drive and half a tank, the way the *Admonition* tried to do and couldn't, five hundred years ago, the way the Concord's frigates tried and couldn't, two hundred and sixty years after that. Til doesn't know about either of them. She knows the board. She flies it.

At the sixth hour, she's clear. High. Out of the Lamp's road, in a long ragged orbit round the giant that Oz would have sworn at for its shape, with the board still red in two places and the air on the cabin gauge reading a number Til doesn't like and doesn't say aloud.

Juno comes forward and straps into the second seat.

Neither of them says anything. They sit together in the small cockpit of the old ugly tug, the captain and her niece, with the knife on Til's belt and the plate on the deck behind the seats, three hundred names face up, the first one cut back over its own scar; and they watch the Lamp go home.

The north lobe goes first.

It goes in at the ninth bell of the second of May, exactly as Oz said it would. They see it from a long way off, high on Gall's day side: a black mountain falling, very slowly, as everything falls from far enough away, toward the banded face of the giant, trailing its tails of white fire, getting smaller as it gets closer, until it's only a black speck against the ochre cloud-tops with a bright line behind it. And then it touches.

There's a flash.

It's white, and then it's every colour, and then it's white again; a light so bright the *Dray*'s port darkens itself and Til still has to shut her eyes, and when she opens them there's a fireball on the face of Gall. A great rising dome of fire, white at the heart, gold and orange at the edges, swelling up out of the cloud-tops on the giant's day side to the size of a moon, of Tarn, of a world; climbing and spreading and slowly, slowly beginning to fall back, and as it falls, leaving under it on Gall's face a black bruise, round, enormous, darker than anything else on the giant, like a hole punched in the clouds into the dark beneath.

Down there, a thousand kilometres under that hole, two thousand, in the deep of the giant where the air is as thick as water and as hot as a forge, there's a grey egg that will never wake again. And a man in a coat. And a big soft engineer with delicate hands. And a dying man in a white cradle who came five hundred years to see his family's world from the inside.

"Gall has them," says Juno, quietly.

"Gall has them," says Til.

The rest of the Lamp goes in after it, all that day. They watch it piece by piece. The fragments of the comet coming round the giant in a long string, like beads, like a necklace with its string cut, each one trailing its own small tail of fire, falling one after another on to Gall's face in a line of flashes, each one leaving its own black bruise under it, until the giant's day side is marked across its middle with a row of dark wounds, like the prints of something enormous that has walked across it. The south lobe goes last, at a slant, skimming, as Oz said; it comes apart in the high cloud in a long tearing streak of light, and some of its pieces skip, and go up again, glittering, out across the black toward the moons. Nothing on them now. Only ice. Only dust and stone and old cold water from the edge of the system, going out.

By the evening the Lamp is gone.

There's nothing in the sky where it was. Only stars. For the first time in five weeks, since the Throat, the cockpit isn't lit by it, and the dark outside the port is just dark, and Gall's night side is only Gall's night side, black, without the faint green glow on it. Til hadn't known how used to the light she'd got. She sits in the dark of the cockpit with her niece beside her and misses it, a little, like a sound you only hear when it stops.

"The long round," says Juno.

"What?"

"That's what he called it. Abel. On the recording. When the Lamp went round the giant and started going out. *That's the long round.*" She's looking at the empty place in the stars. "It doesn't come back now."

"No."

"Nothing comes back."

"Oz would say it does," says Til. "Oz would say it's on its way."

"Oz would say a lot of things." Juno's quiet for a while. "What do we do now?"

Til looks at the board. At the air gauge. At the fuel. At the long ragged orbit, and the Throat, two days off on the far side of the giant, and the raw lane beyond it, and Cinderhaven, and a stone vault on the Ledger Ring with a bond in it for seventeen thousand nine hundred and forty marks.

"I don't know," she says. It's the truth. She doesn't think she's said it aloud to anybody in eleven years.

Juno nods slowly, as if that's an answer.

At the eleventh bell, the comms light comes on.

Til doesn't notice it at first. She's dozing in her seat, with her hand on the yoke out of habit. It's Juno who sees it: the small green light on the board that's been dark since the Throat, since Cinderhaven, since the last time anybody in the world wanted to talk to the *Dray*. Not the open band. The direct one. Somebody calling the tug by her name.

Juno puts her hand on Til's arm.

Til wakes, and looks at the light, and doesn't touch it for a long moment. Then she does.

"*Patience to the tug* Dray," says a voice.

It's a woman's voice. Not young. Calm, unhurried, with an accent Til almost knows: the accent on the second recorder, the air-warden's, changed a little more, the way an accent changes over a long time in a small place. It comes in very clear, from somewhere below them and to the left, from the direction of a small white cracked moon whose shadow they've been watching cross the face of the giant for five weeks.

"*Patience to the tug* Dray. *Tug* Dray, *do you hear me? We see you.*"

Juno's hand has gone tight on Til's arm.

Til picks up the handset. Her hand's shaking now. Of course it is. She takes a breath.

"*Dray* here," she says. "This is the *Dray*. Til Brask. Captain." She swallows. "Who's calling?"

"*This is Ama Ocampo,*" says the voice, "*Air-Warden of Patience.*"

Juno makes a sound.

"*We've been watching you,*" says Ama Ocampo, "*since you came through the Throat. Since before. We saw a probe come through, last year, and go back. We've kept quiet. We've kept quiet for a long time; it's an old habit with us, from when the lane was shut, and the last people who came through it were not kind. We didn't know who you were. We still don't, entirely.*" A pause. "*But we watched you go down on the Lamp. We watched you take something off it and carry it across to the north piece, and put it down, and come away. We watched the north piece go into Gall.*" Another pause. "*We know what was on the Lamp, Captain Brask. We've always known. We've known it was coming round this year since before my grandmother was born. We were coming to deal with it ourselves. We had a skiff ready.*" A breath. "*You were quicker.*"

Til can't say anything.

"*Captain. Is there anything aboard your ship from the Lamp?*"

Til looks over her shoulder, at the deck behind the seats. At the plate, lying face up in the dim light of the cabin. Three hundred names. The first one cut back over its own scar.

"There's a plate," she says. "Steel. With names on. The back's written on. And a knife."

On the channel there's a long, long silence.

When Ama Ocampo speaks again her voice has changed. It's not calm any more. It's very quiet.

"*Bring it home,*" she says. "*Please. Bring it home.*"

"We're short on air," says Til. It's the first thing that comes into her head. It's true. "Short on fuel. Our engineer's dead. I've got a girl aboard who's bonded to a house on Cinderhaven for her dead mother's debt, and a hold full of nothing, and a lane between us and home that'll probably kill us on the way back. I don't know what you want with us."

"*We don't want anything,*" says the Air-Warden of Patience. "*Nobody's bonded here, Captain. Nobody's owed. Not for a long time. Not since the year the Lamp came round before this one.*" A breath, and something in it that might, almost, be a laugh. "*Come down. Come down to Kettle. We'll guide you in.*"

She says it the way you'd say a thing about the weather. As if it were the simplest thing in the world.

"*There's air,*" she says.

Til sits with the handset in her hand. Beside her, Juno has both hands over her mouth.

Out beyond the port, very small, very white, cracked across like old ice, the moon called Kettle hangs in the dark beside the giant. There's a light on its night side. One. A single steady point of white, where there should be nothing but ice; not a reflection, not a flare. A lamp. A lamp in a window. And then, as Til watches, another beside it. And another. And another, all along the dark curve of the moon, one after another after another, coming on, as if somebody down there were going from door to door, turning every valve on the tier as far as it would go.

Til turns the *Dray* toward them, and takes her down.
