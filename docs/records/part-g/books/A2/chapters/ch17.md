# The Black Ridge

## Forty metres

[
  Place: #The Wick
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Corin Strand @@Dasha Imre @@Mattias Ebb
  Items: ^The Share Plate:@Isaura Venn
  When: 7 August 2654
]

Up close, the Lamp is black.

I don't know why that's the thing that surprises me most. Everything else about it has surprised me so long and so steadily that I've stopped noticing; the size of it, the silence, the way it fills every port in the *Wick* at once so that there's nowhere to look that isn't Lamp. But I've spent my whole life, and three weeks of this one, thinking of it as a light. A lamp. A smear of green in the Oculus. And on the seventh of August, when Corin brings the *Wick* in under its tail through the last of the dust and the coma thins and we see the body of it for the first time, bare, two kilometres off, it isn't light at all. It's black. Black as soot, black as the inside of a filter, black as the old cable that hangs in coils in the dipper docks, a vast lumpy black thing like two stones pressed together, turning very slowly, with a deep dark crack running round the narrow waist where the stones meet. The light comes out of it. That's all. It comes out of cracks and pits all over the black surface in long white jets, like breath on a cold morning, and goes streaming away from the sun to make the tail.

"It's ugly," says Dasha, at her port.

"It's beautiful," says Corin.

"It's both," says Mattias, at the nav screen. "Matching in four hours. Then we need to know where."

They all look at me.

I've been cleaning the Plate for five days. I've cleaned it through the sling and the long fall out from Gall and three sleepless nights in a seat that doesn't tilt, with my eyes streaming from the solvent and my fingers raw. I've cleared the strip of numbers down the left side, and the line in the middle, *it sleeps in the cold*, and a little either side of it, not enough to read. The rest is still grey. I've got perhaps a third of it.

"I need light," I say. "And I need everybody to stop looking at me."

They stop looking. I clip the work-lamp to the bulkhead and lay the Plate face down on my knees and take up the brush.

I go down from the numbers. That's what Malachy said, in the notes he gave me, in his neat old hand: *The numbers say where the Lamp is. Whatever follows them says where on it. Go down from the numbers.* So I go down. Below the six lines of elements there's a gap of bare steel I've already cleared, a finger's width, and then the grey again. I dab. Wait. Wipe.

It comes up letter by letter. The same awl, the same hard shaking hand, the same crossed sevens. A word. Two. I can't stop my hands shaking and I have to put the brush down and hold my own wrist to steady it.

*SOUTH LIMB*

Dab. Wait. Wipe.

*BENEATH THE BLACK RIDGE*

Dab. Wait. Wipe. The lamp's hot on my face. The solvent's in my eyes. Corin, in front, has stopped whistling.

*FORTY METRES*

I sit back.

"Isaura?" says Dasha.

I read it out. I read it the way I'd read a meter. *South limb, beneath the black ridge, forty metres.* The cabin's silent. Then Corin swings the *Wick* round, without a word, so that the Lamp's lower lobe comes sliding into the forward port, the bigger of its two stones, the one that faces away from the sun; and there, along the bottom of it, catching the edge of the light like the crest of a wave, there's a ridge. A long jagged wall of something blacker than the black around it, broken and uneven, like the spine of some enormous animal half-buried in the ice, throwing a long dark shadow across the ground beside it.

"The black ridge," says Mattias softly.

"There's something on it," says Dasha.

There is. I see it as she says it. Near the ridge's eastern end, in its shadow, almost lost against the black: a speck that isn't black. A grey speck. A small hard angular shape with a faint dull glint where the edge of the sunlight touches it, sitting on the ice at the foot of the wall as if it had been put down there by a careful hand.

"That's a ship," says Corin.

"That's a tug," says Dasha. Her voice has gone strange. "That's a scoop-tug. An old one. That's the old pattern. My gran had a picture."

Nobody says anything else for a long time. The *Wick* drifts in toward the Lamp's south limb on her thrusters, and the grey speck grows in the forward port, and becomes a box, a squat armoured box with thrusters at its corners and an arm folded under its nose like an insect's, frosted white along every edge, sitting at the foot of the black ridge where somebody landed it two hundred and sixty-three years ago, and left it, and never came back.

## The Gull

[
  Place: #The black ridge
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Corin Strand @@Dasha Imre @@Mattias Ebb
  Items: ^The Share Plate:#The Wick
  When: 8 August 2654
]

We go out at the start of the eighth, all four of us, on lines.

There's no walking on the Lamp. Corin tells us that over and over in the suit lock, checking our harnesses, his face very serious for once behind his visor. The Lamp has almost no pull at all; you weigh less than a feather. Jump and you won't come down. Push off too hard from anything and you'll go off the surface and keep going, slowly, gently, for ever, until your air runs out. You move by the lines. Hand over hand. Clipped on, always, to the *Wick*, or to an ice-anchor screwed into the ground, or to each other. Never unclip. Never, never unclip.

"Never," says Dasha, nodding. She's done this on the giant's moons, she says, on the ice-rafts that the dipper fleet sometimes chases for water. "My gran lost a cousin that way. He unclipped for a second to get round a rock. They saw him go. They watched him for two days."

Mattias says nothing. He's in the spare surface suit, the *Wick*'s emergency one, an old orange thing with a cracked faceplate that's been taped. His face behind the tape is very pale.

The surface of the Lamp is like nothing I have words for. It's black, and soft, like old snow gone grey with soot, a crust you push your glove into and it gives, and under the crust it's hard and glittering, ice, white and blue and very old. It's broken everywhere into pits and humps and little cliffs, and the light of the sun lies across it so low that every hump throws a shadow a hundred metres long. The jets are all round us. You can't hear them. You see them: a pit fifty metres off suddenly spitting a long silent plume of white straight up into the black, so fast and so high you can't see the top of it, and then subsiding, and another going off somewhere else. The ground trembles, very faintly, under my boots. It's like walking on the back of something asleep.

And above it all, over the horizon, which is so close here that it curves away from us like the top of a hill, there's Gall. Huge, banded, turning. We're three days from its closest pass. It fills a third of the sky.

The tug is fifty metres from where Corin set the *Wick* down.

We go to it hand over hand along a line Dasha strings from anchor to anchor across the soft black crust, and stand round it, and nobody says anything. It's smaller than it looked from above. Squat and armoured and very old, its paint long gone to bare grey metal pitted like the skin of an orange, its corners rimed with frost. Its nose is down. Its folded arm is buried to the elbow in the ice, a grapple biting deep. Its hatch, on the near side, stands open.

Corin puts his helmet lamp inside.

It's empty. A single seat, very battered, with its straps hanging loose. A board, dead, frosted over. A space behind the seat where something big was once clamped: a cradle, welded in roughly, its clamps standing open. A spool on the wall, empty. Nothing else. Somebody stripped this cabin bare before it left.

On the outside of the hull, by the hatch, half-hidden under the frost, there are letters. Corin wipes them with his glove. Old stencilled capitals, the paint almost gone, just the ghost of them in the metal.

*GULL*

And under that, smaller:

*S.V. PATIENCE*

Dasha makes a sound in her helmet. Corin straightens up very slowly.

"*Patience*," he says.

"It's ours," says Dasha. "It's off the ship. Off the Spine. It's off *our* ship." Her voice is shaking. "They came here. The founders. They came here in the founding year, in the old ship's tug, and they landed here, under the ridge, like the Plate says." She turns round, clumsily, on her line, and looks along the foot of the black wall. "Forty metres. Forty metres where?"

Mattias has found it.

He's gone ten metres along the ridge's foot from the tug, clipped to Dasha's line, and he's standing over a place where the crust is different: a shallow round depression in the black, a metre and a half across, like a dent in snow where something heavy once lay. In the middle of it there's a hole. Not open. Filled, almost, with a plug of grey frost, glittering, the kind of frost that grows over two and a half centuries of cold. But round its rim, frozen into the ice, there's a line. A light line, thin, pale, the kind riggers use for small loads, coming up out of the frost and running across the surface to a little anchor screwed into the ridge's foot, an old anchor, corroded, with something hung on it: a small grey box with a stub of aerial.

"Relay," says Corin. "That's a radio relay. For talking up out of a hole."

"Out of forty metres," says Mattias.

We clear the shaft. It takes us four hours. The frost isn't hard, not the way ice is hard; it comes away under Dasha's pick in soft glittering flakes that float up round us and hang in the black and sparkle, like being inside a shaken globe of snow. Under it the shaft is a clean round hole the width of a man's shoulders, going straight down into the dark, with the old line running down its side frozen into the wall. Corin drops a lamp down it on a cord. We watch it go down and down, a little spinning star, smaller and smaller, until it stops.

"Forty," says Corin, reading the cord. "Forty-one."

Nobody moves.

"I'll go," I say.

Corin looks at me. "You?"

"I've got the Plate. I've read it. I'm the one who's meant to find out what's there." I don't know where the words are coming from. My heart is going so hard I can feel it in my visor. "Malachy said. *Read it first.*"

"I'm coming," says Corin.

"And me," says Mattias.

Corin looks at him for a long moment through the two visors. "Why you?"

"Because I've seen the card," says Mattias. "And you haven't. If it's what I think it is, I'm the only one of us who'll know it."

Dasha stays at the top. Somebody has to, Corin says, to mind the lines and talk us down and haul us up if anything happens, and Dasha has done more lines than anybody. She screws a fresh anchor into the ridge beside the old one and clips three lines to it, and checks each of ours twice, and pats my helmet, once, the way you'd pat a child going into school.

"Say hello for me," she says. "To the Tether-Woman."

## The chamber

[
  Place: #The chamber
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Corin Strand @@Mattias Ebb @@Dasha Imre:remote

It's not dark at the bottom.

That's the first thing. I come down the last metres of the shaft hand over hand on my line, with my helmet lamp swinging, and Corin's below me already and Mattias above, and I expect dark, a well's dark. But the ice down here glows. Faintly, blue, the deep strange blue of very old very clean ice, lit from somewhere far above by the sun on the surface, the light coming down through forty metres of it like light through deep water. The walls of the shaft are blue glass. My breath is very loud.

At the bottom, on the left, there's a door.

Not a door. An opening. Somebody cut it into the side of the shaft with a pick; you can see the marks, the long rough scallops, like the marks in the ice on the surface of the Ninth's old water tanks. It's low, waist-high, a rough arch. Corin's lamp is shining through it into a space beyond. He's not moving. He's kneeling on the bottom of the shaft with his lamp held out in front of him through the arch, quite still.

I come down beside him and look.

It's a small room. A little round room cut out of the blue ice, big enough for one person to sit in with their back against the wall and their knees up. Somebody is.

They're sitting against the far wall in the blue light, with their knees drawn up and their arms round their knees and their head bowed forward on to their arms, the way you sit when you're very tired and you're waiting for something and you've decided to rest your eyes. They're wearing a coat. A heavy quilted pressure-coat, old, patched at the elbows and the knees, with its hood up over a helmet and rings round its collar for a helmet seal, the quilting gone stiff and white with frost. A knife hangs on a loop at their hip, in a sheath: a short hooked blade, a line knife, the kind riggers still carry. Their gloved hands are clasped round their shins. In one of them, held loosely against the knee, there's a small dark box with a strap.

Beside them on the floor of the little room, against the curve of the wall, there's a cylinder. Grey brushed steel, waist-high, on a squat base, with a ring of little lights round its upper rim. The lights are dark. Its lid has a window in it, small, frosted solid white.

Nobody says anything.

"The Tether-Woman," says Dasha's voice in my helmet, from forty metres up, very small. "Isaura? Is she there?"

I can't answer. I go in through the arch on my hands and knees, slowly, and Corin lets me. I kneel in front of the figure in the coat. I don't touch them. I can see nothing of a face; the hood's up and the helmet visor under it is turned down toward the knees and frosted over, solid white, like the cryostat's window. They're very still. They're so still they're not like a person at all; they're like something that grew here, out of the ice, slowly, over a very long time.

I lean round, carefully, and put my lamp on the back of the coat.

There's a word stencilled across the shoulders, in old white paint gone grey. The same kind of capitals as the GULL on the tug's flank. One word.

*TSOLO*

"It's her," says Dasha, in my ear. She's been listening. She can hear my breathing, she must, she must hear it stop. "Isaura, it's her, isn't it. It's the Tether-Woman. With her name on her back. Like my gran said."

"I don't know," I say.

Mattias has come in behind me. He doesn't look at the figure. He goes past me on his knees to the cylinder against the wall and bends over it, and puts his gloved hand on the frosted window in its lid, and rubs. The frost doesn't come away; it's too old, too hard. He takes his knife from his belt and scrapes at it, very gently, with the back of the blade, and the frost comes off in a little white curl, and he bends close with his lamp and looks through the window.

He stays like that for a long time.

"Isaura," he says. "Come and look."

I come.

Inside the cylinder, in a nest of old grey foam gone hard and brittle with cold, there's an egg. Grey. Matte grey, without any shine at all, so that my lamp seems to sink into it rather than lie on it. Smooth. Bigger than my forearm. With a fine seam round its widest part, like the join between a cup and a saucer. On its upper face, stencilled in black, in neat old capitals, three lines.

  *SANITATION AGENT*
  *PATTERN H*
  *FACTOR'S AUTHORITY ONLY*

And under the lines, very small, a green wheel with eight spokes.

"That's the card," says Mattias. His voice is quite steady. "That's the picture on the card. That's Asset Seven."

Behind us, Corin Strand lets out a long breath.

## I'm wearing my sister's coat

]

I take the recorder out of the figure's hand.

I don't know if I'm allowed to. I don't ask. The gloved fingers are curled round the strap and they don't want to let go, they've been holding it for two hundred and sixty-three years, and I have to work them open one by one, very gently, the way you'd open the hand of someone asleep without waking them. It's a rigger's log. A little voice recorder in a hard black case, the kind the dipper crews still carry to make notes on runs, with a jack for a suit. Its old cell is long dead. I put it inside my suit, against the chest heater, and hold it there with my arm, and sit with my back against the cold blue wall of the room beside the figure in the coat, and wait for it to warm.

Nobody says anything while I wait. Corin kneels in the arch with his lamp. Mattias kneels by the cylinder. Up above, Dasha has gone quiet.

After twenty minutes I take it out and plug its jack into my suit, and switch it on, and the little light on its case comes on, very dim, amber; and I open the suit channel so that the others can hear, all of them, Dasha too, forty metres up on the black surface of the Lamp; and a voice comes into my helmet out of two hundred and sixty-three years.

It's a man's voice.

"*My name is Abel Tsolo,*" says the voice. "*I'm a rigger on the skimmer* Patience, *of the Halloran-Vey Combine, Corva operation. Or I was. I don't know what the* Patience *is now. I don't know what anything is. It's the thirtieth of November, twenty-three ninety-one, and I'm forty-one metres down in a comet, in the cold, and I'm going to tell whoever's listening what I've done and what this is, because somebody ought to know.*"

A breath. A small laugh, very tired.

"*I'm wearing my sister's coat.*"

## Forty minutes

]

He talks for forty minutes.

I'm not going to set it all down here. It's his. I've kept it, and it's in its box, and whoever comes after can listen to it for themselves the way I did, sitting in the blue light beside him with his voice in their ear. But I'll say what he said, the bones of it, because the bones are what matter, and because I sat there with my back against the ice and listened to two hundred and sixty-three years of my city come apart in my helmet, and I'd like somebody to know what it felt like.

He said the Combine was closing Corva. He said the crew of the *Patience* was being sold to a war, and the Camp on Kettle left to die in the ice, two thousand households, with nobody coming. He said a runner named Lusine Arda found out, and told him, because she loved him, and he told the chief warden of the ship, a man named Corwin Hale, who was the Combine's informer; and Hale gave him a jeweller's file and told him to take her harness clip two-thirds through, to scare her, and he did it, and she died on the line. He said it plainly. He didn't ask anybody to forgive him for it. He said her name three times.

He said the bonded took the ship. He said they took it by force, in the night, with blood: a rigger called Danek Sorell, shot in a cold hold; a clerk called Ferrand, with his hands up; marines; dock girls; Jory from the Bucket crew. He said the Combine had brought a weapon to use on them if they didn't go quietly, a Sanitation Agent, Pattern H, the thing the bonded called the Hush, and he said what it did, and he said a name, Tamsin's Rock, and four thousand. He said the bonded took the Hush too. He said Corwin Hale wanted to use it on the Combine's cutter, the *Admonition*, eighty people, to put them to sleep in their bunks; and that the bonded nearly voted for it. And that his sister stood up in the drum hall and said no. And that his sister said, put it on the Lamp. Put it where nobody can use it, not them and not us. And they voted for her.

"*Her name's Maren Tsolo,*" he said. "*She's the tether-chief. It was her plan and her line, and she was going to come herself, because that's what she does. And I didn't let her. I put her to sleep in the dock with the factor's brandy and Ekene's green bottle and I took her coat and I came instead. So if you find me here, and somebody's told you a story about the tether-chief going out on the Lamp, it isn't true. It's me. She's back there on the board. She threw me. Straightest throw anyone ever threw.*"

A long pause, there, on the recording. Breathing. The hum of a cryostat, faint.

"*Don't wake it,*" said Abel Tsolo. "*Whoever you are. Don't take it home. I don't know what you've got there now, back on the ice, if there's anything. I hope there is. I hope they made something. But don't take this back to it. It's not a gift. It was never a gift. It's the worst thing anybody ever made, and we couldn't kill it, so we sent it round. Send it round again. Or kill it, if you know how. But don't wake it.*"

And then he said goodbye to his sister, and to somebody called Odile, and somebody called Ekene, and somebody called Teska, and the recording ran on for a little while with nothing on it but his breathing and the hum, getting slower, and then it stopped.

## It doesn't matter

[
  Place: #The chamber
  Characters: @@Isaura Venn @@Corin Strand @@Mattias Ebb
  When: same day
]

When it's finished, nobody moves for a long time.

I'm sitting with my back against the wall beside him, beside Abel Tsolo, with his recorder in my lap. Mattias is on his knees by the cryostat with his hands flat on the frosted lid, his head bowed. Up on the surface, Dasha is crying, a long low raw sound in my helmet that she's not trying to hide. I'm not crying. I'm thinking of the Plate, face down on my knees in the *Wick*, and the line of words in the middle, *it sleeps in the cold*, and the grey paint over everything else. I'm thinking of the Blank Share.

I'm thinking of a man in a fur collar on a back bench in the old bridge saying *cut the line to save the ship*, as if it were a proverb.

Corin Strand is kneeling in the arch.

He hasn't moved since the recording began. His lamp's still in his hand, held out in front of him, shining on the cryostat. In its light I can see his face behind his visor, and it's not the face from the posters any more. It's very white and very young and quite blank, the face of somebody who's been hit very hard and hasn't fallen down yet.

"Hale," he says.

Nobody answers him.

"Corwin Hale." His voice is strange. "The chief warden. Of the *Patience*." He laughs, a short broken sound. "Do you know who Edda Hale was, warden? You won't. They don't teach it on the Ninth. Edda Hale was the first woman of my family. Edda Hale-Strand. My seventh grandmother. Her father was a founder. It's on the family tree in the Strand house, in gold, the first name on it. *Corwin Hale, First Shareholder, a Purchaser of the* Patience." He swallows. "That's us. That's where we come from. The Strands. We come from him."

"Corin—"

"An informer. A company man. He had a girl killed with a file." He looks at the figure in the coat, and then at me. "And the Purchase. *We bought our air.* It's in the catechism. I've said it every Lampfast since I could talk." He shakes his head inside the helmet. "They didn't buy anything. They took it. In the night. With a dead clerk with his hands up."

"Yes," I say.

"And they had this." He turns the lamp on the cryostat. "All along. They had this. And they sent it away, and they painted it over, and they told us we'd bought our air."

He's quiet for a moment. Then he gets up, slowly, in the nothing gravity, off his knees, and stands bent over in the low arch with his lamp, and when he speaks again his voice is different. Quite steady. Quite calm. His father's voice.

"It doesn't matter," says Corin Strand.

"What?"

"What they were. What they did. Any of it." He's looking at the cryostat. "It doesn't matter now. It's two hundred and sixty years ago. They're dead. All of them. Him." A jerk of the lamp at the figure in the coat. "And there's forty thousand people on the Rime, warden. Your mother's one of them. And twenty days of air, if that, and thirty-eight ships between them and the giant, and a commodore with orders to empty the city if he can find this thing first." He moves the lamp to the egg in its window. "And here it is."

"Corin."

"*Sanitise the claim*, the lieutenant says. Fine. We'll sanitise his." He's moving now, bending over the cryostat, feeling round its base for the clamps that hold it to the floor of the ice room. "One shot. The *Steadfast*. Their flagship. We take it back through the pickets, under the tail, the way we came. My father has a fleet of dippers with grapples that can put a cryostat on a hull. We put it on the *Steadfast*'s hull, by her intakes, and we open it. That's the end of the siege. Then the others go home. Then the dippers fly. Then everybody comes down off the Rime."

"It's not a gift," I say. "You heard him. *Don't wake it.*"

"He was a murderer," says Corin, "and a rigger, and he's been dead two hundred and sixty years. My father's alive. Your mother's alive. For now." He finds a clamp and works it. It's frozen; it doesn't move. "Help me with this, Ebb."

Mattias doesn't move.

"Lieutenant."

"No," says Mattias.

Corin looks at him.

"There are eleven thousand people on the *Steadfast*," says Mattias quietly, on his knees by the cryostat with his hands still flat on its lid. "Conscripts, most of them. Bonded. They owe the Concord for their fathers' fishing boats. I flew landers off her for a year. I know the cooks." He looks up at Corin through his taped faceplate. "And if it vents on her hull, by her intakes, it goes in. And she's docked, half the time, to the *Vigilant* and the *Resolute*, for supply. It goes through the collars. Like it would have done here." He looks at the figure in the coat. "Like he said."

"Then they shouldn't have come," says Corin.

"No," Mattias agrees. "They shouldn't. I shouldn't have. None of us asked."

Corin takes his sidearm out of the thigh pocket of his flight suit.

I didn't know he had one. I suppose I should have; the dipper pilots carry them, for the moon-rafts, for things that go wrong. It's small and black and he holds it quite steadily, pointing at Mattias, in the blue glow of the ice.

"Help me with the clamp, Lieutenant," he says.

Mattias doesn't move.

Corin shoots him.

It's very quiet. That's the thing about the Lamp. There's no air in the little room; there's no sound. There's a flash, and a jerk of Mattias's orange suit, and a spray of something that sparkles in my lamp and freezes as it flies and hangs in the air like the frost we cleared from the shaft. Mattias folds over against the cryostat with his hand on his side. In my helmet, on the suit channel, I hear him make a small surprised sound, and then breathe, fast, hissing.

"Suit's holed," he says. "Left side. It's—I've got a patch. I've got it."

"Then patch it," says Corin, not unkindly, and turns the sidearm on me. "Warden."

I'm still sitting against the wall beside Abel Tsolo. My hand is on the floor of the room. Beside it, at the figure's hip, on its loop, is the line knife in its sheath.

"Help me with the clamp," says Corin, "and we'll all go home."

I help him with the clamp.

## Cut the line

It takes an hour to get the cryostat up the shaft.

Corin rigs it. He's a dipper; he knows how to haul a load up a line in no gravity, and he does it well, quickly, without any wasted movement, with the sidearm in the pocket of his flight suit where he can reach it. He clips the cryostat to the old line, Abel's line, and to one of Dasha's, doubled, and goes up ahead of it, hand over hand, hauling it after him, a grey steel cylinder floating up the blue shaft like a bucket from a well. He tells me to stay at the bottom with the lieutenant. He tells Dasha, up on the surface, that if she so much as touches the anchor he'll shoot her when he comes out. Dasha says nothing.

Mattias has patched his suit. He's lying on the floor of the little ice room with his back against the cryostat's empty place, his hand pressed over the patch, breathing in short hard hisses. He's lost some blood. Not a lot, he says. Enough.

"Go," he says. "Go up. I'm fine. Go."

I go up.

I come out of the top of the shaft into the sun, into the long hard shadows and the glittering black crust and the silent jets, with Gall filling a third of the sky; and Corin's already halfway to the *Wick*, hand over hand along Dasha's surface line, with the cryostat floating on its tether behind him like a dog on a lead. Dasha is standing by the anchor at the shaft mouth. She looks at me through her visor and shakes her head, once.

"He's unclipped," she says on the suit channel. Very low. "From the anchor. He needed both lines for the hoist. He's on the surface line now. Just the surface line. One clip."

I look. She's right. There are three lines on the anchor by the shaft: mine, and Mattias's, going down into the hole, and the long one Dasha strung across the crust to the *Wick*. Corin's on the long one. One clip, at his harness, on a short lanyard, sliding along it as he goes. The cryostat's clipped to his harness beside it.

I don't think. I want to be honest, as I've tried to be honest all through this. I don't make a decision. I don't do the arithmetic. My hand is at my hip, and at my hip, where I put it in the little ice room while Corin was working the clamp with his back to me, there's a line knife on a loop: a short hooked blade, very old, very sharp still, the kind tether-chiefs carried on the old skimmers, with a sheath of leather gone hard as wood. I don't remember taking it off him. Off Abel. I remember the sheath under my fingers, and the hilt.

I go along the surface line after Corin, hand over hand.

He hears me on the channel. He turns round, clumsily, on the line, with the cryostat swinging slowly behind him, and sees me coming, and puts his hand on the pocket where the sidearm is.

"Go back, warden."

"Corin, don't take it home."

"Go back to the shaft." He's got the sidearm out. "I don't want to. I'll do it. You saw me."

"I saw you."

"Then go back."

I'm three metres from him. Between us, along the line, there's his lanyard, his one clip, sliding. On the other side of him, the cryostat, on its own short tether clipped to his harness. On the other side of the cryostat, the line runs on across the crust to the *Wick*, thirty metres off, its hatch open. Under all of it, the soft black ground of the Lamp, so close and so slight that it barely holds us; and over all of it, the sky, black and enormous and full of the giant.

"My mother's on the Rime," I say.

"Then help me."

"She gave me her blessing," I say, "to stop you."

I don't know if he understands what I'm going to do. I think he does, at the last; I think he sees my hand come off the line with the knife in it. He brings the sidearm up. I'm already moving. I don't go for him. I go for the cryostat's tether, where it's clipped to his harness, and I catch it in my free hand and pull myself to it along it and get my glove round the cryostat's cold steel flank, and hold on; and with the other hand, the hand with the knife, I reach across him and hook the curved blade round his lanyard, where it runs from his harness to his one clip on the surface line, and pull.

It's a good knife. It's the best knife I ever held. It goes through the lanyard like it isn't there.

He's holding the cryostat's tether, too, at the clip, with the hand that isn't holding the gun. That's the thing. For a moment we're both holding the same steel cylinder, him at one end and me at the other, and I've got my boots locked round the surface line and he's got nothing. And the cryostat weighs almost nothing here, and he weighs almost nothing here, and the only thing holding him to the Lamp is his grip on the cryostat that I'm holding on to.

"Let go," I tell him.

He looks at me through his visor. His face is very young.

He fires. The shot goes past my helmet; I see the flash and nothing else. He fires again and I feel it hit the cryostat, a jolt through my glove, a spark off the brushed steel. And then he does what I told him to, without meaning to, the way anybody would who'd just felt a gun go off in a hand that was also trying to hold on to something: his grip opens.

I hold the cryostat. He goes.

He doesn't go fast. That's the worst of it. Nothing goes fast on the Lamp. He goes up and away from the surface of it, very slowly, with his arms and legs spread out, turning a little, end over end, as gently as a seed going up from a dandelion in the wind tunnels on the Third; and the line is out of his reach before he understands that it's out of his reach, and then he understands, and reaches for it anyway, for a long time, and keeps reaching. He's shouting on the channel. He's shouting my name, and his father's, and then nothing, just breathing, hard, and the sound of a man trying with his whole body to swim in something that isn't there.

I watch him go. Dasha comes along the surface line behind me and puts her hand on my shoulder, and watches him go too. Neither of us says anything. Neither of us has anything to say. He goes up into the long black shadow of the ridge and out of it again into the sun, small, and smaller, a little tumbling figure against the banded face of Gall, getting further; and his breathing on the channel gets faster and then slower, and then, after a long time, fainter, as he goes out of range of our little suit radios, out into the coma of the Lamp, the dust and the glittering ice, going out with it.

The last thing I hear him say is *Dad*.

I'm holding the cryostat in one hand and the knife in the other. There's a bright new scar on the cryostat's flank where his second shot hit it, a long scrape through the brushed steel. Its lights are still dark. The frost on its window has cracked across.

Through the crack, very faintly, in my helmet lamp, I can see the egg, grey and smooth and asleep, exactly as it was.

"Cut the line to save the ship," says Dasha Imre, very quietly, beside me.

I don't answer her. I can't. I stand on the black ice of the Lamp, clipped to the line, with the founders' weapon in one hand and a dead man's knife in the other and a living man's last word in my ears, and I look at Gall turning in the sky, two days from its closest pass, and at the long bright river of the Lamp's tail going out behind us into the dark; and somewhere under that, very far away, too small to see, a white cracked moon, with a city in it, and forty thousand people on its surface in old domes, breathing what's been spared.
