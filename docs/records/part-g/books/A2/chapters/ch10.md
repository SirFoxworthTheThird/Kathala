# Seizure

## Outside

[
  Place: #The cold hold
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Odile Arda @@Danek Sorell:dead @@Marta Oyelowo @@Aurelian Quist
  Items: ^The Hush:@Maren Tsolo ^Quist's key:@Maren Tsolo
  When: 17 November 2391
]

They went at the fourth bell of the night, under the noise of the dip.

That was Teska's idea. *They'll be listening for quiet,* she said, in the Belly, the last time they met. *Every guard listens for quiet. So give them noise.* So Maren had put a night dip on the roster, the first in a month, a short shallow one into the high cloud that would bring up almost nothing; and the factor had approved it without comment, because the last weeks of a season were for squeezing, and a tether-chief who wanted to squeeze was a tether-chief earning her supervisory assessment. At the third bell the drum began to turn. By the fourth it was running at full spin, and the whole forward half of the *Patience* was shaking with it, a great steady thunder that came up through every deck and every bulkhead and every bone, so loud in the collar section that the marine on the *Clement*'s side of the lock had his ear-guards in.

Maren was outside by then, on the hull, with Odile and Danek, going hand over hand along Odile's running line.

Three of them, because the egg was heavy and a cryostat was heavier, and because Danek had asked. *I'm big,* he said. *If there's a marine, I'm big.* She had said yes. She would think about that later, a great deal; she had said yes because he was big and because she wanted someone big beside her in that cold dark hold, and because he had three children in the third shaft and a wife called Tamar with the cough, and he wanted to be doing something for them with his hands instead of sitting in the Belly listening.

Gall turned below them. The Lamp hung above, so bright now it threw faint shadows on the hull: three small black figures crawling on a long rust-coloured whale, each with a shadow beside it from a comet.

The maintenance hatch opened as easily as before. They went in feet first. The inner door. The cold.

There was a marine in the cold hold.

There had not been one before. There had been the cryostat, and the racks, and the frost, and nobody; the hold was checked every four hours by a cargo hand and every day by the factor, and otherwise left alone. Tonight there was a marine sitting on a crate by the forward door in a cold-weather jacket with his rifle across his knees and his hood up, reading a slate, with a heater cube glowing orange at his feet. He looked up when the inner door of the maintenance lock opened. He was very young. He had the slate in one hand and the other on the rifle, and for a long moment he simply stared at them, three figures in white runner's suits coming out of a wall, as if they were ghosts.

Danek went for him.

He did it before Maren could say anything. He went across the hold in four long strides, enormous in his suit, and the boy got the rifle half up and Danek knocked it aside with one arm and took him round the chest with the other and bore him down on to the deck, and they rolled, and the heater cube went skittering away across the frost, and the boy was shouting something inside his hood and Danek was saying *quiet, quiet, quiet, I won't hurt you, quiet*, over and over, like a man gentling a dog.

"Tape," said Maren. "Odile."

Odile had the tape. They taped the boy's wrists and ankles and taped his mouth, carefully, so he could breathe, and sat him against a rack. He stared at them over the tape with huge wet eyes. Danek knelt in front of him and patted his shoulder.

"Sorry, son," he said. "Nothing personal."

Maren went to the cryostat.

The status lights round its rim were still green. The egg lay in its smoking foam under the frosted lid, grey and indifferent. The thick power cable ran to its socket in the wall. The thin cable ran to the grey box beside it, the box with the single red light and the keyhole.

"How do we move it?" said Odile.

"We unplug the power. It's got its own cells; Ekene says they last days. We unplug the box." Maren looked at the thin cable. "The box is how the factor opens it from here. Without the box, he'd have to open it by hand."

"Then pull it."

"Wait."

She didn't know why she said it. She stood with her hand on the cryostat's cold flank and looked at the box on the wall, at its red light, and something at the back of her skull, the animal that lived through twenty-six years on the line, said *wait*.

The forward door opened.

It didn't slide open, the way it had the last time. It slammed back on its track, hard, and Sergeant Oyelowo came through it fast and low with her sidearm up in both hands, and behind her, in a dark coat thrown over his sleeping clothes, came Aurelian Quist.

"Hands," said Oyelowo. "Hands where I can see them. All of you. Now."

There was a tamper sensor on the box. Of course there was. Maren understood it in the half-second it took Oyelowo to come through the door: the red light was not a light, it was a sensor, and when the maintenance hatch opened the cold hold's pressure had dipped, by a breath, and the box had felt it, and woken somebody. She'd had the right of it. She'd been told to wait, and she'd waited, and it hadn't mattered at all.

Odile put her hands up. Maren put hers up.

Danek didn't.

He was between the sergeant and the cryostat, on his knees by the taped boy, and he stood up, slowly, all of him, unfolding the way he always did out of the cramped spaces of the drum, and he put himself in front of the cryostat with his arms spread a little, as if it were a child.

"Sit down, rigger," said Oyelowo.

"Can't do that," said Danek. "Sorry."

"Last time."

"I've got three kids in the Camp," said Danek Sorell, conversationally. "Tamar's got the cough. You've got a key to a box that's going to let them go to sleep. I can't sit down, Sergeant. You see that."

Oyelowo shot him.

She shot him twice, in the chest, very fast, the sidearm making two flat cracks in the cold hold like boards breaking, and Danek sat down after all. He sat down against the cryostat with a look of mild surprise and put his hand on his chest and looked at it, and then he looked up at Maren, and opened his mouth to say something, and didn't.

Odile hit Oyelowo with a pry bar.

Maren never saw where she got it from. The racks, perhaps; there were pry bars in every hold, for the crates. She came in from the side while the sergeant was still looking at Danek, low and fast, the way runners move on the line when something's going wrong, and swung it with both hands into the back of Oyelowo's knee, and then, as the sergeant went down, into her forearm, and the sidearm went skating away across the frost under the racks. Oyelowo lay on the deck holding her arm and swearing in a dull steady monotone. Odile stood over her with the bar raised, shaking.

"Don't," said Maren.

Odile didn't. She didn't put the bar down either.

Quist hadn't moved.

He was standing just inside the forward door, where he'd stopped when the shooting began. He was not looking at Danek, or Odile, or the sergeant. He was looking at the grey box on the wall beside the cryostat; and he had his hand at his throat, and in his hand, on its cord, was the key.

Maren knew, as clearly as she had ever known anything, that she could not reach him in time. He was three paces from the box. She was eight from him.

"Factor," she said.

He looked at her. His face was white and very calm.

"If you put that key in that box," Maren said, "it goes into the hold. And through the hold into the *Clement*. And through the collar into the *Patience*. Three hundred people. Your own crew. Your sergeant. That boy. You."

"Yes," said Quist.

"Is that what your orders say?"

"My orders say," said Quist, "that if the bonded of the Corva operation take up arms against the Combine, or attempt to seize Combine property, the factor is authorised to exercise Schedule H at his discretion." He said it the way he had said everything since the day he came aboard, as if he meant it, and he did. "That's what I've been sitting up for, every night for two weeks. In case this. I've known for a week you'd come, Chief. I've known since the boy Brandt came back with his hand in a sling and a note from your medic." He took a step toward the box. "I've thought about it a great deal."

"Then do it," said Maren.

Odile made a sound.

"Do it, if you're going to," said Maren. She didn't put her hands down. She stood beside the cryostat with Danek dead against its flank and the egg asleep under the frosted lid, and she looked at Quist across eight paces of frozen deck. "Put the key in the box. I can't stop you. Nobody in this hold can stop you. Do it, and go to sleep with the rest of us, and in a year they'll send four thousand new bonded to dip Gall and you'll be a line in somebody's report. An example of what can be achieved."

Quist stood with the key in his hand.

The drum thundered through the collar, far away. On the deck, Oyelowo had stopped swearing. The taped boy against the rack was crying without any sound.

"I was at Bellamy Deep," said Quist, quite quietly. "And the Hollow Moons. I wasn't at Tamsin's Rock. A man I trained was. He used to send me letters, after. Long letters. About his garden. He'd taken up gardening. He wrote very well about roses." He looked down at the key. "He hanged himself four years ago, in his greenhouse. They found him among the roses." He was silent for a moment. "I have been the factor of the Corva operation for fifteen days, Chief Tsolo, and every night of the fifteen I have sat up in that cabin with that hideous painting and this key, and asked myself whether I'm the kind of man who takes up gardening."

"And?"

"And I don't know," said Quist. "I still don't know. I've been standing here trying to find out."

He took the cord from round his neck. He held it out, at arm's length, the key swinging a little on the end of it, catching the light from the cryostat's ring of green.

"Take it," he said. "Quickly. Before I find out."

Maren crossed the eight paces and took it.

## The bridge

[
  Place: #The bridge
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Corwin Hale @@Bram Kovac @@Abel Tsolo @@Odile Arda @@Aurelian Quist @@Grandam Teska
  When: same day
]

The rest of the night came apart into pieces, and afterward Maren could never put them back in the right order.

She remembered the cryostat on its sled, the power unplugged, its own cells humming, being walked by Odile and the taped boy, untaped now and too frightened to do anything but push, back down the *Clement*'s long spine corridor toward the collar. She remembered the collar standing open, which meant Senna and Ivo had done their part, and the marine on the *Clement*'s side of it sitting against the bulkhead with his hands on his head and his ear-guards still in. She remembered Danek. She had left him in the cold hold, against the place where the cryostat had been, with his hand still on his chest and Odile's coat over his face, because there was no time; and she remembered thinking, quite clearly, *I'll come back for him*, and knowing as she thought it that she wouldn't be the one who did.

She remembered coming through the collar into the *Patience* and the noise. The drum was still running; nobody had thought to stop the dip. Over its thunder came other sounds, shouting, boots, a high thin screaming somewhere aft that went on and on and then stopped. Riggers were running in the corridors with spanners and pry bars and lengths of chain. Somebody had broken open the stores and there was a smell of spilled fuel. On the wall by the forward stair, somebody had already chalked, in big ragged capitals, OURS.

She remembered the bridge.

Hale had taken it. He had said he would and he had. His trusties stood at its two doors with their shock-sticks and the two old flechette carbines the Combine had forgotten it issued, and inside, at the consoles, there were riggers in grey coveralls who did not know how to use the consoles and were afraid to touch them, and in the middle of the deck, very calm, with his slate under his arm, stood Corwin Hale.

At his feet were two bodies.

One was a Combine marine, a big man, face down, with the back of his head wrong. The other was a clerk. Maren knew him: a thin, prim, middle-aged man called Ferrand who kept the *Clement*'s cargo books and had come across to the *Patience* most afternoons to argue with the dock section about manifests. He was lying on his back with his eyes open and his hands still half raised in front of his chest, palms out, the way you hold your hands when you are saying *please*.

Bram Kovač was standing over him. There was blood on the end of Bram's shock-stick.

"What happened?" Maren said.

"The marine came at us from the lift," said Hale. "Bram dealt with him." He nodded at the clerk. "This one came after. He was trying to get to the comms."

"He had his hands up."

"He was trying to get to the comms," Hale said again, patiently. "If he'd got to the comms he'd have raised the *Admonition*. On the long beacon. They'd have known by the morning." He looked at her. "We couldn't have that, Chief."

"He had his hands up, Hale."

"I know," said Bram Kovač. He was twenty-two and his face was white and blank and his eyes were very bright. "I saw. I did it anyway." He looked down at the clerk. "He was going to tell them."

Abel was there.

Maren hadn't seen him at first, because he was standing in the corner by the navigation console with his back to the wall, the way he stood at the edge of every crowd, and because he was so still. He was in his rigger's coveralls with a spanner in one hand, hanging down by his leg. He was looking at the clerk's body. He didn't look at her.

"Where were you?" she said.

He looked up. His face was grey. "Here," he said. "With the warden. He asked me." He swallowed. "I was on the lift door. With Bram. When the marine came."

"Did you hit him?"

"No." He looked down at the spanner as if he had just noticed it. "No. Bram did. I just—I was there."

"The rest of them," said Hale, who had been waiting for this to finish, "are in the *Clement*'s crew deck. Four marines, eleven hands, the cargo master. They put up a fight at the stair and then they didn't. We've lost two of ours. Jory from the Bucket crew and one of the dock girls, I don't know her name." He made a note on his slate. "And you've lost Sorell, I think."

"Danek's dead."

"I'm sorry." Hale said it the way he had said it to her in the drum hall the day Lusine went into Gall, with exactly the same soft regret. "He was a good rigger." He tucked the slate under his arm. "The question now is what we do with them. The crew. The marines. The factor." He nodded past her, to where Quist stood in the bridge doorway between two riggers with a cut over his eye, in his dark coat and his sleeping clothes, quite calm. "We can't keep them. We haven't the air. We haven't the people to guard them. And every one of them will talk, the moment the *Admonition* gets here." He looked at her. "I'd put them in the *Clement*'s cargo bay and open the outer doors. It's quick. It's quiet. Nobody has to see."

The bridge went very still.

"No," said Maren.

"Chief, think about it—"

"I have thought about it. No." She looked round the bridge, at Hale and Bram and the riggers at the consoles and the trusties at the doors and her brother in his corner. She was holding the key in her fist. She could feel its small hard teeth biting into her palm. "Lock them in the cargo bay. Doors shut. Give them water and the floor ration. Nobody opens anything."

"They'll talk."

"Then they'll talk," said Maren. "The *Admonition*'s coming whatever they say. Let it come and find them alive."

Hale looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, very slightly, as if she had made an interesting move in a game, and turned to Bram and gave the orders.

Teska came at dawn, helped up the bridge ladder by two young runners. She stood in the doorway in her two coats and looked at the bodies and the riggers and the trusties and the strange new faces at the consoles, and at Maren, sitting on the deck against the navigation console with her head on her knees.

"Well," said Teska. "Now she's ours."

Then she sat down beside Maren on the deck and put her old claw of a hand on Maren's knee, and they sat there together while the drum ran down at last, and the line came in, and the Bucket came home with its little useless load of night cloud; and somewhere in the comms, faintly, on the open band from the ice, they could hear the Camp. Somebody on the Kettle side had heard. Somebody had told somebody. They were cheering. Two thousand households, in the warrens of the third shaft and the second and the first, cheering on an open channel at the break of an ochre day, because the *Patience* was theirs.

## What it does

[
  Place: #The infirmary
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Aurelian Quist @@Ekene Adu
  Items: ^The Hush:#The infirmary ^Quist's key:@Maren Tsolo
  When: 18 November 2391
]

They put the cryostat in the infirmary, because it was the warmest room on the ship, and Ekene said that was the stupidest reason he had ever heard and then, when Maren explained, went white and said it was also the most sensible.

"It wakes when it's warm," Maren said. "Quist says. So it needs its own power. Its cells have three days. After that it needs a socket. The infirmary has the best sockets on the ship and the best backup power and you." She looked at him. "And you know what it is."

"I know what it is," said Ekene.

The cryostat stood in the surgical bay behind the curtain, plugged into the bay's dedicated power line, its ring of status lights glowing green, humming. Ekene had put a sheet over it. He said he couldn't work with it looking at him. Then, an hour later, he had taken the sheet off again, because, he said, it was worse not seeing.

Quist sat on the end of the nearest bunk with a dressing over his eye and his wrists tied in front of him with a length of medical tubing, which was Ekene's idea of restraint. Two riggers waited outside the door. Maren had sent them out. She sat on the bunk across from the factor with the key on its cord round her own neck now, under her coverall, where she could feel it against her breastbone, cold.

"Tell me what it does," she said.

"You know what it does. Your medic knows."

"Tell me how. Tell me how to kill it."

Quist looked at her with his one uncovered eye. Then he looked at the curtain to the surgical bay, where the cryostat hummed.

"You can't," he said.

"Tell me anyway."

He sighed. "It's an aerosol agent in a suspension, in a ceramic shell, in a cryostat. The agent is dormant below about forty kelvin. Above that, it begins to wake, slowly, and once it's awake it replicates, in a medium; it doesn't need a host to do that, only moisture and warmth, which is why it's kept so cold. The shell is designed to vent the agent at pressure, through a ring of ports along the seam. It vents on the factor's key. It also vents," he said, "if anybody does anything to it the Combine doesn't like."

"Such as."

"Such as breaching it. Drilling it. Crushing it. Cutting it. There's a pressure layer under the outer ceramic; breach that and it vents. Such as heating it. If the internal temperature rises past forty kelvin, it vents, whatever else is happening. Such as unplugging it and leaving it to warm up. Such as throwing it on a fire. Such as putting it in your fusion plant, or under your drive, or in front of a cutting torch. The heat reaches the shell before it reaches the agent, and the shell vents, and the agent goes out ahead of the heat at two kilometres a second, and you've just sprayed it all over the inside of your own engine room."

"Space," said Maren. "Throw it into space."

"It survives vacuum. It goes dormant. It drifts. A cloud of it, spores, so small you'd never see them, drifting round Gall for ten thousand years, in the same orbits as your ships, waiting for one of your intakes to swallow a breath of it." He shook his head. "The Combine designs very well, Chief. They designed this so that the people they use it on can't take it away from them and turn it round. And so that nobody who captures it can get rid of it. They call it *spite engineering*. I've heard them laugh about it in the Halloran mess."

"There's always something."

"There is one thing," said Quist slowly. "In theory."

"What?"

"Pressure and heat. Together. Sustained. Enough pressure that it can't vent, enough heat that it can't survive, for long enough to cook it through." He nodded toward the deck, toward the keel, toward what was below the keel. "The deep of a gas giant would do it. Gall would do it. A thousand kilometres down. Two. Where the air's as thick as water and as hot as a forge." He paused. "But you'd have to get it there cold. You'd have to get it down through the upper atmosphere, through the cloud-tops, through your own skim layer where your Bucket feeds, faster than the heat of entry can wake it. If it woke in the upper cloud it would vent there. Into the layer you dip. And your next load would bring it home to you." He looked at her. "Your Bucket can't go deep enough. Your line can't. Nothing you have can. Nothing anyone has can, that I know of, except a falling mountain."

Maren sat with that.

"Why didn't you?" she said, after a while. "In the hold. Turn the key."

"I told you. I didn't know."

"You know now."

"No," said Aurelian Quist. "I know I didn't. That's not the same thing." He looked at his tied hands. "There's something else you should know, Chief. The *Admonition*'s captain is a man called Lachance. I've met him. He's very courteous. He carries his own Schedule H authority, as every escort captain on an H-carrying run does, and his own orders, which I've read. If the Sanitation Agent is lost to the bonded, he is to recover it. If he cannot recover it, he is to *sanitise the operation by other means*." He met her eyes. "He has a torpedo bay. Twelve tubes. The *Patience* is a very large target, and the Camp is a very large hole in the ice."

"So holding it kills us."

"Holding it is the only reason he won't kill you on sight," said Quist. "He won't fire on a ship that might have the agent aboard. If he holed you and the cryostat lost power, it would warm, and vent, and he'd be breathing it with the rest of you once he docked to see. So he'll talk. He'll want it back. He'll offer you a great deal. And the moment he knows where it is and that it's out of your hands, he'll do what he's told." He spread his tied hands as far as the tubing let him. "You've taken a sword by the blade, Chief Tsolo. You can't keep holding it. And you can't put it down."

## There

[
  Place: #The blister
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Ekene Adu
  When: 18 November 2391
]

She went up to the blister alone, at the end of that long second day, and Ekene came after her, because he always did.

He didn't say anything. He sat down beside her on the cold floor under the scratched dome, against the frame, where she and Abel had sat two weeks before, and took her hand. Her other hand was at her throat, on the key, through the coverall. She could feel her own pulse in it.

Gall filled half the dome. The great southern storm was on the limb again, the one the riggers called Old Purple, turning slowly with its single dark eye. And above it, filling a quarter of the sky, the Lamp.

It was enormous now. Twelve days from its closest pass, coming in fast; Teska, who knew, said it would go by inside a day's flight of Gall this time, close as a dipper's spit. Its head was a hard white point too bright to look at, and its tail had split into two, then four, long pale streamers of green and gold fanning out across half the black, so delicate and so vast that it made the stars behind it look like dust. It lit the blister. It lit Ekene's face, beside her, and her own hands. You could have read by it.

"Teska says it goes out past the cold," Maren said.

Ekene said nothing.

"She says it goes out so far the sun's just a star. She says the ice out there is so cold it rings. Thirty degrees off nothing. Twenty. She says it stays out there two hundred and sixty years, and then it comes back." Maren's voice was very level. She was doing the arithmetic out loud, the way she did on the gallery when the trace was walking and the riggers were looking up at the glass. "Forty kelvin, Quist says. Below forty it sleeps. And it comes in close past Gall. Close enough to reach. If you had a fast boat, and a good line, and a tether-chief who knew how to throw."

Ekene's hand had gone still in hers.

"And it goes out," said Maren, "and nobody can follow it. Not the *Admonition*. Not anybody. Nobody can match a thing like that once it's going out. It's too fast and too far and it's going the wrong way. It'd be out past Lachance's reach in a day and past anybody's in a month. And then it's two hundred and sixty years in the cold." She was looking at the Lamp. "Two hundred and sixty years, Ekene. Long enough for the Combine to forget it ever had a thing called Corva. Long enough for everybody who wants to use it to be dead. Long enough for somebody to be wiser than us."

"And if nobody is?"

"Then it comes round," said Maren, "and they'll have to decide. Like we're deciding." She was silent for a moment. "At least it'll be them. At least it won't be Hale with the key."

Ekene was quiet for a long time.

"Teska says it takes the dead out," he said at last. "And brings them back."

"Then let it take this out," said Maren. "And let's hope to God it's dead when it comes back."

She took his hand in both of hers and held it, and they sat together under the cold dome in the light of the comet, the tether-chief and the medic, saying nothing more. Below them, behind the curtain in the infirmary, the cryostat hummed in its ring of green. Above them the Lamp burned on, coming round, as it had come round when Teska's grandmother's grandmother was a girl and before that, for a million years, for longer than there had been anyone to name it, falling in out of the dark toward the giant on its long patient orbit and going out again; and Maren Tsolo looked up at it with her brother's lie and Danek's blood and Lusine's voice and two thousand households in her chest, and she thought, very clearly, the way she thought on the line when everything else had gone quiet:

*There. We put it there.*
