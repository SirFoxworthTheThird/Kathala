# Roche

## Inside the limit

[
  Place: #The Dray
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Ozias Mbeki @@Hollis Vey @@Cato Marchetti @@Ilona Szabo:phone
  When: 30 April 2917
]

On the thirtieth of April, at the second bell, the Lamp crosses Gall's limit, and begins to die.

Til watches it happen from the bottom of the sling. The *Dray* is as deep in Gall's sky as anything built in the Tarn Reach has ever gone and come out again: so deep that the cloud-tops are a floor under her belly, ochre and white and boiling, close enough to see the shadows of the thunderheads, and the old tug is shaking from end to end with a long fine buzzing tremor that Oz says is the shield and Til hopes is the shield. The drive's at full. The board's red in four places. The giant is swinging them round its flank like a stone on a string, faster and faster, and Til has both hands on the yoke and her teeth locked and is watching, in the upper port, the Lamp.

It's coming apart.

Not all at once. Not the way a ship goes, with a flash. Slowly, almost lazily, the way a cake of old ice breaks in the bottom of a tank when the water's warmed: the long dark crack round its waist widening, widening, a black seam opening into a black gap with light pouring out of it, white jets roaring silently out of the raw new faces on either side. The two lobes of the Lamp, the two stones pressed together, beginning to drift apart. And all round them, shaken off by the pull of the giant, smaller pieces. Dozens. A slow-motion explosion of black boulders the size of stations and houses and drift-bergs and rooms, glittering where the sun catches their new-broken faces, scattering out along the comet's path like grain from a split sack.

"Thirty seconds to burn-out," says Oz, from the engine space. His voice is hoarse. "Twenty."

"I see it."

"I know you see it. Twenty seconds."

The drive cuts. The shaking stops. The *Dray* comes up off the bottom of the sling out of Gall's sky like a fish breaking water, with the giant falling away behind her and the black ahead full of the Lamp's breaking body, and for a long moment nobody in the cockpit says anything at all.

"We're on it," says Oz. "We're on its path. Four hours to match." He coughs. "That's the sling. My mother's people's sling. Nobody's done it in five hundred years." Another cough. "I'd like a drink."

Behind them, high and to port, the *Carrion Hymn* comes out of the giant's glare on her own long curve. She's made the sling too, on her own engines, which are better than the *Dray*'s; she's been flown by Szabo's captain round the bottom of Gall at a distance just safe enough to show that Szabo's captain is very good and very careful. She settles in five kilometres off the *Dray*'s starboard quarter, black against the white of the Lamp, and stays there.

"Captain Brask," says Mother Szabo's voice on the open band. "That was very exciting. I've had to have a little lie-down."

Til doesn't answer her.

Hollis Vey is at the cabin port, in his cradle, propped high on the pillows. He hasn't slept. He hasn't, Til thinks, slept for three days. His face in the light of the breaking comet is grey and shining and so thin it's almost not a face any more, and his pale eyes are wide.

"Which piece?" he says. His voice through the grille is barely there. "Captain. Which piece is it on?"

"That's the question."

"It's the question I've asked for thirty years," says Hollis. "I'd like to know the answer before Gall eats it."

Oz has the scope up. He's been running it since the bottom of the sling, sweeping the Lamp's body, both lobes and the cloud of pieces round them, looking for the one thing out here that isn't ice or rock or dust. Metal. Old metal, the kind that gives a hard bright return on the *Dray*'s salvage radar, the kind it was built to find in the wreck-fields of the outer Reach.

He finds it on the ninth sweep. On the southern lobe, the bigger of the two stones, the one that's kept most of its shape: a small hard return, very bright, at the foot of a long dark ridge on its underside.

"That's not ice," says Oz.

He puts the scope on it at full magnification. It's a smudge. A grey speck at the foot of a black wall, too small to make out. But it's square. It's got corners. Nothing in nature has corners.

"There," says Hollis Vey. "Oh, there."

"What is it?" says Cato Marchetti, in the cabin hatch. He's been there for some time. He doesn't come in.

"It's a ship," says Oz. "A little one. Sitting on the ice. Under that ridge." He sits back. "Somebody landed there, Til. Somebody landed a ship on the Lamp."

"How long ago?"

"Long enough," says Oz, "that it's still there."

Til looks at the speck, at the long black ridge, at the great southern lobe of the Lamp turning slowly in the sun with its raw white faces and its silent jets.

"Oz," she says. "Where's that lobe going? When it goes in?"

He's already running it. He's been running every piece he can track since the bottom of the sling; it's his habit, the way checking anchors is hers. It takes him a minute. When he looks up his face has changed.

"Not in," he says. "Not straight in. Not that one." He puts it on the screen: the long curve of the southern lobe's path round Gall, and the dashed circle, and the giant's limb. "The north lobe's going straight down. Dead centre. Gone. But the south one's skimming. It'll go through the cloud-tops at a slant, high, and the heat'll break it, and the pieces—" He traces them with his finger. "Some of them go in. Some of them skip. Out. Across the moons. Across Kettle." He looks at her. "It's the worst one, Til. Of all of them. It's on the worst one."

## The Gull

[
  Place: #The black ridge
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Ozias Mbeki @@Cato Marchetti:dead @@Juno Brask @@Ilona Szabo:phone @@Hollis Vey:phone
  When: 1 May 2917
]

They land on the first of May, at the fourth bell, on the southern lobe of the Lamp, two hundred metres from the old ship under the ridge.

There are five of them on the ice. Til, and Oz, and Cato Marchetti, and two of Szabo's house men, big quiet men in grey surface suits with stun-carbines slung across their chests, called Riis and Gant. Juno is on the *Hymn*. Hollis is in his cradle in the *Dray*'s cabin, on the open channel, listening to their breathing. The *Dray* sits on the ice behind them with her grapple boom unfolded under her belly and its claws sunk deep into the black crust, holding her down, the only way to hold anything down on a thing with no gravity; her big salvage winch is run out to a ground anchor, and from the anchor a surface line runs across the ice toward the ridge, and they move along it hand over hand, five figures in a row, clipped on.

The Lamp's surface is soft and black and crusted like old snow gone grey, and under the crust it glitters. The jets are everywhere. Silent white plumes going up out of pits and cracks, sudden as coughs, and coming down again in slow glittering curtains of ice dust that fall on their visors and their shoulders and sparkle in the sun. The ground trembles under their boots, a long deep shudder every few minutes that Til can feel through her soles and her knees. Over the close curved horizon of the lobe, a few kilometres off across a gap that wasn't there yesterday, the north lobe hangs in the sky like a black mountain upside down, turning, breathing white fire out of its broken face. And over everything, Gall. So close now it isn't a planet; it's a wall of weather filling half of everything.

The old ship's a scoop-tug. Oz knows it the moment he sees it close. An old pattern, a very old one, squat and armoured, sitting nose-down on the ice at the foot of the black ridge with a grapple arm buried to the elbow, frosted white along every edge. Its hatch stands open. Its cabin is empty.

There are letters on its flank, under the frost. Til wipes them with her glove.

*GULL*

And under it, smaller:

*S.V. PATIENCE*

"That was the skimmer," says Hollis's voice, very faint, on the channel. "The *Patience*. The register names her. She dipped Gall for my family for sixty years." A breath, with the hinge in it. "This was hers."

Ten metres along the ridge from the tug there's a hole.

It's half-filled with frost, glittering, a shallow round dent in the black crust a metre and a half across, with a shaft going down through the middle of it, round and clean, the width of a man's shoulders. Beside it, screwed into the foot of the black wall, there are anchors. Two. Old ones, both of them, corroded and furred with frost; one older than the other, a different pattern, with a little grey box hanging from it on a strap. A line, thin and pale and frozen stiff, runs from the older anchor down into the shaft.

"Somebody dug," says Oz.

"Somebody dug," says Til.

Cato Marchetti kneels at the shaft's lip and looks down it. He doesn't say anything. He drops a lamp on a cord, and watches it go, a little spinning star going down and down into the dark, and reads the cord when it stops.

"Forty," he says. "Forty-one." He looks up. "Riis."

Riis goes down. That was always the plan, Til understands, watching him clip on and swing his big grey body into the hole: the house men go down and bring it up, and Marchetti stands at the top with Til and Oz in front of him where he can see them. Riis goes down the shaft hand over hand, his helmet lamp going away into the dark, and they hear him on the channel, breathing.

"Ice is blue," he says, after a while. "It's blue down here. It's lit. Funny." A pause. "There's a door. At the bottom. Somebody cut a door."

Under their feet, the Lamp shudders.

It isn't like the other shudders. It goes on. It goes on and on, a long deep grinding tremor that comes up through the crust into Til's boots and her knees and her teeth, so hard that for a moment she can't see properly; and along the black ridge, fifty metres off, a jet goes up that isn't a jet at all but a sheet, a long ripping curtain of white fire tearing up out of a crack that wasn't there a second ago and roaring silently into the sky. Ice dust comes down on them like snow. Through it, Til sees the ground beyond the ridge tilt. Very slightly. The whole horizon tilting, a degree, two, the way a floe tilts when it's cracked from underneath.

"It's coming apart," says Oz, very calm. "The lobe. Under us. It's going to come apart."

"Captain Brask," says Mother Szabo, on the open band, sharply, without any sweetness in it at all. "Major. We're seeing a great deal of activity from out here. We're seeing pieces coming off that lobe. Big ones." A pause. "I would like my cargo now, please. Now."

"It's forty metres down, Mother," says Marchetti.

"Then I shall come and get it. Bring the *Hymn* in to five hundred metres, Captain Alder. We'll take it off them on a line." A pause. "And the girl can come across on the same line, Captain Brask, the moment it's aboard. Fair exchange."

Til looks across the ice at the *Dray*.

She sits on her grapple, two hundred metres off, squat and ugly and unkillable, her winch run out to the anchor. On her belly, folded up under the boom, there's a thing Til has used perhaps a dozen times in her life, on the biggest and deadest hulks in the Harrow: the harpoon. A salvage harpoon. A great barbed bolt on the end of a hundred kilometres of cable, fired by a charge, made to punch into the hull of a dead ship and hold it while the tug hauls.

Oz is looking at it too. Til can see his face through his visor. He looks at her and his eyes are wet and very happy.

"Tugs push ships," he says, on their private channel. "And pull them."

The *Carrion Hymn* comes in.

She comes in slow and careful and beautiful, the way her captain does everything, sliding down out of the dust-fall on her thrusters toward the Lamp's southern lobe, long and black against the white curtain of the jet, her ram bow lit by Gall, her four turrets swivelling to cover the ice. She stops at five hundred metres, exactly, and holds there, sitting in the sky over the black ridge like a great dark bird.

"Line, please, Captain," says Szabo.

"Oz," says Til.

Oz is already moving. He goes along the surface line to the *Dray*, hand over hand, fast for a big old man, faster than Til has seen him move in years, and Marchetti turns his head to watch him, and frowns, and says, "Mr Mbeki," and Oz reaches the *Dray*'s flank and the exterior winch panel by her grapple boom, and opens it, and puts his hand inside.

"Mr Mbeki," says Marchetti again, and draws his gun.

The harpoon fires.

There's no sound. There's a flash under the *Dray*'s belly, and a long silver streak going up from it into the sky, so fast Til can barely follow it, trailing a thread of cable behind it like a spider's line, up and up through the falling ice dust; and then, five hundred metres up, the streak meets the long black flank of the *Carrion Hymn* just aft of her bow, and stops, and the cable behind it goes taut.

"*What*," says Mother Szabo.

"Mother," says Til, on the open band. "Your ship is now tied to the Lamp. By a hundred kilometres of salvage cable, with a barbed bolt through your forward hull plating and into your frame. That's my winch at the other end of it, and my tug, and my tug's grapple is in the ice of this lobe up to the shoulder." She can hear her own voice, the weather voice, steady as a floor. "This lobe's coming apart, Mother. In an hour, maybe. And when it does, it's going into Gall. At a slant. And so are you, unless you cut that cable. And you can't cut that cable from your end, Mother, because it's tungsten-cored salvage cable and you haven't got anything that'll cut it." She takes a breath. "But I have."

There's a long silence on the open band.

"What do you want?" says Szabo.

"Juno. Across the cable. Now. In her suit. Hand over hand."

"And then you'll cut it."

"And then I'll cut it."

"And the cargo?"

"The cargo stays where it is," says Til.

On the ice beside the shaft, Cato Marchetti has his gun on her. He's been holding it on her for a minute, very steady, the way he holds everything. Now he swings it round toward the *Dray*, toward Oz, standing by the open winch panel with his hand still inside it.

"Mr Mbeki," he says. "Take your hand away from the winch."

"No," says Oz.

Marchetti shoots him.

Til sees it. She'll see it for the rest of her life. A small flash at the end of Marchetti's arm, and nothing else, no sound, the Lamp swallows sound; and Oz, two hundred metres off, by the *Dray*'s flank, jerks once, and his hand comes out of the winch panel, and he sits down on the ice against the tug's armoured hull, slowly, the way a tired man sits down at the end of a shift, with his back to the steel and his legs out in front of him and his hand on his chest.

"Oz," says Til.

"It's all right," says Oz on the private channel. His voice is quite clear. "It's all right, Til. He's too late. The brake's off. It's on the free spool. It'll run till you stop it." A breath. "Get the girl."

Then Til is moving. She doesn't decide to. She's along the line toward Marchetti before she knows it, hand over hand, three metres, two, and he's turning the gun back to her, and Gant, the house man, is unslinging his carbine, and the Lamp chooses that moment.

The ground under the shaft opens.

It goes the way the jet went, all at once, with no warning but a long rising shudder: a crack, black, a metre wide, tearing across the crust between the shaft's lip and the ridge, right under Cato Marchetti's boots; and white fire comes up out of it in a sheet. Marchetti's still on the line. He's clipped on. Til sees it, the clip at his harness, sliding. But the jet's under him, a column of gas and ice coming up out of the Lamp's raw inside at hundreds of metres a second, and it takes the line, and the anchor, and him, all together, and throws them up.

It isn't fast. Nothing on the Lamp is fast. It's very slow and very gentle and quite final. The anchor comes out of the ice like a tooth. The line goes slack and then whips. And Cato Marchetti goes up into the white fire of the jet with his arms out and his gun still in his hand, turning over and over, a small grey figure in a column of glittering light, up and up into the falling dust, and keeps going, and doesn't come down.

He doesn't say anything on the channel. Til will think about that, later. Not a word. As if he'd been expecting it, all along, for twenty years, and had only been waiting to see when.

Gant has dropped his carbine. It's floating away from him, turning slowly. He's clinging to the surface line, the part of it that's still anchored, by both hands, staring up at the jet.

"Get back to your ship," Til tells him. "Along the harpoon cable. Now. And take your friend."

Riis is coming up the shaft. She can hear him on the channel, swearing, climbing fast. When he comes out of the hole he looks once at the jet and once at Til and doesn't argue. The two big grey men go along the surface line to the *Dray* and up the harpoon cable, hand over hand, toward the *Hymn*, five hundred metres up, as fast as she's ever seen two men climb anything.

Halfway up, they meet Juno coming down.

## Hand over hand

[
  Place: #The black ridge
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Juno Brask @@Ozias Mbeki @@Ilona Szabo:phone @@Hollis Vey:phone
  When: same day
]

She comes down the cable from the *Hymn* in her small new suit, the one Til bought on Cinderhaven without saying anything, hand over hand, clipped on with a single lanyard, against a sky that's half Gall and half fire.

Til watches her all the way. She stands at the foot of the cable by the *Dray*'s flank, clipped to the tug's hull, beside Oz, who's sitting with his back against the armour and his hand on his chest and his visor turned up toward the sky; and she watches the small figure come down out of the falling ice dust, and she doesn't breathe.

The cable's moving. That's the trouble. The *Hymn* is five hundred metres up and the Lamp's lobe under them is shaking itself to pieces, and every time it shudders the cable whips, a long slow wave running up it from the *Dray*'s winch to the *Hymn*'s bow like a wave down a forty-kilometre tether, and Juno, on it, swings out and back, out and back, like a weight on a pendulum.

"Hold on," Til says on the channel. "When it whips, hold on and count. It goes by."

"*Counting*," says Juno, between her teeth.

"Captain Brask." Szabo's voice, on the open band, sharp and fast. "Captain Brask, there's a piece the size of the Ledger Ring coming off the north side of your lobe. It's coming round. My captain says it will pass within a kilometre of us in four minutes. Cut the cable."

"When she's down."

"*Now*, Captain. Or it hits us, and we go, and the cable goes, and she goes with it."

"When she's down," says Til.

"You'll kill her. You'll kill all of us." Szabo's voice has gone high. "Cut the line, Captain Brask. That's what you do. Everybody knows it's what you do. Cut it."

Til doesn't answer.

Juno is halfway. She's a hundred metres up, two hundred, swinging; Til can see her face now, a pale shape behind the visor, her mouth moving as she counts. Above her, over the curve of the lobe, something huge and black and glittering is rising into the sky, slowly, turning, a mountain of ice with white fire pouring out of its broken underside: the piece Szabo saw, coming round. It isn't going to hit the *Hymn*. Til can see that already. It's going to pass behind her. Close. Close enough to fill the sky behind her with black. Close enough that its jets will hit her.

"Juno," says Til. "Faster now."

"I'm *going* faster."

"Faster than that. Pretend it's me."

A sound on the channel that might, very nearly, be a laugh.

The piece comes round. It passes behind the *Hymn* at what can't be more than six hundred metres, a black mountain blotting out half of Gall, and its jets hit the *Hymn* broadside: a long silent blast of white that shoves the black ship sideways in the sky like a hand pushing a door. The *Hymn* rolls. Her thrusters fire, all of them, a crown of blue sparks round her hull. The harpoon cable goes taut with a jerk Til feels through the *Dray*'s armour, through her boots, through her spine, and then slack, and then taut again, and the wave runs down it toward the ground, fast, much too fast.

Juno's on it.

"Hold on," says Til.

The wave hits her. Til watches it hit. The cable snaps out sideways in a long white arc, and the small figure on it goes with it, swung out over the black ridge like a stone in a sling, out and out, her lanyard holding, holding, her gloves on the cable, and her voice on the channel, very high, counting. *Nineteen. Twenty. Twenty-one.* And then the cable comes back. And she's still on it.

"Twenty-two," says Juno. "Twenty-three."

"Good girl."

"*Don't* call me that."

"Keep counting."

Twenty metres. Ten. Til has her hands up. Juno comes down the last of the cable to the *Dray*'s flank like a runner coming in off a line, hand over hand, and Til gets her by the harness and clips her to the tug's hull with her own spare lanyard and holds her there, both of them pressed against the old scarred armour of the *Dray* with the Lamp shaking under them, and for a moment neither of them can say anything at all.

"Cut it," says Juno, breathing hard. "Cut it now. They'll go. They'll all go."

Til has the knife out already. Not a line knife. A salvager's cable-shear, a short heavy thing on her belt that the Directorate's patrols call a tin-opener; it's the only thing aboard that'll go through tungsten-cored cable, which is why she bought it. She sets its jaws on the harpoon cable where it comes out of the *Dray*'s winch, and looks up, at the black ship rolling in the sky five hundred metres overhead with fire behind it.

"Mother," she says on the open band. "Go home."

She cuts the line.

The cable goes up and away from the winch like a released spring, a long silver whip uncoiling into the sky. The *Carrion Hymn*, free, rolls once more and steadies, her thrusters flaring, and turns, slowly, scarred and smoking along her starboard flank where the jet hit her; and lights her drive, and goes. Up and out and away from the Lamp's breaking body, with a hundred kilometres of salvage cable trailing from her bow like a broken kite's string, back toward the Throat.

Mother Szabo doesn't say anything else. Her ledgers will go on. They always do. But not here.

## On its way

[

]

Oz is still sitting against the *Dray*'s flank, with his hand on his chest and his visor turned up to the sky.

Til kneels beside him on the ice. Juno kneels on his other side. Up close, through his visor, his face is grey and wet and smiling, his old bag-of-stones face, and his eyes are open, looking up at the black where the *Hymn* has gone and the white where the Lamp is burning.

"There," he says. His voice is very faint. "Told you. Tugs pull ships."

"You told me."

"Good harpoon. Good cable." He breathes, slowly. "Is she down?"

"She's down, Oz. She's here."

"Hello, Juno."

"Hello, Oz," says Juno, very quietly.

"You held the clamp," says Oz. "In the lane. I told her. You held it." His hand moves on his chest, the delicate engineer's fingers, as if feeling for something. "Til. Tell her. Tell her about the knife. Not on the Ring. Here. On the Lamp. Tell her."

"I will."

"You won't."

"I will, Oz."

"Good." He looks up at the sky. Gall fills half of it, enormous, banded, turning; and across the other half the Lamp is breaking, slowly, beautifully, its pieces spreading out along its path like a necklace with the string cut, each one trailing its own small tail of white fire. "Look at that," says Ozias Mbeki. "Look at it go. My mother would've—" He stops. He takes a breath, the last one Til hears him take, and lets it out in the old Roundsman cant, the word you say at the chapel brazier when you put in the salt. *On its way.*

Then he's quiet.

They sit with him a while, on the ice. There isn't time, and they do it anyway. Juno holds his hand. Til unclips his lanyard from the *Dray*'s hull, and clips it to her own, and doesn't let it go.

"Captain," says Hollis Vey's voice, very faint, from the cradle in the *Dray*'s cabin. He's been there all along, on the channel, listening to everything. "Captain, I'm so sorry."

"I know."

"The lobe," says Hollis. "Captain. The lobe's coming apart. The shaft. Whatever's down there—"

"I know," says Til.

She stands up. Juno stands up with her.

## The door at the bottom

[
  Place: #The chamber
  Characters: @@Til Brask @@Juno Brask
  When: same day
]

The shaft is where it was. The jet that took Marchetti has died back to a long glittering curtain of dust, and the crack beside the shaft's lip has stopped widening, for now; the older of the two anchors is still in the ice, and the stiff pale line from it still runs down into the dark. Til clips them both to it.

"You don't have to come," she says.

"I'm witnessing," says Juno. "It's my job."

They go down.

It isn't dark. Riis said so, and Riis was right. Ten metres down, the black of the crust gives way to ice, and the ice is blue, and lit: a deep strange glowing blue, like the inside of a lamp, the sun forty metres overhead pouring down through it like light down through deep water. The walls of the shaft are blue glass. Their breathing is very loud in their helmets. Every few seconds the whole shaft trembles, a long grinding shudder that comes up from somewhere far below, and ice dust drifts down past their lamps, glittering.

At the bottom, on the left, there's a door.

An arch, waist-high, cut roughly into the shaft's side with a pick; you can see the marks. Through it, a little round room in the blue ice.

Til goes in first, on her hands and knees. Juno comes after her.

Somebody is sitting against the far wall.

They're sitting with their back to the blue ice and their knees drawn up and their arms round their knees and their head bowed forward on to their arms, the way you sit when you're very tired and you've decided to rest your eyes for a moment. They're wearing a coat. An old heavy quilted pressure-coat, patched at the knees, with its hood up over a helmet, gone stiff and white with frost. They're so still they're not like a person at all, but like something that grew there, out of the ice, very slowly, over a very long time.

Juno stops in the arch behind her. Til feels her stop.

Neither of them says anything. The Lamp shudders round them, and the blue light trembles on the walls, and ice dust falls past the arch like snow; and in the little round room forty metres down in the breaking comet, the figure in the coat sits on, with its head on its knees, waiting, as it has waited for longer than anybody alive has been alive, to see who would come.
