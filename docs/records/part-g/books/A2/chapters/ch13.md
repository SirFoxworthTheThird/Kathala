# Shares

## Three hundred names

[
  Place: #The Drum
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Corwin Hale @@Abel Tsolo @@Odile Arda @@Grandam Teska @@Ekene Adu
  Items: ^The Share Plate:#The Drum
  When: 22 November 2391
]

The Plate was Hale's idea.

He brought it to her on the twentieth, three days after the night in the cold hold, when the ship had begun to understand that it belonged to itself and did not yet know what that meant. The crew had been dipping, because Teska said they must: *we keep her flying, we keep the Camp breathing, the rest is talk.* They had been sleeping in shifts and eating whatever the *Clement*'s stores held, which was more and better than anybody aboard had eaten in years. Kettle Camp had sent up a shuttle-load of children to look at the ship that was theirs now, and the children had run shrieking up and down the drum gallery until Teska threw a boot at them. And the bonded had begun, in small groups, in corners, to argue.

They argued about everything. Who would run the bridge. Who would hold the stores. Whether the Camp's two thousand households were partners or passengers. Whether the prisoners in the *Clement*'s cargo bay should be fed from the ship's ration or the *Clement*'s. Whether anybody was in charge, and if so who, and if not why not. Maren had found herself in the middle of most of it, because people came to her, and she was too tired to send them away.

"They need something to hold on to," Hale said. He had come up to the gallery at the end of a dip, slow and heavy, with his slate under his arm, and stood beside her at the board looking down at the drum. "Something with their names on it. They've never owned anything in their lives. Not even their own debts; the Combine owned those." He nodded down at the drum hall. "Let them sign for the ship. All of them. Every one of the crew. One share each. Put it in steel, where everybody can see it."

"And the Camp?"

"The Camp's the Camp." Hale shrugged his big shoulders. "The crew took the ship. The crew owns the ship. The Camp can have the air. That's fair, I think." He looked at her mildly. "We can talk about the Camp after the *Admonition*. Nobody's going to argue about shares with a cutter off the bow."

She should have argued. She knew it at the time, and knew it far better afterward. But she was very tired, and the *Admonition* was coming, and Hale was right that nobody would argue about shares with a cutter off the bow, and she said yes.

The welders cut the Plate from a section of the *Patience*'s inner hull on the twenty-first, a rough grey rectangle of plate steel the size of a door panel, with its edges black and wavy from the torch. They bolted it to the end of the drum, at chest height, where everybody could reach it. And on the twenty-second, at the change of shift, the whole crew of the *Patience* came into the drum hall, three hundred less the dead, and lined up, and one by one, with whatever they had, they cut their names into it.

Hale wanted Maren's name first.

"You led them," he said, in front of the hall, with his soft voice raised just enough. "You went into the cold hold. You took the key. Your name goes first. That's only right." And the hall cheered, and somebody pushed her forward, and she stood in front of the blank grey steel with a rigger's scribe in her hand, a hardened awl for marking cable, and found she couldn't think how to spell her own name.

She cut it in the end. MAREN TSOLO, at the top of the first column, in shaky capitals, pressing so hard the awl skidded on the second T and left a long scratch. The hall cheered again.

After that it went quickly. Teska cut hers, with Odile holding her hand steady. Odile cut hers, and then stood looking at the steel for a long moment with the awl in her fist, and then cut, very small, beside it, LUSINE, and nobody said anything. Pirro. Senna and Ivo, side by side. Riggers, runners, dock hands, galley hands, the Bucket crews, the drum crews. Hale cut his own name halfway down the second column, neat and deep, like the hand on his forms. Bram Kovač cut his, frowning, his tongue between his teeth like a child. Some of them couldn't write; they cut a mark, a cross or a star or a little drawing, and somebody who could write wrote the name beside it. Somebody cut DANEK SORELL for Danek, and somebody cut the names of Jory from the Bucket crew and the dock girl whose name it turned out was Mira; and Teska, watching, said that was right, the dead ought to have their share, and the hall agreed.

Abel cut his near the end. He came up laughing, with a bottle of the *Clement*'s brandy in one hand, and made a show of it, bowing to the hall, flourishing the awl like a duelling sword; and the young riggers whooped, and he cut ABEL TSOLO in big careless sloping letters across a whole column's width, so that the next man had to squeeze his in underneath. Then he turned and grinned at Maren across the hall, the old grin, *don't worry, it's me*.

She smiled back at him. She would remember that, too.

When the last name was cut the hall drank. The *Clement*'s stores held a great deal of brandy. Ekene, who drank very little, stood beside her at the back under the drum with a cup he wasn't drinking, watching the crew dance on the deck of the drum hall to somebody's squeezebox, and said, "They're happy."

"They are."

"You're not."

"I'm counting," said Maren. "Two thousand households in the Camp. Three hundred names on the Plate."

"You agreed."

"I agreed." She looked at the Plate, at her own shaky capitals at the top, at Abel's big careless ones across the column. "I'll fix it. After."

"After what?"

"After the *Admonition*," said Maren. "After the Lamp."

## The warden's ledger

[
  Place: #The tether deck
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Odile Arda
  When: 23 November 2391
]

Odile came to find her the next night, in the gallery, after the second dip, and she had a ledger.

It was the warden's ledger: a fat grey slate in a hard case with Hale's mark on the back, the one he carried everywhere under his arm like a priest's book. Except it wasn't that one, Odile said, setting it down on the board between them. That one was still under Hale's arm. This was its twin, the backup, the copy the warden's office kept in a locked drawer in case the first was dropped down a shaft. Odile had opened the drawer with a cable-cutter at the third bell while Hale was on the bridge.

"I wanted to see her account," Odile said. "Lusine's. The form he made me sign. I wanted to see it in his book." Her face was very white and quite steady. "So I looked."

"And?"

"And I found hers. And then I looked at the days before." She turned the slate toward Maren and touched it, and a page came up, and Maren read it.

It was the warden's daily record for the first of November, the day before the dip. Duty rosters. Ration issues. Two disciplinary notes. And near the bottom, a line in Hale's neat hand, in a column marked *Reports to Factor*: *Informant re: Runner L. ARDA (relay). Unauthorised access to Combine dispatch traffic. Discussing contents with crew. Forwarded to Factor-designate by beacon, 01.xi. Advised: contain.*

"*Contain*," said Odile.

Maren read it again.

"He sent it to Quist," Odile said. "On the beacon. Before the *Clement* even got here. That Lusine had read the dispatch and was talking. And Quist said contain." She took the slate back and touched it again. "And then I looked at the day after. The third. The day after she died. At the accounts page, where he writes up anything he's credited or debited on his own authority. Rewards. Fines."

Another page. Another line, near the bottom, in the same neat hand.

*TSOLO, A. (Rigger). Special duty, warden's discretion. Credit 2,400 marks against outstanding markers held by warden's office. Account cleared.*

Maren looked at it for a long time.

The gallery was quiet. Below them, through the long window, the drum hall was empty, the drum still, the Plate a small grey rectangle on its end, too far away to read. Somewhere aft, faintly, someone was still playing the squeezebox.

"He owed Hale," said Odile. "For gambling. Everybody knows Abel owed Hale. Everybody knows Hale holds half the riggers' markers on this ship." Her voice was perfectly level. "And Lusine was seeing somebody funny who was always in debt and didn't care. And she told somebody about the dispatch, and she shouldn't have. And Hale reported her to the factor the next morning. And the factor said *contain*. And the next day her clip went. And the day after that, your brother's debt was paid."

"Odile."

"I'm not asking you anything, Chief." Odile picked up the slate and held it against her chest. "I'm telling you. Because you're the chief. Because you'd want to know." She looked at Maren with her swollen fierce young face. "And because I want you to know I know. So that when I do something, you won't be surprised."

She went down the gallery ladder without another word.

## The jeweller's file

[
  Place: #The Belly
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Abel Tsolo
  When: same day
]

Abel was in the Belly.

Maren knew where to find him. He'd gone there to sleep off shifts since he was sixteen; he'd gone there the night their mother went into Gall, and she had crawled along the cable tray with a lamp in her teeth and found him curled up between two bearing housings, three years old, asleep with his thumb in his mouth. He was sitting in the same place now, between the same two housings, with a lamp on the deck plates beside him and a bottle beside the lamp, and when she came out of the end of the cable tray on her hands and knees he looked up at her and knew.

She could see him know. It went across his face like a shadow across a hull.

"Odile," he said.

"Odile."

"She broke into his office."

"She broke into his office." Maren sat down on the deck plates opposite him, cross-legged, with the lamp between them. "Tell me, Abel."

He didn't. He sat and looked at the lamp. His hands were shaking, the small fine tremor she'd seen in the blister, and he put them between his knees to stop them, and they didn't stop.

"Tell me," she said again. "Or I'll tell you, and you can say whether I've got it right. I'd rather you told me."

He told her.

He told it badly, the way he'd always told things he'd done wrong, in pieces, out of order, backing up, starting again. He'd owed Hale since he was twenty-two. Cards, mostly. Some dice. A bad night three years ago that had put him two thousand under on the markers, and Hale had held the markers and said there was no hurry, no hurry at all, and then asked him to keep his ears open in the rigger's mess. Just keep his ears open. Who was saying what about the rations, about the Combine, about the warden. Nothing much. Gossip. Hale took a little off the markers every time. Abel had told himself it wasn't anything; everybody knew Hale had ears everywhere; what was one more pair.

And then Lusine.

"She was lovely, Mar," he said. "She was so—she laughed at everything. She thought I was funny. Nobody thinks I'm funny any more, they think I'm Abel. And she came to me in the lock after shift, the first of November, all white and shaking, and she said she'd read something on the relay and she had to tell somebody and she couldn't tell Odile, because Odile would do something stupid. And she told me. About Sorrel and the Camp and everything." He swallowed. "And I went to Hale. That night. I don't know why. I do know why. I was frightened. I thought, if this gets out, there'll be trouble, and if there's trouble Hale'll want to know where it came from, and he'll know I knew, and I'll be—I don't know what I thought. I went to Hale. I told him."

"And he told you to cut her clip."

"No. Not like that. He'd never say it like that." Abel laughed, a horrible sound. "He said she was a danger to herself. He said if she went round the ship talking about Sorrel there'd be a riot and the factor would bring the marines down on us and people would get hurt. He said what she needed was a scare. Something to make her think twice. A slip in the lock, at her check, when she loaded her clip. Something to shake her up." He looked at Maren with wet red eyes. "He gave me a file. A fine one, a jeweller's file. He said, *take the spring two-thirds through. It'll go when she loads it at the lock check. She'll get a fright and a bruise and she'll think about what she says.* He said—he said he'd clear the markers. All of them. Twenty-four hundred."

"And you did it."

"I did it. The night before. In the runners' lock, while she was in the shower block." He put his face in his hands. "It was supposed to go at the lock check, Mar. That's what he said. It was supposed to go when she loaded it, in the lock, with the deck under her. I thought it would. I lay in my bunk the whole dip waiting for somebody to say Arda's clip went at the check and she's got a bruise. I was waiting for it. And then the shear came and the line walked and I heard her on the channel and I knew, I knew, I knew it hadn't gone at the check, it had held, it had held just long enough—"

He stopped.

Maren sat on the cold deck plates of the Belly, with the lamp between them, and looked at her brother.

She had carried him on her hip through the warrens of the third shaft. She had wiped his nose and taught him to read the duty board and stood between him and two drunk dock hands when he was fifteen and had said something smart. She had sat in the gallery for four hours and fifty minutes with her hand on the damper and Lusine Arda's voice going faint on channel four, and run the dip. *I don't think it was the clip.*

"Hale told you it would go at the lock check," she said.

"Yes."

"Hale's been on this ship twenty years. He knows how a clip fails. He knows a spring two-thirds through holds in calm and goes in shock." Her voice was very far away. "He knew it wouldn't go in the lock, Abel. He knew it would go on the line."

Abel took his hands away from his face.

"He knew," said Maren. "He used you to kill her. Because she'd read the dispatch and she was talking. Because the factor said *contain*." She stood up. Her knees were stiff from the cold of the deck. "And then he came to the Belly and offered us his sticks."

"Mar—"

"Don't."

"What are you going to do?"

She looked down at him. Her little brother, twenty-nine years old, the same height as her and twice as handsome, with their mother's eyes.

"I don't know," she said, and it was true. "The *Admonition*'s overdue. She could come any hour. Hale has the bridge and the trusties and both the carbines. If I stand up in the drum hall and tell three hundred people that the warden's a Combine informer who had a runner killed, half of them won't believe me and the other half will go for him, and his trusties will fight, and there'll be blood on the deck with a cutter off the bow." She heard her own voice, the tether-chief's voice, doing the arithmetic. "So I don't know. Not tonight."

"And me?"

She didn't answer.

She crawled back along the cable tray in the dark without her lamp, and left him there with his.

## The Admonition

[
  Place: #The bridge
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Aurelian Quist @@Corwin Hale @@Severin Lachance:phone
  When: 24 November 2391
]

The *Admonition* came out of the black on the twenty-fourth, two days late, and hailed them on the open band at the first bell of the morning.

She was a small ship. That was the first thing everybody said, crowding the gallery windows and the blister and the forward ports to look: a slim grey dart with a short stubby bow, a third the length of the *Clement*, with the green wheel on her flank. She didn't look like anything. Then she turned, a few thousand kilometres off, showing them her length, and they saw the twelve dark mouths in her bow, in two rows, and the hall went quiet.

Captain Lachance had a pleasant voice. Elderly, unhurried, slightly amused, with the accent of the Halloran schools. He addressed himself, he said, to *the persons presently in unlawful occupation of the Combine vessels* Patience *and* Clement, and he hoped they were well, and he regretted the circumstances. He had been informed by beacon of a disturbance aboard the *Patience*. He would be grateful to speak to the factor.

They brought Quist to the bridge. They let him speak. He said, in his grave tired voice, that he was alive and unhurt and being reasonably treated, and that the bonded held the Sanitation Agent, and the key; and then a rigger took the handset away from him.

"Thank you, Factor," said Lachance. A short silence. "In that case, I'll speak to whoever is in charge."

There was a pause on the bridge. Everyone looked at everyone else.

"Nobody's in charge," said Maren, and took the handset.

"I see," said Lachance pleasantly. "Then I'll speak to nobody. My terms are these. You will return the factor and his staff and the *Clement*'s crew, unharmed. You will return the Sanitation Agent, unopened, with its key, in its cryostat, to my ship. You will surrender the vessels *Patience* and *Clement* to my officers. In return, the bonded complement of the *Patience* will be relocated as previously planned, to Sorrel, with their accounts adjusted to reflect the costs of this disturbance. Persons identified as having led the disturbance will be returned to Halloran for the Combine's courts. There will be no other reprisals. You have until the twenty-sixth." A courteous pause. "I would ask you to consider that I am not obliged to offer terms at all."

"And the Camp?" said Maren.

"The Kettle installations are outside my instructions," said Lachance. "Good morning."

## Cut them before they cut us

[
  Place: #The Drum
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Corwin Hale @@Grandam Teska @@Odile Arda @@Abel Tsolo @@Bram Kovac
  When: same day
]

They argued all day. By the evening the argument had filled the drum hall.

Everybody came. The crew. The Camp's delegates, who had come up on the shuttle and would not go back down. The children, again, though Teska didn't throw a boot at them this time. They stood and sat on every inch of the drum hall's deck and hung off its galleries and its ladders, three hundred of the crew and twice that many from the Camp, with the drum still and silent at the end of the hall and the Plate on its end, three hundred names cut in steel, and the whole of it lit from above through the gallery windows by the Lamp.

Hale spoke first.

He stood on the gallery stair, three steps up, so that everyone could see him. He didn't raise his voice. He had never needed to. He talked to them the way he had talked to them every day for twenty years when he read out the duty roster, patiently, reasonably, as if he were sorry to have to say it.

"We have a weapon," he said. "We didn't ask for it. They brought it here, to use on us. It's in our infirmary now, in its box, with its key. And there's a ship off our bow with twelve torpedoes and orders to take it back." He spread his hands. "They're offering us Sorrel. They're offering to try our leaders in their courts. They're offering the Camp nothing. Nothing at all. And they'll keep their word on every bit of it. The Combine always keeps its word."

A rumble from the hall.

"But they can't fire on us," said Hale. "Not while we have it. The factor told us so; the factor knows. They daren't hole us in case it warms and vents and drifts back on them. So they sit there and talk. And they'll sit there and talk until we give in, or they'll sit there until another ship comes, and that one won't talk."

"So what do we do?" somebody shouted.

"We give it back to them," said Hale.

Silence.

"We give them exactly what they asked for," said Hale softly. "The Sanitation Agent, in its cryostat. Returned. With the key. We put it on a crawler, the way we send a dead runner down the line, and we send it across to the *Admonition*'s docking port, and we tell Captain Lachance we accept his terms. And when it's at his lock, we turn the key." He looked round the hall. "Eighty people. Marines who'd shoot us on the gun-decks at Sorrel. A captain who'd let the Camp freeze. And then there's no ship off our bow, and no torpedoes, and nobody to take anyone anywhere. The Combine won't send another for a year. Maybe never. And we'll be free."

The hall made a noise Maren had never heard it make. Not a cheer. Something lower and hungrier, like the drum spinning up.

"Cut them," said Hale, "before they cut us."

People were shouting now. Some of them were shouting yes. Maren looked out over the hall, at the faces in the Lamp's light, and saw the yes on a lot of them: on young riggers and old dock hands, on Camp men with children on their shoulders. She saw Bram Kovač at the foot of the stair with his shock-stick, nodding, his eyes very bright. She saw Abel at the edge of the crowd by the riggers' ladder, grey-faced, not shouting anything, looking at the floor.

She saw Hale look at her, across the hall, mildly, waiting.

She went up the gallery stair. She went past him, three steps higher, and turned round, and the hall saw her, and went quiet in waves.

"I've been in the cold hold," Maren said. "I've seen it. I've seen what's stencilled on it." She didn't raise her voice either. She used the tether-chief's voice, the one that went out on the line channel to runners at twenty kilometres with Gall under them. "Ekene Adu saw what it did. At a place called Tamsin's Rock. Four thousand people. He went through every bunk. Every child. He wrote down the times." She looked out over them. "If we turn that key on the *Admonition*, we're the Combine. That's all. We're them. We've got the box and we use it, the way they'd have used it on us. We'll have killed eighty people in their sleep to save ourselves and we'll be the people who did that, for ever."

"For ever's a long time, Chief," somebody called. "Sorrel's soon."

A laugh. An ugly one.

"There's something else," said Maren. "The *Admonition*'s got a beacon. Every Combine ship does; it talks to Halloran on the long band every day. If she goes quiet, Halloran knows. And the next ship they send won't be a cutter with a captain who talks. It'll be three cutters, and they won't hail us, and they won't care what we've got in a box. They'll put twelve torpedoes in the *Patience* from ten thousand kilometres and twelve more in the Camp, and they'll write it up as an example of what may be achieved." She looked round the hall. "We can't keep it. If we keep it they'll come for it for ever. And we can't use it, unless we want to be what they are. So we put it where nobody can use it. Not them. Not us."

"Where?" shouted Hale's voice, below her. Mild. Interested.

Maren lifted her hand and pointed up, through the gallery windows, at the blaze in the sky.

"There," she said.

She told them. The Lamp, six days from its closest pass, going in by Gall close enough for the Gull to reach, if the *Patience* threw her right. Out past the cold for two hundred and sixty years, where the ice was so cold the egg would sleep as long as the Lamp did. Out past the reach of the *Admonition*, of any ship, the moment it was going. Gone. Not used. Not kept. Gone.

"And when it comes back?" said Hale.

"Then it comes back to somebody else," said Maren. "And they'll have to decide. Like us. And maybe they'll be wiser."

The hall was very quiet.

"The tether-chief's right," said a voice from the bench by the drum.

Teska had got to her feet. She was holding on to Odile's arm. She stood there very small in her two coats with her one good eye shining, and the hall turned to look at her.

"I've been on this ship sixty-one years," said Teska. "I've seen the Lamp once before, in the old pictures my grandmother had. It takes the dead out. It brings them back." She looked up at it through the windows. "Let it take this out. Let it take this dead thing out, before it makes us dead things too."

They voted by hands. It was close. Afterward, nobody could agree how close; some said by forty and some by four. But the hands for the Lamp were more than the hands for the key, and Teska counted them, and said so, and nobody argued with Teska.

Hale, on the stair below her, looked up at Maren with his mild patient face and smiled, very slightly, the way he had smiled on the bridge when she told him to lock the prisoners in the cargo bay. As if she had made an interesting move.

"The tether-chief's plan, then," he said to the hall. "Of course."

## My line

[
  Place: #The Belly
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Abel Tsolo @@Odile Arda
  When: 25 November 2391
]

Odile went for Abel the next night, in the Belly, with her line knife.

Maren had been waiting for it. She'd told Odile she'd want to know, and Odile had told her she'd know, and so for two nights Maren had slept in her clothes on the gallery floor with her boots on, and on the second night, at the third bell, she heard a shout in the drum hall below, and was down the ladder and across the deck and behind the number four brake and into the cable tray before she was properly awake.

They were in the hollow between the two bearing housings, where Abel always slept. The lamp had been knocked over and was lying on its side throwing a long low wedge of light across the deck plates. Abel was on his back. Odile was on top of him, kneeling on his arms, with her hook-knife in her fist, and there was blood on his face, a long line of it from his eyebrow down his cheek, black in the lamplight. He wasn't fighting her. That was the thing Maren saw. He was lying there with his arms under her knees and his face turned up to her, and he wasn't fighting.

"Odile," said Maren.

"Go away, Chief."

"Odile, put it down."

"He cut her line." Odile's voice was very calm. "He cut her line, and he lay in his bunk while she went into Gall. I'm going to cut his. That's all. It's fair. It's the only fair thing anybody's done on this ship since I was born."

"I know."

"You'd stop me."

"Yes," said Maren.

"Why?" Odile looked round at her, and her face in the wedge of lamplight was wet. "Because he's yours? Because he's your brother? She was my sister, Chief. She was my *sister*."

"Because I need him," said Maren. "For six days. That's all. The *Admonition*'s off our bow and Hale's got the bridge and I have to throw a tug at a comet, and I need every rigger on the drum who knows how to set a sling. He's the best I've got on the number three brake. You know he is." She came forward a step, slowly, her hands open. "After the Lamp, Odile. After. I swear it on the line. When it's gone and the Gull's thrown and the *Admonition*'s gone home, I'll give him to you. I'll put him in front of the whole drum hall and tell them what he did and what Hale did, and you can do what you want, and I won't stop you." Her voice cracked. "I swear it on the line. On Lusine's name on the drum."

Odile knelt there a long time with the knife in her fist.

Then she got up off Abel's arms, and stood, and wiped the blade on her leg, and walked past Maren into the cable tray without looking at either of them, and was gone.

## Twelve stitches

[
  Place: #The infirmary
  Characters: @@Maren Tsolo @@Ekene Adu @@Abel Tsolo
  When: same day
]

Ekene stitched him in the infirmary, at the fourth bell, behind the curtain, beside the humming cryostat. Twelve stitches, from the eyebrow to the cheekbone. Abel lay on the surgical table and didn't make a sound.

Maren sat on the end of the nearest bunk outside the curtain and waited. When Ekene came out, drying his hands, she said it. She had decided on the gallery floor, two nights ago, without sleeping, and she had not told anybody, and she said it now, to him, in the quiet of the infirmary with the curtain half open and the green ring of the cryostat glowing through it.

"I'm going to fly the Gull."

Ekene stopped drying his hands.

"Somebody has to," she said. "The Gull goes out on the sling and she meets the Lamp near the bottom of its pass, and somebody has to be in her to land her, and dig, and put the egg deep enough in the ice that it stays cold. Somebody who knows how to work on a body that's falling. Somebody who can think when everything's walking." She looked at her hands. "And she can't come back, Ekene. The sling'll throw her fast enough to catch the Lamp, and then she's going the Lamp's way, and she hasn't got the fuel to turn round. Not by half. Whoever goes, goes out with it."

"No," said Ekene.

"It's the only way."

"Then somebody else."

"Who? Odile? She's nineteen. Teska? She's eighty-three. One of the riggers? A dock hand? Hale?" She laughed, a short hard sound. "I'm the tether-chief. It's my plan. I stood up in the drum hall and told them to put it on the Lamp. I'm not going to ask anybody else to ride it."

"Maren." He came and sat beside her, and took her hands, both of them, in his long gentle cold ones, and held them very hard. "Maren, please. There must be another way. Let the sling throw it. Let it go on its own. On a drone, a crawler—"

"A crawler can't land on a comet. A drone can't dig. If it just hits the Lamp it'll bounce, or break, or lie on the surface in the sun, and warm, and vent." She shook her head. "It has to be put down, Ekene. Deep. By hand. By somebody who'll stay with it till it's cold." She looked at their hands together on her knee. "Somebody has to carry it. It's my line."

He didn't say anything. He sat holding her hands and wept, without any sound, the way Odile had wept in the Belly, and she let him, and didn't weep, because she hadn't in nine years and wasn't about to start now.

Behind the curtain, on the surgical table beside the humming cryostat, with twelve stitches in his face and his eyes open in the dark, Abel lay very still and listened to every word.
