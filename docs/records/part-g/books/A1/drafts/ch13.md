## The Turn

The swell slowed on the eleventh of April at nine minutes past three in the morning, exactly where the sum on the back of the envelope had said it would.

Ambrose had been waiting for it for ten nights. He had stopped going to his bunk. He slept in the Lectern, in his chair, in two-hour snatches between readings, with the headphones round his neck and the buoy's feed running in one ear and the harp's in the other; and every night he logged the swell's period, the slow rise and fall under the throat's chord, to the second, in pencil, in the little column in his notebook that had begun on the night of the first of March with the word *probably*. Four minutes and eleven seconds. Four minutes and nineteen. Four twenty-six. Lengthening, night by night, as the long tide approached its turn, exactly as a pendulum lengthens its swing when you let the string out — exactly as the sum said. He had told himself every night that it was not proof. That a curve can be made to fit anything, if you want it badly enough. That he wanted it very badly.

At nine minutes past three on the eleventh the period lengthened by eleven seconds in a single swing, which it had never done; and then by eleven again; and he sat bolt upright in his chair with the headphones pressed to his ears and listened to the long tide under the Needle slow, and slow, and hang — he could hear it hanging, a vast slow pause at the bottom of something, like a breath held at the end of an exhalation — and then, at nineteen minutes past four, begin, very faintly, to come back.

Not the waist. The waist would go on narrowing for another eighteen days; the short breath, the Narrowing itself, still had eighteen days of falling in it. But the long tide underneath it had turned. He could hear it. Anyone could have heard it, with the headphones on, who knew what to listen for.

He sat until it was light — what the station called light — with the headphones on, and wept, quietly, for no reason he could have explained to anybody, and then he got up and washed his face in the little sink at the back of the dome and went to work.

He made it into a proof. It took him two days. He knew by then exactly what a proof had to be, for this: not a hypothesis, not a curve on an envelope, but a thing that could be put in front of somebody who did not want to believe it and could not be argued with. So he made three. He made the numbers: the old paper traces, corrected against the fork, the four Narrowings laid side by side with the swell drawn through them, and the swell's turn at three in the morning on the eleventh plotted on the same axes, sitting exactly on the line. He made the prediction: the date and the hour the waist would bottom out, at a little above twenty-one per cent, on the first of May; the date and the hour it would begin to recover, on the second, with nothing done to it; the curve of its recovery, day by day, out to the end of May. Something that could be checked. Something that would be true or false in front of everyone.

And he made the sound.

That was the one he cared about. He took the buoy's feed and the harp's, the raw sound of the throat, and ran it through a filter he built that night from the harp's own spare resonators, tuned — he did it by hand, with the fork, string by string — to pass only the long low swell and nothing else. Then he sped it up. A thousand times. So that a month of the long tide went by in forty minutes, and three hours in eleven seconds. And he sat in the Lectern at dawn on the twelfth with the headphones on and played it.

It was a breath. That was all. A vast slow breath, in and out, in and out, very deep, like someone sleeping in the next room. And at the end of the forty minutes, the last few seconds — the eleventh of April — you could hear it reach the bottom of an exhalation, and hang there, and begin to draw in again.

Anyone could hear it. A child could hear it. You did not need a theory. You only needed ears.

He copied all three onto a data spool, and put the spool in his breast pocket with the unsent letters to his daughter, and the fork in his jacket pocket, and sat for a moment longer in the grey light under the dome.

Then he went to find Teo Kesh.

## The Shaft-Head

The strike was nine days old and the shaft-head looked like a siege, because it was one.

Brandt's men had not come in. They had not gone away either. There were sixty of them now, in a ring along the fence, in shifts, day and night, and on the fifth day they had shut off the water main to the shaft-head and on the seventh the power to the canteen, and the cutters were living on what could be carried in at night over the back of the slag heaps from the Pans. Teo had been carrying it. She had flown the Mercy round to the Pans after every run and loaded her cargo bay with whatever the Lantern Row kitchens would give, and walked it in over the slag in the dark with her helmet lamp off, three or four times a night. Brandt knew. She was certain he knew. His men had watched her go past, twice, and said nothing. She did not understand it and did not like it.

She was sitting on the canteen step at noon on the thirteenth with a bowl of Fat Diz's salt-eel stew going cold in her lap when Ambrose Lyle came up the access road from Tallow in his cardigan, alone, past the men at the gate — who looked at him and let him through, the way they let Mother Salome through — and across the yard to her, with a portable reader under his arm and his face grey with no sleep.

She did not get up. She had not seen him since the dock, since she spat.

He stopped in front of her.

"I need you to listen to something," he said. "And your brother. And Mother Salome. And as many of the others as will come." He hesitated. "And then you can spit again, if you want. I'd understand."

She looked at him. Then she put the bowl down on the step and went to get Nico.

They played it in the canteen, in the dark, because the power was off. Ambrose had brought batteries. He set the reader up on a table at the front with a pair of old speakers from the Lectern, and the cutters came in from the yard and the galleries and the cage-head, two hundred of them, filthy, hungry, sitting on the tables and the benches and the floor with the dust of nine days on them, and Mother Salome at the front with the Tally across her knees in its blanket, and Nico beside her. Teo stood at the back against the wall. She did not know why. She thought perhaps she wanted to be able to leave.

Ambrose told them who he was. He did not need to; half of them knew his name from the charts, and the other half had heard it in the Lantern. He told them what the harp was, and the buoy, in a few sentences. He told them about the swell. He did not use the word *beat*. He did not use any word they would have to ask about. He said, *The Needle breathes. Twice. A short breath every four thousand days, and a long one, much slower, underneath. When the short one comes at the bottom of the long one, it goes deep. That's what's happening now. That's all that's happening.*

Then he said, "I'm going to play you the long one. Forty days of it, in forty minutes. You don't have to understand anything. Just listen."

He played it.

Teo had expected — she did not know what she had expected. Numbers. A noise. What came out of the old Lectern speakers in the dark canteen was a breath. A vast, slow, sleeping breath, in and out, deep and soft and patient, filling the room the way the harp filled the dome, so that after a minute or two you stopped hearing it as a sound at all and started feeling it, in your chest, in time with your own. In and out. In and out. Two hundred people sat in the dark and breathed with the Needle, and nobody moved, and nobody spoke. Somebody near the front was crying. Teo found, after a while, that she was holding the bead at her throat so hard that its edge had cut into her palm.

At the end — the last few seconds — the breath went out, and out, and out, longer than any before, until it seemed it must stop; and hung there, at the bottom, for one long, terrible, silent moment.

And began to come back.

Ambrose switched the reader off. The canteen was perfectly silent.

"That was two days ago," he said into the dark. "The eleventh of April. The long breath turned. It's coming back now. The short one will go on down until the first of May, because it has to, and then it'll come back too, on its own, the way it always has — because the long one's carrying it. Whatever we do. Whatever anybody puts into it or doesn't." He took a breath of his own. "The Needle doesn't need us. It never did. It isn't starving. It isn't closing. It's breathing. And on the second of May it will start to open, and by the end of May it'll be as open as it was in January, and that'll be true whether nine pilots fly into it on the first or none."

Nobody said anything for a long time.

"And Corrigan," said Teo from the back.

Two hundred heads turned.

Ambrose found her in the dark. "Corrigan went in at the start of a pause that was coming anyway," he said. "The Director knew when the pause was. She asked my assistant. I didn't tell her. I didn't stop her either." His voice did not change. "He died for nothing, Teo. I'm sorry. I should have said so on the dock, the way I'm saying it now. I didn't, because I wanted to go home." He was quiet for a moment. "I still want to go home."

Nico was on his feet.

"Then we tell them," he said. His voice was shaking. "Now. Tonight. We break out the canteen's broadcast set — we've got batteries, he's got batteries — we put that on every channel in Tallow. Every screen. We let everybody hear it breathing. Let them hear that and then let her stand up and tell them it's starving — "

"She will," said Ambrose. "Stand up. And tell them it's starving."

"Let her try."

"She'll stand up," Ambrose said, "and say that the waist is still narrowing, which it is, and will go on narrowing for eighteen days, which it will. And that a noise from a man who wanted to go home is not a reason to let the door close. And that the trial worked. Every one of them saw the trial work." He spread his hands. "And then, on the second of May, when the waist begins to recover, she'll say the Keeping did it. And they'll believe her, Nico. Because they'll have seen nine pilots go in on the first."

"Then nobody goes in on the first," said Nico.

"Somebody will," said Mother Salome.

She had not moved. She sat at the front with the Tally across her knees and one hand flat on its blanket, the way she always did, and she was looking not at Ambrose or at Nico but at the dark speakers on the table, as if the breath were still coming out of them.

"Somebody will," she said again. "She's offering to strike their debts. All of it. Their families'. There isn't a pilot on that station who hasn't lain awake since the fifteenth of March doing the sum. She'll find nine. She'd find ninety." She turned her head and looked at Ambrose. "You're right, harp man. And it's not enough to be right. Not here. Not with her holding the paper." She looked down at the Tally. "Who's it for, your proof? Who do you think is going to hear it, that she can't talk over?"

"Ardent," said Ambrose.

The canteen stirred.

"The Board," he said. "The Inward. The Concern's licensed through the Ardent courts; the Charter's a public instrument. There are inspectors. There are newspapers. There are people on the other side of the door who have never heard of Hesper Vane and don't owe her anything. If I put this in front of them — the proof, the prediction, the sound — they can't say I'm a frightened old man who wants to go home. They can watch it come true. From there. The second of May." He touched his breast pocket. "I sail on the twenty-eighth. On the *Providence*. I'll carry it. I'll put it in front of everyone I can find in Ardent. And when the waist turns on the second, exactly when I said it would, they'll know."

"And the nine who go in on the first?" said Teo.

He did not look away from her. "I'll broadcast it here too," he said. "On the twenty-eighth. From the *Providence*, as she goes into the throat, when she's too far for anyone to stop it. On every channel. The sound, the prediction, all of it. Three days before the Keeping. It's the best I can do, Teo. If I do it sooner she'll take the spool and the berth both, and then it never gets out at all."

Nobody spoke. In the dark at the front, Nico sat down slowly on his bench.

"Three days," said Teo.

"Three days," said Ambrose. "And then it's theirs. The pilots'. Every one of them will know, before they fly. What they do with it — " He stopped. "I can't fly it for them. I can only tell them."

Teo pushed herself off the wall. She walked down through the dark canteen between the benches, between the cutters, to the front, and stopped in front of the old man at his table, and looked at him.

Then she held out her hand.

He looked at it, startled. Then he took it. His grip was dry and thin and surprisingly strong.

"Twenty-eighth," she said.

"Twenty-eighth."

## Every Civilization

Hesper Vane sent for him the next morning, which he had known she would.

She was standing at the window when he came in, with her back to him and her hands folded behind her, looking out at the mouth. The rim of fire was tighter than it had been a week ago; the dark at the centre smaller and deeper. It looked like an eye squinting against pain. She did not turn round.

"You went to the shaft-head," she said.

"Yes."

"And played them your swell. In the canteen. In the dark." She sounded, if anything, interested. "Marshal Brandt's men listened from the yard. They tell me it was very moving. One of them asked me afterwards whether it was true." She turned. "I told him it was a recording of the Needle made by a very distinguished scientist, and that it was entirely accurate, and that it had no bearing whatever on the Keeping. He was quite satisfied."

Ambrose did not answer. He stood on her red Inward rug in his cardigan with the spool in his breast pocket and waited.

"Sit down, Ambrose."

He sat. So did she, behind the desk, with her hands folded on it, the way she always did.

"I'm not going to cancel your berth," she said. "I expect you thought I would. I thought about it. I'm not going to." She tilted her head slightly. "You'll sail on the twenty-eighth, as planned, in cabin twelve. You may take with you whatever you like. Your proof. Your sound. Your prediction. Every paper trace in the Lectern, if you can fit them in your luggage. You can play it to the Board, and to the Ardent courts, and to every newspaper in the Inward. I won't stop you."

He stared at her.

"Do you know why?" she said.

He did not.

She unfolded her hands, and reached to the side of the desk, and touched — just touched, with one fingertip — the black wooden cabinet that stood there with its lock. "Because the Board already knows," she said. "Not your swell. Your swell is new and very beautiful and I'm sure it will be written up in all the journals. But the Board knows that the Keeping has nothing to do with whether the Needle closes. I told them so. In writing. On the twentieth of March, in a memorandum, which they approved on the first of April, the day before the trial. It's in there." She tapped the cabinet once. "I'll show it to you, if you like."

"You — told them."

"Of course I told them. They're my employers. I don't lie to my employers, Ambrose, I find it inefficient." She leaned back. "I told them that the Narrowing was very probably a natural event, and very probably temporary, and that the Concession's own throat-reader could find no evidence that anything done to it would affect it either way. And then I told them what to do with it."

"Which is?"

"Govern," said Hesper Vane.

She said it quite simply, the way you would name a tool.

"The Concern is leaving," she said. "You know that. It's leaving because the Reach costs more to hold than it pays — the salaries, the ships, the Security, the doctors. But the glass still pays. The glass will always pay. The Board would like the glass to go on coming through the Needle after the Concern has gone, at a fraction of the cost, without anyone here to make it come. That was the problem I was sent to solve. How do you keep forty thousand people cutting glass and shipping it through a door to people they will never see, who own their debts, after you have taken away everyone who might make them?" She spread her hands. "You give them a reason. You give them a door that needs keeping. A door that only you know how to keep. A covenant: *give, and the door stays open; stop, and you are alone in the dark forever.* You make the giving sacred and the keeper indispensable. And then you don't need Security, Ambrose. You don't need the *Providence*. You don't need anyone at all but a reader who knows when the door will open, and someone to tell the story."

He sat very still.

"Every civilization," said Hesper Vane, "is a story about what will happen if you stop. That's all a civilization is. The story is never true. It doesn't have to be. It only has to be believed, by enough people, for long enough, and then it's the world." She looked at him almost kindly. "I'm giving the Reach a story. It's a better one than most. It asks a great deal, once every eleven years, and in return it gives them a door, and a purpose, and an order, and each other. The Board approved it in an afternoon. It will outlast the Board. It will very probably outlast the Concern. That's what I'm for."

"And the pilots," said Ambrose. His voice did not sound like his own. "The ones who fly in."

"Are volunteers. Who will be paid more than anyone in the Reach has ever been paid for anything. And whose names will be remembered." She paused. "And some of them will come back. Most, if they're good. Mr Dace was unlucky."

"He wasn't unlucky. You sent him in to make a pause look like a miracle."

"Yes," she said. "I did. And it worked. Shaft Nine is the only shaft on strike, Ambrose. The other eleven are cutting Keeping glass on triple shifts, singing. Did you know they'd started singing? Somebody made up a song about him. I don't know who." She looked at the window. "In a year they'll have forgotten it was a trial. In ten they'll have forgotten it was a choice."

He stood up. He did not decide to; he found himself standing.

"I'll tell them," he said. "Here. Not in Ardent. Here. On every channel. Before the first."

"You can try," said Hesper Vane. "And I'll stand up after you and say what I said to Marshal Brandt's man: that it's accurate and beautiful and has no bearing on the Keeping. And they'll believe me, because the waist will still be narrowing, and they'll be frightened, and frightened people don't want a breath, Ambrose, they want something to do." She did not raise her voice. She never raised her voice. "And then, on the second of May, the door will open, and they'll have done it, and nobody in the Reach will ever listen to your swell again."

She stood too, and came round the desk, and stood in front of him on the red rug, a slight straight woman a head shorter than him, and looked up at him.

"Go home," she said. "Sail on the twenty-eighth. Give your daughter her fork. Write your paper. You'll be famous, I should think. You've earned it. It's very good work." Her mouth moved slightly. "It's only that it doesn't matter."

He looked at her for a long time. Then he looked past her, at the desk, and the black cabinet beside it with its lock, where a memorandum lay that said everything she had just said, in writing, approved by the Board of the Ostrander Concern on the first of April, the day before Corrigan Dace flew into the dark.

"Thank you, Director," said Ambrose Lyle, and went out.
