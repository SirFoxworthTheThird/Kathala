## The Cells
[
  Place: #The cells
  Characters: @@Teo Kesh @@Nico Kesh
  Items: ^Keeper's bead:@Nico Kesh ^Teo's bead:@Teo Kesh
  When: 30 April 2291
]
They let her see him on the last night, because Hesper Vane had said they should.

The Concession's cells were at the bottom of Anchorage's spindle, under the docks, six of them and a guardroom, built of a grey steel that had been painted once and never again. They had been made for drunks and for men who hit overseers; they smelled of disinfectant and old fear. Nico was in the fourth. He was sitting on the bunk with his back against the wall and a chain from his ankle to a ring in the floor, and when the guard let her in he looked up and saw her flight coveralls and went white.

"No," he said.

"Hello, Nic."

"No. Teo. No. They told me. The guard told me, this morning, I said he was lying, I said she'd never — " He was on his feet, as far as the chain would let him. "For *me*? You're flying into that thing for *me*?"

"Sit down."

"I'm not — "

"Sit *down*, Nico." She said it the way their mother used to, and he sat, because he always had, and she sat down on the bunk beside him, close, with her shoulder against his, the way they had sat on the shaft-school steps when he was six and she was fourteen and their father had just been brought up from the Lower Nine under a sheet.

The guard was in the doorway. He was listening. She did not look at him.

"The debts are struck," she said. "Both of ours. She did it in front of me. I watched the numbers go." She found she could almost smile. "Forty thousand seven hundred and twelve marks, Nic. Gone. Like they were never there. And yours. You're free. You walk out of here on the morning of the second whatever happens. She gave her word. She keeps her word, that woman. That's the worst thing about her."

"I don't care about the debts." His voice cracked. "I don't — Teo, you heard him. In the canteen. The harp man. It doesn't *need* it. It's *breathing*. You heard it breathe, you were there — it's going to open on the second whether anybody goes in or not, it doesn't need you, it doesn't need anyone — "

"I know."

"Then *why* — "

"Because she needs me," said Teo quietly. "And because you're in a cell." She felt him go still against her shoulder. "I know what I heard, Nic. I'm not going in to feed anything. I want you to know that. Whatever they say afterwards. Whatever she says. I'm not going in to feed it."

He turned his head and looked at her. She looked back. She did not say anything else, because the guard was in the doorway; but she held his eyes, and after a long moment she saw something come into them, slowly — not understanding, quite; the beginning of it.

"Teo," he said, very low. "What are you going to do?"

"Turn into the wave," she said. "Like I always do."

She put her hand in the pocket of her coveralls and took out the bead the Director had given her — the Keeper's bead, cloudy glass on a black cord — and held it out to him.

"She gave me this. For the Keeping. So I'd be *part of it*." She put it in his palm and closed his fingers over it. "You keep it. I don't want it. I've got yours." She touched her throat, where his bead lay, the one he had cut from his first seam at sixteen. "I've always got yours."

He held the Director's bead in his fist and looked at it, and then at her, and his face crumpled, and she put her arms round him on the bunk with the chain on his ankle and the guard in the door and held him, the way she had held him on the shaft-school steps, until the guard cleared his throat and said it was time.

"Remember the breath," she said into his hair, very quietly, too quietly for the doorway. "Remember Ambrose. Remember what he played you. Whatever she says, afterwards. Whatever they sing. *Remember.*"

"I will," said Nico.

"Promise."

"I promise," said her brother. "Teo. I promise."

## The Blessing
[
  Place: #Dock Four
  Characters: @@Teo Kesh @@Salome Okafor @@Lulu Dace
  Items: ^Small Mercy:@Teo Kesh ^The data spool:@Teo Kesh
  When: 1 May 2291

There were nine tugs on Dock Four at four in the morning on the first of May, each with a cradle welded under its belly, each cradle loaded with two tonnes of the best glass the shafts of Tallow had ever cut, and there were nine pilots standing in front of them in a line, in their flight coveralls, with their helmets under their arms and the Director's beads at their throats.

Teo knew them all. You did, on the lanes. Haldane, who had flown the Pans run for twenty years and had six children and a seventh coming. The Moss brothers, both of them, who had signed together, because neither would let the other go alone. Ida Okonjo, whose cousin had died under a sheet at the shaft-head six days ago. Jory Ferris, nineteen, who had got her licence in January. Big Sven, who could not read. Marguerite, who had flown a tug called the *Ardent Rose* for thirty-one years and said she was tired of looking at the door from outside. And Corrigan's empty place, which nobody stood in, and then Teo.

Nine. Eight debts, and Nico.

The Concession had sent its chaplain to bless them. He stood at the end of the line in his white with the sunburst on it, a thin nervous man from the Inward whom nobody had ever seen before the trial, holding a book; and before he could open it Mother Salome came across the dock from the lift, tall and stooped, with the Tally under her arm in its blanket and Lulu Dace beside her holding her hand, and walked straight past him as if he were not there, and stopped in front of the pilots.

"I'll do this," she said. To the chaplain; not looking at him. "They're ours."

He did not argue. Nobody ever argued with Mother Salome.

She did not have a book. She did not say anything about the Needle, or hunger, or doors. She went down the line of pilots, one by one, and stopped in front of each of them, and put her hand on their face — just her hand, flat, on their cheek, the way you would touch a child with a fever — and said their name. That was all. *Haldane.* *Tomas Moss.* *Ewan Moss.* *Ida.* *Jory.* *Sven.* *Marguerite.* She came to Teo last.

"Teodora," she said.

Her hand was dry and warm and smelled of salt. Teo closed her eyes.

"I know what you're going to do," said Mother Salome, very low, so that only Teo could hear. "Your brother told me. Not in words. He doesn't know it in words. But I know your face. I've known it since you were born." Her hand did not move. "Turn into the wave, girl."

Teo opened her eyes.

Lulu was standing beside Salome, holding her other hand, looking up at Teo with her father's eyes. She had her bag of bolts over her shoulder. She took one out — a bright steel bolt, a cradle bolt, the kind they had used to weld the Brannock's frame — and held it up.

"For luck," she said.

Teo took it. She put it in the breast pocket of her coveralls, over her heart, and buttoned the pocket shut.

They went to their tugs. Teo climbed the Small Mercy's strut into the cockpit, which smelled of coolant and oranges and forty years of other pilots, and of her, and sat down in the pilot's seat and put her helmet on the console and her hand, for a moment, flat on the casing of the flight recorder under the console, where the spool was. Sealed in. Waiting. The flight recorder was the one part of a tug that was built to survive anything; it had its own power, its own transmitter, its own hull. If the Mercy came apart, it would not.

She had been through it all a hundred times, lying awake in her bunk-cube for three nights. It was very simple. The nine would go in at the hour the readers called. They would fly the throat to the waist. At the waist, the eight would pull their white levers and release their glass and turn round and come home, if they could. She would not pull hers. She would not add her two tonnes to Corrigan's scar or to the eight others' on top of it, ringing at the waist forever. She would go on. Through the iris. Past the waist. Down the far throat, through the Narrowing, while it was at its narrowest, nine hours to Ardent in an ordinary week and nobody knew how many in this one; and if she came out at the other end, at Port Ardent, she would open the flight recorder's transmitter on every frequency the Inward had and play them the breath, and the sum, and nine pages in numbered paragraphs ending *Approved by the Board. Proceed.* To everyone. The courts and the newspapers and the inspectors and every ordinary person in Ardent with a receiver in their kitchen. And then let the Board explain.

And if she did not come out —

Then the flight recorder would keep it. Wherever she ended up. For whoever came.

Nobody had ever flown the throat in a Narrowing. Nobody had ever been past the waist and come back to say what was there. Corrigan had gone to the waist and said there was a light in the middle with no name.

She put her hand on Nico's bead at her throat. It was not leaning to the left any more. It was standing out from her skin on its cord, straight out, taut, toward the mouth, like a dog that has seen its master.

"Mercy, Lectern," said a voice in her ear. Not Ambrose. Bettany Shore, very young, very frightened, trying to sound like him. "Keepers, Lectern. Stand by. The Director will speak."

## The Keeping

Hesper Vane spoke for ninety seconds. Teo did not hear much of it. She heard *door* and *covenant* and *the first Keeping* and *you will be remembered*. She heard the Director say that at the moment the Keepers entered the throat, an instrument would be struck in the Lectern — *the instrument by which the Narrowing was first heard* — so that all the Reach would know the hour. She thought of a brass fork turning in a slight woman's fingers on a blood-red rug. Then the Director said *Go*, and Bettany Shore's frightened voice said *Keepers, Lectern, you are clear for the mouth, the waist is at twenty-one point four and steady, God help you*, and Teo fed the Small Mercy's mains and took her off the dock.

They went in a line. Haldane first, because he had flown the longest; then the Moss brothers, side by side; then the others; and the Mercy last, because she had asked to be last. Out past the beacons, which were all amber now, every one; out past the outer markers; into the shear.

She did not remember the shear afterward, much. It was the worst it had ever been and she did not remember it. She remembered Bettany's voice, calling the waves, not as well as Ambrose had, late every time, so that the pilots had to read them themselves; and she remembered reading them, turning into them before Bettany finished speaking, the way she had at the inner markers with an old man in a cardigan behind her saying *Good. That was very good.* She remembered the Mercy screaming. She remembered the inner markers going past on either side, the two dead beacons, the place where she had held for three hours; and then she was past them, for the first time in her life, and there was nothing in front of her but the mouth.

Somewhere far behind her, two million kilometres behind, in the Lectern, somebody struck a fork.

She heard it. She would swear afterward that she heard it — that it came to her through the channel, thin and pure, laid across the roar of the shear like a ruler. Ambrose's note. Clare's. And the bead at her throat, which had been straining toward the mouth on its cord, gave a single sharp *tick* against her skin.

Then the throat took her.

It was folded light. Corrigan had been right. Light folded on itself like the inside of a shell, grey-gold and white and a colour that was neither, going down and down and in, and the Mercy going down into it, and the light going past on every side so close she could have put out her hand. There was no shear in here. There was no sound but the Mercy's engines and her own breath. The other tugs were ahead of her in a line, small, stretched, pulled long, like beads on a string, and the light went past them too.

She reached down under the console with one hand, without looking, and found the flight recorder's switch, and turned it to *record*.

"This is Teodora Kesh," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her in the helmet, very close. "Pilot. Small Mercy. Out of Dock Four, Anchorage. First of May, twenty-two ninety-one. I'm in the throat. I'm not a Keeper. I want that on the record before anything else. My name's on a list of Keepers and I'm not one. I've got two tonnes of glass in my cradle and I'm not going to release it." She took a breath. "There's a spool in this recorder. It's not mine. It's Ambrose Lyle's. He was the Senior Throat-Reader at the Lectern for twenty-three years and on the twenty-fourth of April he was murdered for what's on it — "

The waist was coming.

She could see it ahead. The folded light drawing in, and in, all of it, the whole throat, toward a single point far ahead of her where it tightened like an iris, like an eye squinting against pain; and at the centre of the iris, very small, the light. Corrigan's light. The one with no name. It was not white. It was every colour and none. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen.

And around the light, at the iris's edge, there was something else. Small and hard and bright, clinging to the folded wall like frost to a pane. She could hear it. Even in the helmet, over the engines. A thin high ringing.

*Something stayed.*

Ahead of her Haldane's tug reached the waist and slowed and hung there at the edge of the iris, small against the light. She saw his cradle open. She saw the glass go — a dull blue spill, tumbling out of the cradle into the light — and the light flare, white, then sickly blue, then dark; and the ringing at the iris's edge grow louder, very slightly, a new note joining the old. Then the next tug. Then the Moss brothers together. One after another, at the waist, the cradles opening, the glass spilling, the flare, the ringing, louder each time, and the tugs turning, slowly, laboriously, in the narrow folded light, and starting back up the throat toward her, past her, going home.

Marguerite went by her on the way out so close that Teo could see her face through two canopies. She was crying and laughing at the same time. She raised one hand.

Teo raised hers back.

Then there was nobody ahead of her but the iris, and the light.

"I'm at the waist," she said into the recorder. Her voice was quite steady; she noticed that. "The others have released and turned. I'm not releasing. I'm going on. Through the waist. To the Inward. If I come out at Ardent I'll broadcast this on every channel they've got. If I don't — " She stopped. "If I don't, whoever finds this, listen to it. All of it. The breath and the sum and the paper. And tell them. Tell everyone. Tell them it doesn't need us. Tell them his name. Ambrose Lyle. Tell them Corrigan Dace's name, and say he was a pilot, not a Keeper. Tell them Nico Kesh didn't kill anybody." She swallowed. "And tell Nico — "

She did not finish. She did not know what to tell Nico. She found that she was smiling.

"Turning into the wave," she said, and fed the mains everything they had, and a little more, and the Small Mercy went forward into the iris.

The light was very close now. It filled the canopy. It filled the cockpit. It came in through the delta crack in the forward canopy where her mother had once taken a rock, forty years ago, and lay across Teo's hands on the yoke, every colour and none. The ringing was all round her, and under it something deeper, slower, vast and patient, rising and falling, like someone sleeping in the next room.

The bead at her throat stood out on its cord, straight out, toward the light.

"Oh," said Teo Kesh, to the recorder, very softly, the way Ambrose had said it at the inner markers with the headphones on. "Oh. It's beautiful. I didn't know."

And the iris closed around her, and the light went in.

## The Hunger Tone
[

  Characters: @@Bettany Shore @@Hesper Vane @@Okoro Brandt

  Facts: ?Hesper Vane was the First Warden:reader ?The Founder's Fork was Ambrose's:reader ?Hesper Vane was the First Warden:@Bettany Shore ?The Founder's Fork was Ambrose's:@Bettany Shore

The waist turned at fourteen minutes past six in the morning on the second of May, at twenty-one point one per cent of nominal, eleven minutes later than Ambrose Lyle had said it would on a sheet of paper sixteen days before.

Bettany Shore watched it turn. She had been at the harp for twenty-six hours. She had called the line for nine tugs into the throat and eight out of it, the last of them limping back up through the shear at eleven at night with Marguerite at the controls weeping on the open channel. She had called the Long Night after that — the throat shut, the waist at twenty-one, the harp singing its thin high strained chord into the dark of the dome hour after hour with no change at all — and she had not slept, and she had not left the console, and at a little after five in the morning she had put on Ambrose's old cracked headphones, which were still on the hook beside his chair, and listened to the buoy.

So she heard it before the trace showed it. The long slow breath, under the throat. Turning. And then the short breath turning on top of it, at fourteen past six, and the trace on the console coming up off the bottom, very slightly, twenty-one point two, twenty-one point four — and she sat in his chair with his headphones on and cried, because it was exactly as he had said, and he was not there to see it.

The Director came down to the Lectern at seven.

Bettany had not heard her come in. She looked up and Hesper Vane was standing beside the console, looking at the trace — twenty-three now, and rising — with her hands folded in front of her. She looked as she always looked. She did not look as though she had been up all night, though Bettany knew she had; she had been on the gallery above the dome, at the window, from the moment the Keepers went in.

"The *Small Mercy*," said the Director.

"She didn't come out." Bettany's voice was hoarse. "The other eight released and turned. Marguerite said — Marguerite said on the channel that the Mercy didn't release. That she went *on*. Past the waist. Into the — " She stopped. "Into the light, Marguerite said. And then the throat shut."

Hesper Vane was quiet for a moment.

"Did the *Mercy* transmit?" she said. "On the way in. At the waist. Anything at all."

"Nothing reached the Lectern after the mouth. Nothing ever does, in the throat. Only the buoy, and the buoy only hears — " Bettany stopped again. "No. Nothing."

"Good," said Hesper Vane, very quietly.

She stood looking at the rising trace. Then she went to the harp.

She walked round it once, slowly, the way she had walked across rooms in all the years Bettany had worked under her, as if she were looking at a painting. The frame, gone faintly gold in the dome's light. The nine courses of wire. Ambrose's console, with his chair pushed back and his headphones on the hook. She stopped in front of the frame and reached into the pocket of her jacket and took out something small and brass, and held it in her palm, looking at it. Bettany knew what it was. Everyone in the Lectern knew what it was. It had been struck at four in the morning on the first of May, from the gallery above the dome, by the Director's own hand, at the moment the Keepers went into the mouth — once, against the gallery rail, so that the note rang down through the dome into the harp and out over every channel in the Reach.

"This was Mr Lyle's," said Hesper Vane.

"I know."

"Everything here was tuned to it. He told me so, once. He said if he lost it, nothing would agree with anything." She turned it over. "Strike it for me, Miss Shore."

Bettany looked at her.

"Strike it. Against your hand. Here, in front of the harp."

Bettany took it. Her hands were shaking. She struck it against the heel of her palm, the way she had seen Ambrose do a thousand times, and the note went up into the dome, thin and pure, and met the harp's chord and lay down across it like a ruler; and under the chord, faint, so faint that you would not hear it unless you knew it was there, the long slow breath of the Needle, turning, coming back.

"You can hear it," said Hesper Vane. "Can't you. When it's tuned to that."

"Yes," whispered Bettany.

"Everyone could, with practice. That's what he found. Anyone at all, with ears." The Director held out her hand, and Bettany gave her the fork back, and she closed her fingers over it. "Henceforth, Miss Shore, the harp will not be tuned to this. It will be tuned to a note I will give you, this morning, and teach you, and you will teach to every reader who comes after you. A slightly different note. You will tune to it every morning and not otherwise, and the harp will hear the waist close and the waist open, as well as it ever did, and no one will ever hear that again." She nodded at the faint breath under the chord. "I will call it the Hunger Tone. I think that's a good name for it. Don't you?"

Bettany could not speak.

"And the old records," the Director went on, in the same level voice. "Every trace in the archive before today. Mr Lyle's paper rolls. His notes. The Concession's harp logs back to forty-seven. You'll box them, today, and Marshal Brandt's men will bring them up to the Gallery, and they will be in my keeping. And you'll begin a new daybook, this morning, with today's date at the top." She looked at the console. "Your readings from today onward are the first readings of the new order. The Concession ends today, Miss Shore. The *Providence* has sailed. There's nobody left in the Reach who works for the Ostrander Concern but me, and as of this morning I don't either." She paused. "I'm told the shafts are already calling this the First Keeping. The Keepers, the First Keepers. They'll want someone to keep it. Every four thousand days. Someone who knows how the door is kept. Someone who can read it." The grey eyes rested on Bettany for a moment. "The Wardenship of the Throat, I thought. A Warden keeps a door. I'll be the first."

She turned the fork over in her fingers once more, and Bettany saw the engraving on its stem catch the dome's grey light, the tidy capitals, worn a little bright at the edges where his thumb had gone for twenty-three years: A. L. — 440 — FOR CLARE.

"We'll keep this," said the First Warden. "In the Lectern. On a cushion, I think. The instrument by which the hunger was first heard." She closed her hand over it. "We'll strike it at every Keeping, once, at the hour, so the Reach knows. And otherwise nobody will touch it." Her mouth moved, very slightly. "For clarity."

She went out. Bettany heard her go up the spindle, unhurried, toward the Gallery, and the door slide shut behind her.

Bettany sat at the console for a long time. The trace climbed. Twenty-four. Twenty-five. Twenty-six. The door was opening, all by itself, exactly as he had said, the long breath carrying the short; and in the Lectern's own log, on the console in front of her, there was nothing but the number. *Waist twenty-six. Holding.* After a while she opened a new daybook, black-bound, empty, and took up a pen, and at the head of the first page in her small upright hand she wrote the date; and then she sat and looked at the white page for a long time; and then, because she had been told to, and because she was twenty-two and frightened and alone in the dome with a dead man's headphones round her neck, she wrote the instruction down.

## Pilot

They let Nico out of the cells at eight in the morning on the second of May, as the Director had promised, and he walked out of the spindle into the docks and down to the Pans shuttle and out onto the salt flats of Hollin under a sky where, for the first time in two months, the Needle's rim was loosening.

You could see it from the Pans. You could see it if you knew where to look, and everyone on the Pans that morning knew: a faint smear of wrong stars low over the salt, a bruise with a rim of grey-gold fire round it. The fire was less tight than yesterday. Everyone said so. People were standing on the salt in their hundreds in the cold, in the thin bitter wind off the flats, looking up at it, pointing, crying. Somebody had a horn and was blowing it. Somebody was singing — a tune Nico did not know, low and droning, with words about little ships and glass. People had their hands on each other's shoulders. People were embracing strangers.

*It worked,* they were saying, all round him, all over the Pans. *It worked. They went in, and it opened. The Keeping worked.*

He walked through them with his hands in his pockets and his head down.

Mother Salome was at the shaft-head, on the cage-head step where the three sheets had been. The fence was still down. Nobody had cut glass for a week. She had the Tally on her knees, unwrapped, and Lulu Dace sitting beside her, and when Nico came up the access road she looked up and did not say anything, and moved over on the step to make room.

He sat down.

"Marguerite told me," said Salome after a while. "She came straight from the dock. She said the Mercy went on. Past the waist. Into the light. She didn't release." She looked down at the steel on her knees. "She said she was smiling."

Nico did not answer. He was holding something in his fist, in his pocket. He took it out. The Director's bead, the Keeper's bead, cloudy glass on a black cord. He looked at it for a long time.

"They're singing," he said. "On the Pans. They're singing about it. *They loaded glass in little ships.*" He laughed, a terrible sound. "*It worked.* That's what they're saying. All of them. It opened, so it worked. She'll be on the screens by noon telling them so. And they'll believe her. Because it opened." He closed his fist on the bead. "And nobody will ever know. That she didn't release. That she was trying to — "

"You know," said Mother Salome.

He looked at her.

"You know," she said. "I know. Marguerite knows. The child knows." She put her hand on Lulu's head. "That's four. It's not nothing. It's how everything starts, Nico. Somebody knowing, and saying it to somebody else." She held out the stylus. "Go on."

He took it. She tilted the Tally on her knees toward him, and he looked down at the old steel: the thirty-one at the top in her careful capitals, and the rows below in their dozen hands, and at the bottom the newest, in a nine-year-old's wavering letters, C. DACE — PILOT, and under it, in Teo's own hand, scratched a week ago on this step, A. LYLE — HARP.

He scratched it under those. His hand was steady. T. KESH.

He stopped there, with the stylus on the steel.

"Keeper," said Mother Salome softly. "That's what they'll call her. That's how they'll have it. Her name will be on a list of Keepers in the Director's book, and they'll sing it, and it'll be true for them."

"She wasn't one."

"I know."

"She said so. To me. In the cell. *I'm not going in to feed it.* She said to remember." He looked at the bead in his other fist, and then at the plate. "I promised."

He put the stylus back to the steel and scratched, under her name, carefully, the same word Lulu had scratched for Corrigan Dace: PILOT.

Then he sat on the cage-head step between the old woman and the child with the Tally across all three of their knees, in the bitter wind, with the shaft silent behind them and the Pans singing below, and looked up at the Needle opening over the salt, all by itself, exactly as an old man in a cardigan had said it would; and he held his sister's name under his fingers, the letters of it, cold and shallow and new, and he said it, once, aloud, so that somebody else would have heard it.

"Teo."
