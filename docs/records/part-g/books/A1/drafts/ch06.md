## Wire

The Anchor looked, from the outside, like something that had died with its eyes open.

Isra had seen it before, from the Ossuary — a far-off spindle with two wheels, dark against the bruise of the mouth — but she had never been close, and coming in on it in the Gristle she found that she did not like it. It was too big. It had been built for thousands of people and was lived in by a dozen, and you could see which parts they lived in, because those parts had lights: a cluster of windows at the near end of the spindle, a band of them round one wheel. The other wheel did not turn. It hung there motionless, its windows black, its spokes furred with frost, and somewhere on its rim a loose panel was flapping in a wind that did not exist, opening and closing, opening and closing, catching the grey light of the mouth each time like a slow wink.

"Gristle, Anchor." An old man's voice on the radio, dry, unhurried. "State your business."

"Bone-picker. I've got wire. Resonator wire, good, a full spool. I was told the Wardens buy it."

A pause. "Brother Lucan buys it. Dock Four. Mind the second ring of the dock, it's loose."

The dock was huge and empty and cold and the second ring was very loose. Isra tied the Gristle up with her own lines rather than trust the dock clamps, and climbed out with the spool of wire over her shoulder in its sack, and stood in the middle of the great dark hangar under the one working light, and waited, and felt the place around her: miles of corridor, hundreds of rooms, all of it silent, all of it cold. She had stripped Returns with dead men in them and felt less watched.

The man who came for her was younger than she had expected. A Warden — the grey robe, the silver at the cuffs gone dull and the cuffs themselves frayed — but not much older than her, thin and quick, with a long face and ink on his fingers and a pair of reader's spectacles shoved up into untidy dark hair. He came across the hangar at a sort of half-run and stopped in front of her a little out of breath.

"You've got wire," he said.

"I've got wire."

"Is it — sorry. Brother Lucan." He held out an inky hand and then seemed to think better of it and put it back in his sleeve. "Is it really resonator wire? Not cable? People bring cable. They think because it's thin — "

Isra swung the sack down and opened it. The spool inside was a hand across, wound tight with wire so fine it looked like a strand of light, silver, unoxidised, because she had found it sealed in a cannister in the bowels of an old Compact ore-hauler that had been drifting at the edge of the Ossuary for longer than anybody remembered. She had known what it was because Hanne had told her what to look for, years ago. *If you ever find wire that rings when you pluck it, girl, don't sell it to the Pans. Take it to the Wardens. They'll give you anything.*

Brother Lucan knelt down on the hangar floor and took a length of the wire between his fingertips, very carefully, and plucked it. It rang. In the cold empty hangar the note went up into the dark like a bird. He shut his eyes.

"Where did you get this?"

"Return. Old one. Before the Quiet."

"It's Compact. It's real Compact resonator stock. We haven't had any in — " He opened his eyes and looked up at her. "What do you want for it?"

She had planned to ask for a lot. She found she did not quite know how much was a lot to a man in a frayed robe in a station that was mostly dark. "What've you got?"

"Not much," he said honestly. "Food. We have food. We have a greenhouse on the near wheel that does better than anything in Tallow, and the Prior lets me trade from it. And medicine, some. And — " He hesitated. "I don't suppose you want to see the harp? People sometimes do. Before they decide."

She had not meant to. She went.

He led her up through the spindle, through long dim passages where his footsteps echoed and the air smelled of old metal and incense and cold, past doors that opened on dark rooms full of nothing, until they came to a vaulted gallery lined with empty racks, and an iron door at its end, and through it into the harp hall.

Isra stopped in the doorway.

The mouth filled the dome. She had seen it every working day of her life, from the Ossuary, from the Pans; she had never seen it like this, so close, so huge, leaning over the glass like something looking in. And under it, in the middle of the worn floor, the harp. A frame of something like gold glass, taller than Pike, strung with long lines of wire that held the grey light of the mouth and glowed with it faintly; and it was singing. A low, wide, many-stranded sound that she felt more than heard, in her chest and her teeth and the soles of her feet. It was the Hunger Tone — she knew it from Hanne's cittern, from the Lantern House speakers — only it was not quite. It was the Hunger Tone the way a person's voice in another room is their voice.

"It reads the throat," Lucan said quietly beside her. "The throat makes a sort of — noise, all the time. Gravitational noise. The wires pick it up and sing it. A reader listens and works out the shape of it. Where the waist is. How narrow." He went to the harp and laid his palm flat against the frame, the way you would touch a horse. "This frame is the First Warden's. It's the oldest made thing in the Reach. The wires are whatever we can get." He grimaced. "Which hasn't been much, lately. Three of the low courses are spliced. Two of them are just — wrong. I tune them every morning and by the evening they've drifted. Your wire would — " He stopped, and looked at the spool on her shoulder, with an expression on his face that she recognised from the Pans buyers when they saw good Return glass. Hunger. "It would make a difference."

"Show me," she said.

He restrung one of the spliced courses while she watched. It took an hour. He did it on his knees on the cold floor with his spectacles on and a little pair of pliers, talking the whole time, half to her and half to himself: how the wire had to be tensioned, how it had to be seated, how you could hear a bad seating as a kind of buzz in the chord. When he had finished he stood back and tuned it. He did that by ear. He stood with his eyes shut and his head on one side and *sang* — a low long note, the drone she knew — and turned the peg at the top of the frame a hair at a time until the new wire stopped fighting the note and lay down inside it.

"The Hunger Tone," he said, when he had finished. "That's what we tune to. Every reader learns it at investiture. The Prior sings it, and you sing it back, until you can't not."

"What about that?" Isra nodded toward a little alcove off the hall, behind an iron grille, where a lamp burned in front of something small and brass on a grey cushion. She had seen it when she came in.

"That's the Fork. The First Warden's Fork." He said it with a kind of embarrassed reverence, as if he were introducing her to his grandmother. "It's — well. It's the holiest thing we've got. It's said to be what the First Warden heard the hunger with. It's struck once at every Keeping, here, when the Keepers go in. You can see the motto on it, if you get close. *For Clarity.*" He made a face. "Mostly worn off. It says FOR CL."

"Don't you tune with it?"

"Oh, no. No. It's a relic."

"It's a tuning fork."

"It's a *relic* tuning fork." But he was smiling a little. "You sound like me when I was a novice. The Prior used to send me to the chapel for a day for saying things like that."

She did not smile back. She was thinking of something else. Without meaning to, she had put her hand flat against her chest, over the shard of banded glass that she had worn inside her shirt for twelve days now, on a cord, against her skin. She had been holding it there since he started tuning. It had not stirred. Not once. Not when he plucked the new wire, not when the whole harp took the note and sang it back.

"Can I tell you something stupid?" she said.

He looked surprised. "Of course."

She took the shard out and showed him, and told him. The Return, the layered root, the bands like a cliff. Hanne's cittern. The low string, and the glass humming back.

Brother Lucan held the shard in the light of the reliquary lamp for a long time, turning it, his spectacles down on his nose.

"It hummed," he said. "At your grandmother's cittern."

"Every time she plucked the low string."

He went to the harp and held the shard against the frame, close to the new wire, and plucked it. The note sang out. The shard lay in his hand, dull, silent.

He plucked it again. Nothing.

"Maybe it's broken," Isra said. "Maybe whatever it did, it's stopped."

"Maybe." He did not sound as if he thought so. He gave it back to her, and his eyes went for a second, she noticed, to the reliquary, to the little brass fork on its cushion; and then away again, quickly, as if he had caught himself looking at something he should not. "What's your grandmother's cittern tuned to?"

"I don't know. Whatever she tunes it to. It was her gran's. She tunes it by ear."

"Huh," said Lucan.

He was quiet for a moment. Then, in a different voice, brisk, as if changing the subject on purpose: "Would you like to see the Bell? Before you go? It's the other old thing we've got. Most people don't know it's there."

He took her to a telescope at the side of the dome, an old heavy brass thing on a pivot, and fussed with it, and stepped back. She put her eye to it.

At first there was only the mouth, filling everything, grey-gold at the rim. Then, very small, at the edge, she saw it: a little grey cylinder hanging in the glow, motionless, nose toward the dark, with something at its tail end that might once have been a frame of wires, open like the ribs of an umbrella, now bent and furred with frost.

"It's a buoy," said Lucan. "A listening buoy, we think, like a little harp. It's been there longer than the order's records go back. Longer than the Quiet, certainly. The mouth's pull holds it. It hasn't moved in three hundred years that we know of. It doesn't do anything now. It's dead." He paused. "The old Wardens used to say the First Warden put it there. To listen to the Needle from close by. So they called it the Bell."

Isra looked at it for a long time through the cold brass eyepiece: a small dead thing, holding its place at the edge of the dark, listening to nothing.

"I'll take food," she said, straightening. "And medicine. For a man with glass-lung. Whatever you've got. And you can have the spool."

## The Lamplighter at the Anchor
[
  Place: #The Lectern

The Lamplighter came to the Anchor two days later, and Lucan watched the Prior go to meet her the way you watch an old dog go out to meet a bigger one.

She came with forty people. Her own ship — a converted ore-hauler painted black with the yellow lamp on its bow, bigger than anything else flying in the Reach — and two cutters, and the Deacon, and a crowd of Lantern faithful in black with lamps stitched on their shoulders who filled the hangar and the passages and stood in the harp hall in a silent double rank along the walls, watching the harp sing. Lucan had been given no warning. He was on his knees at the base of the frame with the last of the new wire when the doors opened and they came in, and he had not had time to get up.

Prior Saar came in front of her. He was eighty-one and walked with two sticks and had been Prior of the Anchor for thirty years, and in all that time Lucan had never seen him bow to anyone. He did not bow now. But he stood aside, which was worse.

"The harp hall," said Prior Saar. "As you asked."

Mercy Oduya came past him into the middle of the hall and stopped and looked up.

She was not what Lucan had expected. He had heard her voice for years, on the speakers, in Tallow, on every channel; he had imagined somebody tall, iron, a prophet from a painting. She was small. Smaller than him. A broad, plain, dark-faced woman of fifty in a black coat, her hair grey at the temples, her hands bare and cracked like a cook's. She stood under the dome with the mouth leaning over her and the harp singing in front of her, and she looked up at the Needle, and Lucan saw her face change, and saw — he could not have said how — that she had never been here before. That she had been preaching the Needle for twenty-five years and had never stood this close to it.

"Oh," she said, very softly.

Nobody moved. The harp sang.

She went to it. She walked up to the frame, right up, as close as Lucan was, and he scrambled back on his knees out of her way, and she did not seem to see him. She put out one cracked brown hand and laid it on the old gold glass, flat, the way he did himself. She stood there with her palm on the harp and her eyes closed and the drone going through her, and her lips moved, and Lucan, who was nearest, heard what they said. It was a name. *Tobin.* She said it three times.

Then she opened her eyes and took her hand away and turned, and she was the Lamplighter again.

"Prior," she said. "Thank you. I wanted to stand here. I wanted to hear it." Her voice was the voice from the speakers, warm and low; in the hall it was very quiet and carried to every wall. "Now I'd like to hear from your readers."

"Brother Lucan is our reader," said the Prior.

She looked down at him, on his knees, with wire in his hands. "Brother."

He got up. He was aware of his frayed cuffs, of the ink on his fingers, of the double rank of black coats along the walls. Of the Deacon, standing just inside the door: a thin, neat, pleasant-faced man with his hands clasped behind his back, watching Lucan with polite interest, as if he were an item on a list.

"What does it say?" said the Lamplighter, and nodded at the harp.

"It — " Lucan cleared his throat. "The waist is at nineteen per cent of nominal. It's been below twenty-five since the Fifty-Ninth. It hasn't recovered between Narrowings, the way it used to. Each one leaves it a little narrower." He hesitated. "The readings are noisy. I've only just restrung it. Some of the courses were — I'm not sure how much to trust what it was telling us before."

"At the Sixtieth. What will it do?"

"I don't know."

"Brother." She came a step closer. She was looking up at him; she had to. Her eyes were very dark and very steady. "I didn't ask what you know. I asked what it will do. You listen to it every day. You've listened to it longer than anybody living. What will it do?"

He looked at the harp. He thought of the long nights, the headphones, the drone. He thought of the waist line on the old screen, creeping down, creeping down, year by year, a little lower after every Narrowing, like a tide going out that never came back in.

"I think it will close," he said. "I think — I think it's dying."

"Of what?"

"I don't know."

"I do," said Mercy Oduya.

She did not raise her voice. She said it quite gently, as if she were sorry to have to. "It's hungry, Brother. It's been hungry for a hundred years. We feed it less and less, and it gives us less and less, and now it's giving back what we gave it because it hasn't the strength to keep it. You've seen the Returns. I've seen them. My son is in there." She did not look away from him. "He was seventeen. He went in at the Fifty-Ninth with a cradle of glass and a bead at his throat, and I sang him off from the Lantern House steps, and I have not slept a night through since. And I have asked the Needle every night for eleven years whether it was enough. Whether he was enough." Her voice did not break. "It wasn't. Nine skiffs at the Fifty-Eighth. Seven at the Fifty-Ninth. It wasn't enough. So we will give it enough."

Lucan found he had nothing to say. The black coats along the walls were silent. Somewhere in the double rank someone was weeping, very quietly.

"I'll need your readings," the Lamplighter said, turning to the Prior. "Every day from now until the Sixtieth. Where the waist is. When it narrows. When the Long Night begins, to the hour. I'll need a reader on my ship when we go in, to give the line. And I'll need the Wardens on the steps of the Lantern House at the Great Keeping, in your grey, with your Fork, to bless the Keepers as they go." She paused. "As the Wardens always used to."

Prior Saar leaned on his two sticks for a long moment.

"The Wardens will bless the Keeping," he said. "As they always have."

"Thank you, Prior." The Lamplighter inclined her head, very slightly, which was more than he had done. Then she looked once more at the harp, and at the mouth above it, and at Lucan, standing beside it with the wire in his hands.

"Brother," she said. "Thank you for telling me the truth. Most people don't. I could hear that you were." And she went out, with her black coats behind her, and the Deacon last, who smiled at Lucan pleasantly on his way through the door, and the door closed.

The harp sang. Prior Saar stood in the middle of the hall on his two sticks, very old, looking at nothing.

"Prior," Lucan said.

"Not now, Brother."

"She's going to put a hundred people in the throat. A hundred, Prior, and every gram of glass in the Reach, and I *don't know* — "

"*Not now.*" The old man's voice cracked like a whip, and Lucan stopped. Saar closed his eyes. When he opened them again he looked only tired. "There are twelve of us, Lucan. Twelve. In a station built for six thousand. We eat because the Lantern lets us trade in Tallow. We have air because the Lantern lets the Pans sell us scrubbers. When she asks us to stand on her steps in our grey, we will stand on her steps in our grey. That is what it is to be twelve." He turned toward the door on his sticks. "Finish your wire. Read your throat. Give her the numbers. And for the love of whatever you still believe in, Brother, stop telling people you don't know."

## Levy

  Place: #The Gutter
  Characters: @@Isra Venn @@Ostap Pike @@Hanne Venn @@Deacon Rusk
  Items: ^The glass shard:@Isra Venn

The Lantern came into the Gutter at the change of the shift, when everybody was home, which was how Isra knew they had planned it.

They came down Rope Street in a long black line with lamps, real lamps, oil lamps on poles, swinging, so that the light ran ahead of them along the salt walls in long wavering yellow tongues and the shadows of the doorways jumped. Two by two. Knocking. Every door. Isra heard them coming from the far end of the tunnel and had time to do one thing, and the thing she did was take the shard out of her shirt and put it in her mouth.

It was cold. It tasted of salt and metal and the deep cold of the mouth. She tucked it into her cheek and sat down on the end of Hanne's bed and folded her hands.

"Girl," said Hanne, watching her.

"Shh."

They knocked on the ship-door. Pike, who had been sitting on the floor by the heater with his back to the wall, half-asleep, looked at Isra, and got up slowly and opened it.

There were four of them, young, in black, with the lamp on the shoulder. Behind them, in the tunnel, Deacon Rusk, holding no lamp, his hands clasped behind his back.

"Good evening," said the Deacon. "Old Hanne. Isra. And — Pike, isn't it? Ostap Pike. I didn't know you'd come down to the Gutter." He smiled. "I'm sorry to call so late. It's the levy. You'll have heard."

"We heard," said Pike.

"Then you'll know what we're looking for. Glass. Any glass at all. Cut, raw, dust, Return, beads, bowls, buttons, it doesn't matter. All glass is tithe." He nodded to the four young ones, and they came in.

It did not take them long. Hanne's room was full of things, but there was not much in it worth anything, and almost nothing of glass. They found a jar of glass-dust that Hanne kept for the cittern strings, to rub on them, she said, it made them speak. They found three old beads on a thread in the bottom of a tin of buttons. They found Pike's inhaler, which had a little cartridge of fine glass powder in it that the Pans doctor said eased the lungs, and the boy who found it looked at it and then at Pike, and Pike looked back at him and said nothing, and the boy put it in his bag.

"That's his medicine," said Isra. It came out muffled, round the shard. She did not care.

The boy looked at the Deacon. The Deacon looked at Isra, pleasantly, for a long moment.

"All glass is tithe," he said. "I'm sorry. I'm sure the Pans doctor can find him something else."

"There isn't anything else."

"Then he'll have to manage," said Deacon Rusk. "As we all will. We're all giving something, Isra Venn. The Lamplighter's giving her son. Some of us are giving our lives. A man's cough is not very much to ask." He tilted his head. "Is there anything in your mouth?"

The room went still.

"No," said Isra.

"Open it."

She did not. Pike moved — a small movement, just a shift of his weight, toward the Deacon — and two of the young ones moved too, toward Pike, with their hands going to their belts, and for a second it was all there in the little warm room, ready to happen.

"Oh, for the love of the door," said Hanne loudly.

Everyone looked at her. She was sitting up against the wall with her cittern across her knees, small and brown and folded, and she was glaring at the Deacon with her bird's eyes as if he were a child who had trodden mud on her floor.

"I knew your mother," she said. "Hettie Rusk. She sang flat. She sang flat at the Fifty-Seventh and she sang flat at her wedding and she sang flat at your naming, I was there, I heard her. And she'd be ashamed of you, standing in an old woman's room in the middle of the night going through her button tin. There's nothing in the girl's mouth but her tongue. It's the only thing in this family that's ever been worth anything, and you can't have it." She plucked the low string of the cittern, hard, a single angry note. "Get out."

The Deacon looked at her for a long moment. Then, to Isra's astonishment, he laughed — a real laugh, light and surprised.

"My mother did sing flat," he said. "Good night, Old Hanne." And he nodded to the four, and they went, with the jar and the beads and Pike's inhaler in their bags, and the lamps went away down Rope Street swinging, and the next door along began to be knocked on.

Isra spat the shard into her palm. She was shaking.

Under the low string's dying note, in her cold wet hand, she felt it hum.

Pike sat down on the floor by the heater, slowly, the way he did everything now, and put his face in his hands. When he coughed, it went on for a long time. She sat down beside him and held the rag for him, and when it was over he did not take his hands from his face.

"I could have had him," she said. "Rusk. I could have — you shouldn't have stopped me."

"I didn't stop you."

"You were going to."

"I was going to stop *them*," Pike said through his hands. "Stop them stopping you. There's a difference." He lowered his hands. His eyes were red. "Isra. Listen. Don't fight the Lantern. You don't know what they are."

"I know what they are. They're Rusk and a lot of boys with lamps."

"I was one of the boys with lamps." He said it flatly. "Hanne told you. I know she did. I flew escort for the Lamplighter at the Fifty-Ninth. I was on the *Patience* with Rusk." He looked at the heater, at its little orange glow. "We took the Keepers out to the mouth. Seven skiffs. Kids, mostly. Volunteers. They'd been in the Lantern House for weeks, singing, they'd been — " He moved his hand. "They were happy. You've never seen anything like it. They were so happy. And we took them out to the mouth at the start of the Long Night and they went in one after another, in a line, like birds. And one of them turned round."

Hanne had gone very still, on the bed. Isra did not move.

"Just turned round," said Pike. "Right at the edge. Pulled up and turned and came back out toward us. We didn't know if something was wrong with the skiff. Or with him. And Rusk got on the channel and told him to turn back to the mouth, and he didn't, he just kept coming, and Rusk told him again, and he said something. On the open channel. We all heard it." His voice was very quiet. "He said, *I don't want to. I'm sorry. I don't want to.*"

"Pike," said Isra.

"And Rusk said, *Fire,*" said Pike. "And we did. The *Patience*'s forward guns. I was on the second gun. I fired." He looked at his hands, the big ruined hands that were so gentle on the grapple arm. "We hit the engine. The skiff went dead. And it drifted. Just drifted, back toward the mouth, very slowly, with the pull. We watched it go in. Rusk said, *Well, he's a Keeper now,* and we turned for home." He was quiet for a while. "I never knew his name. They didn't give us names, on the escort. I asked, after. Nobody would tell me."

The heater ticked. Down Rope Street the knocking went on, door after door, and the lamps swung, and somewhere far off somebody was shouting.

"That's what they are," said Ostap Pike. "That's the Lantern. Don't fight it, Isra. It doesn't lose."

Isra sat with the shard in her fist, and her other hand on his back, and said nothing. On the bed Hanne put the cittern aside, very gently, and lay down with her face to the wall, and after a while Isra realised that the old woman was crying, without a sound, the way you do when you have been doing it all your life.
