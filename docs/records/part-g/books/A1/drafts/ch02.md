## Convoy
[
  Place: #The Shoals

  When: 6 October 2608

The raider came out of the shadow of a rock called Dorcas Four, which was exactly where Juno Asante had said it would, and for one clean second she let herself enjoy being right.

"Contact," said Marsh at tactical. "Bearing two-one-one, low, one ship. Small. She's running cold, Captain, I've only got her on optics — there. There. She's going for the third sled."

"I see her." On the main display the convoy strung away ahead of the *Vigil* in a long careful line: four ore-tugs and their tow-sleds of Shoals glass, bound in for Hollin and the Keeping stores, with the *Vigil* riding behind them like a dog behind sheep. The raider was a fleck of shadow crossing the face of the rock. "Helm, come to two-one-one, all ahead full. Weapons, wake up the forward battery. Hollis, I want everything you've got in the next ninety seconds and I don't care what it costs the bearings."

"It'll cost the bearings," said Chief Hollis from engineering, sounding pleased about it, and the corvette leapt.

The *Vigil* was nine years old and had been at war for six of them. She had been built as a fast picket, long and narrow and lightly armoured, with a forward battery that could ruin a freighter's day and not much else, and she had been patched and re-patched through the whole of the Severance until there was more repair in her than original hull. Juno had commanded her for two years. She loved her the way you love a dog that has bitten you more than once in your own defence.

The raider saw them coming. It broke off from the third sled — too late; it had already put a grapple on the tow-frame and was trying to cut the sled loose and run with it — and for a moment the two ships hung there on the display, the little raider tethered to forty tonnes of glass it could not let go of and could not drag, the *Vigil* coming down on it like a thrown knife.

"Hail her," Juno said. "Open channel. Tell her to cut the grapple and stand down and nobody gets hurt."

Marsh hailed. The raider did not answer. It fired its thrusters again, hard, and the sled lurched on its frame.

"She's trying to snap the tow," Marsh said. "If she snaps it the sled goes tumbling and takes out the fourth tug behind it."

"Forward battery," Juno said. "Target her engines. Not the hull. Engines only."

"Captain —"

"Engines, Danny."

The battery fired. On the display the raider's stern bloomed white and then dark, and the little ship slewed round on its own grapple line and hung there, spinning slowly, venting something that glittered. The sled steadied. The convoy came on.

"Good shooting," Juno said, and meant it, and let her breath out.

They took the raider under tow themselves, because there was nobody else to do it, and Juno went across with the boarding party in a suit that smelled of the last person who had worn it. The raider was a Shoals-built cutter, home-made from three other ships, with a hand-lettered name on the airlock that read *NO MORE*. Inside it was very cold, because the engine hit had taken out the heat. Inside it there were six people.

The oldest was perhaps twenty. The youngest — Juno looked at her for a long time — the youngest could not have been more than fifteen, a thin girl with a shaved head and a Shoals clan tattoo along her jaw, holding a pry-bar as though she meant to use it on a corvette's boarding party. Behind her the others had their hands up. One of them was crying with the steady, exhausted crying of someone who has been crying for a while.

"Put it down," Juno said, through her helmet speaker.

"You put yours down," said the girl.

"I'm not holding anything."

"You're holding a warship," the girl said, and her voice cracked across the middle of the word, and she put the pry-bar down.

They were Draw-dodgers. Juno had known before she came across. The Shoals sent a quota of names into the Wheel at every Draw, one for every three hundred souls, and this time the Shoals' Free League had refused to send any at all — that refusal was the whole of the war — and the Compact had sent the Navy into the Shoals to take the names by registry instead. Six years. Six years of boarding habitats and copying registries and escorting Draw officers with their slates, and boys and girls like these slipping out on stolen ships to wherever the registries could not find them, and raiding glass convoys on the way because a sled of glass would buy you a new name in any of a dozen rocks that did not ask questions. Juno had boarded fifty ships like the *No More*. She had not got used to it. She had got used to not getting used to it.

"We need medical across," she said to Marsh on the private channel. "The heat's gone. They'll be hypothermic in an hour."

"Aye, Captain. And — Captain. Signal from Squadron. Admiral Greaves."

She went back across with the medics and took it in her day cabin with her suit still half on. Tobiah Greaves's face on the screen was square and grey and tired, the face of a man who had been winning a war for six years and had begun to suspect he was not going to finish it.

"Good work on the convoy," he said. "The Shoals cutter. You took her intact?"

"Engines only, sir. Six aboard. Draw age, or near it. One's a child."

"Registered?"

"I don't know yet, sir."

"They'll be registered somewhere. Hand them over to the Wardens' officer at Hollin, they'll know what to do." Greaves looked at something off his screen. "Asante, there's another matter. You're relieved for seventy-two hours as of arrival. Marsh can take her round to the yards. You're to attend the Draw in person."

"Sir, I —"

"Your mother's orders, not mine," Greaves said. "Or the Speaker's office, which I'm told is not the same thing. The Speaker would like her family seen at the Hall. It's the Thirtieth, Asante. It's a deep one. People need to see the Speaker's family standing up with everybody else." He paused. "Your sister's of age this time, isn't she?"

"Nineteen, sir."

"Well," said Greaves, and seemed to look for something to say, and not to find it. "Well. One in four thousand. She'll be fine. Go home, Commander. Stand where people can see you."

The screen went dark. Juno sat in her half-suit in the cold cabin and listened to the medics moving through the passage outside with the girl from the *No More*, who was swearing at them in a Shoals dialect, steadily and inventively, as if that was the only warm thing she had left.

## The Wheel

The Compact Hall had been built, so the guides said, on the floor of the old Concession meeting-house, and if you stood at the very back under the gallery, where the ceiling came down low, you could still see the original salt wall: rough, grey-white, with the marks of the cutting machines on it in long curved grooves like the inside of a shell. Everything else had been built up and out from there over three hundred years. The Hall rose in tiers now, cut terraces of salt dressed with pale stone, and a light-well opened at the top of it to the surface eight hundred metres above, so that when Caul was up the whole Hall filled with a slow banded amber like the inside of a cut agate. Caul was up. It was always up for the Draw. The Wardens arranged it so.

Juno stood on the Speaker's terrace in her dress greys between her mother and her sister and did not look at the Wheel.

She looked at the Tally instead. It hung on the original salt wall behind the Wardens' bench, as it had for as long as there had been a Compact: a plate of old steel half a metre across, dark, pitted, oddly unimpressive, in a frame of polished brass far too fine for it. The Founders' Tally. As a child she had been told it bore the names of the dead of the Concession, the miners and cutters who had died in the salt before there was a Compact, and that the first Keepers were on it too, and she had stood on tiptoe and looked for them and been disappointed, because three centuries of hands had touched it and worn it smooth, and you could not read a single name. You could feel them, if you were allowed to touch it, which children were, once, on their Naming Day — rows of shallow scratches under your fingers, like braille in a language nobody spoke any more. She remembered the cold of it. She remembered thinking that it was the oldest thing she had ever touched.

"Stop fidgeting," her mother said, without moving her lips.

"I'm not fidgeting."

"You're doing the thing with your thumb."

Juno stopped doing the thing with her thumb. Efua Asante, Speaker of the Assembly of the Compact of the Reach, stood beside her in black and silver with her hands folded and her face composed into an expression of grave civic attention that Juno had seen her wear at funerals, budget debates and her own husband's cremation, and that Juno had never once been able to see behind. She was sixty-one. She had been Speaker for eight years and had spent the last six of them trying to end a war that the Assembly would not let her end, and she looked, Juno thought, very tired, in a way that only someone who had known her face for thirty-one years would see.

On Juno's other side Pell was not fidgeting at all.

She stood very straight in the plain white smock that everyone of Draw age wore to the Hall, with her hands loose at her sides and her chin up and her eyes on the Wheel, and she was smiling a little. Not the nervous smile you saw on some of the faces in the tiers around them, the bright fixed grin of someone telling themselves it was one in four thousand. Something quieter. As if she were listening to music.

"Pell," Juno said.

"Mm."

"Are you all right?"

"I'm fine." Pell glanced at her, and the smile widened into something more like her own. "You look awful. Did they not let you sleep on the way in?"

"I boarded a ship full of children."

"Oh, Juno." Pell's face changed. "Were they —"

"They're fine. They're cold and they're in a cell and they're going to be handed over to the Wardens, but they're fine." She heard her own voice and did not like it. "Sorry. It's not — sorry. Ignore me."

Pell reached over and squeezed her hand, briefly, the way she had done when she was six and Juno was eighteen and leaving for the Academy, as if Pell were the one who had to be brave about it. Then the bell rang, and the Hall went quiet, and the Wardens came in.

They came from the back, through the salt door, in procession: thirty or forty of them in the grey of the order with the silver thread at the cuffs, walking slowly, two by two, singing. Juno had never liked the song. It was very old, and it had no tune she could hold onto — a long held drone that the Wardens called the Hunger Tone, under which the voices of the procession wandered up and down in a kind of chant, so that the whole thing sounded less like singing than like something enormous breathing in its sleep. The Hall took it up. You were supposed to. Thousands of voices finding the drone, holding it, until the salt walls hummed with it and you could feel it in your teeth. Pell was singing. Their mother was singing, in her perfect civic contralto. Juno moved her lips.

At the end of the procession came the High Warden.

Cassius Mor was sixty-six and walked like a much younger man, upright, unhurried, his white hair cropped close to a long well-made skull. He did not sing. He carried, on a cushion of grey velvet held out before him in both hands, the Founder's Fork: a tuning fork of brass, fifteen centimetres long, its tines worn bright, its stem engraved with letters three centuries of polishing had worn down to ghosts. Juno had seen it up close once, at a reception at the Anchorhold when she was a cadet. You could make out FOR CL and nothing after. The Wardens' motto came from it. *For Clarity.* It was said to have been the First Warden's own, the instrument by which the First Warden had first heard the Needle's hunger; it was the holiest object in the Reach, and it was carried before the Wheel at every Draw, and at every Keeping it was struck once in the Lectern at the Anchorhold at the moment the Keepers went in.

Mor laid it on the bench beneath the Tally, on its cushion. The drone died away. In the silence he raised his hands.

"The First Warden heard the Needle's hunger," he said, "and the Reach answered."

"*We feed the door, that it may stay a door,*" said the Hall, eight thousand voices at once, and Juno with them, because that much she could never not say; it was in her like her own name.

"What the Needle keeps," said Mor, "it keeps for us."

"*It keeps for us.*"

"Twenty-nine times," said Mor, "the door has narrowed in our keeping. Twenty-nine times we have answered, and twenty-nine times it has opened again. We come now to the Thirtieth." He let the silence hold. His voice was warm, unhurried, beautifully pitched; he spoke the way a good doctor tells you it is going to hurt. "The readers at the Anchorhold tell me it will be deep. Deeper than any in two hundred years. Some of you are afraid, and you are right to be. I am afraid. But we have done this before, and we know how it is done. We give what is asked of us, and the door opens. That is the covenant. It is the oldest promise in the Reach. It has never been broken." He turned, and laid his hand on the Wheel. "Nine are asked. Let the Wheel choose them."

The Wheel was brass, a drum the size of a man lying down, on a cradle of black iron. It held, so the guides said, every name in the Reach between the ages of eighteen and forty, each on its own slip of steel foil, and it turned on its cradle with a long grinding hush that you could hear from every tier of the Hall. Two Wardens turned it. Juno watched it turn. Despite herself, despite everything, she watched it turn, and the foil slipped and hissed inside it like rain on a hull, and her mouth was dry.

It stopped. A Warden opened the hatch. Mor reached in and drew out a slip of foil and held it up to the light-well, and read.

"Tavish Orme, of the Fourth Terrace."

Somebody screamed, high up in the tiers. Not the man himself — someone near him. A mother, a wife. Juno did not look. The Wardens went up into the tiers, two of them, toward the sound, and the Wheel turned again.

"Abbe Sorrell, of Corrie Rock in the Shoals."

A murmur, this time. A Shoals name, by registry; Abbe Sorrell would not be in the Hall. Somewhere out in the Shoals a Navy boarding party would be carrying a slate to a habitat to find her. It might be the *Vigil*. Juno closed her eyes for a second and opened them.

"Ezra Lind, of the Dockside."

A young man in the tier below them sat down suddenly on the salt step as if his legs had been cut, and stayed sitting, with his hands over his mouth. He had a dock clerk's ink stains on his fingers. He looked about twenty-two.

The Wheel turned. Four. Five. Six. Juno counted them without hearing the names. She was aware of her mother beside her, not moving; of the light-well's amber shifting slowly on the far wall as Caul turned; of the Tally's dull steel square above the bench. She was aware of Pell's hand, which had found hers again at some point and was holding it, quite loosely, warm and dry.

The Wheel stopped for the seventh time. Mor reached into it. He drew out a slip and held it up to the light, and looked at it. And then he looked — Juno would think about this afterward, for a long time, turning it over and over the way you turn a stone in your pocket — he looked up, across the floor of the Hall, at the Speaker's terrace. Only for a moment. Then he read the name, slowly, as if he wanted every syllable of it to reach the back of the Hall.

"Pell Asante," said the High Warden, "of the Speaker's house."

The Hall made a sound Juno had never heard it make: a great soft collective intake of breath, eight thousand people drawing in air at once. Then silence.

Juno did not move. She could not. Her body had become a single held thing. Beside her, she heard her mother make a very small noise in her throat, the kind of noise you make when you have been cut and have not yet looked at the cut.

And Pell let go of her hand.

She stepped forward to the edge of the terrace, into the light, in her white smock, and stood there so that the Hall could see her. She was smiling. It was not a brave smile; it was not for anyone. It was the smile she had worn all morning, the listening smile, and Juno understood, watching her sister's face lifted toward the amber light-well with tears running quietly down it, that Pell was happy. That Pell had wanted this. That she had stood in the Hall all morning listening to the Hunger Tone and waiting to be chosen, the way you wait for someone you love to come through a door.

The Wardens came for her. Pell went down the steps to them without looking back.

## Terrace
[
  Place: #The Asante apartment
  Characters: @@Juno Asante @@Pell Asante @@Efua Asante
  When: same day
]
The Speaker's rooms were on the Terrace level of Tallow Under, high enough to have a window onto one of the great light-wells, and when Juno was a child she had thought it was the finest view in the Reach: a hundred metres of shaft dropping away below, terraced and planted, lit by Caul's banded glow from above and by the thousand small lamps of the houses cut into its walls, like looking down into a well full of stars. She stood at it now with a glass in her hand that she had not drunk from and looked at nothing.

The Wardens had brought Pell home at nineteen hundred. They would come for her again in the morning to take her up to the Anchorhold, where the nine would live and train together until the Narrowing — fifty-eight days, now. She had been given the night with her family. It was customary.

Pell was in her room, packing. She had been packing for an hour. Juno could hear her through the wall, moving about, humming. Humming the Hunger Tone.

Their mother sat at the table with a slate in front of her that she was not reading.

"I'll put in for substitution," Juno said to the window.

"Juno."

"It's allowed. A volunteer of age can stand in for a drawn Keeper with the High Warden's leave. I'm thirty-one. I'm of age. It's in the Articles."

"It's in the Articles," her mother agreed. "It has been granted four times in three hundred years."

"Then it'll be five."

"Juno." Efua Asante put the slate down. "Sit down."

"I don't want to sit down."

"I know. Sit down anyway."

Juno sat down. Her mother looked at her across the table, and for a moment the civic face slipped, and what was under it was so naked and so old that Juno had to look away.

"You think I haven't thought of it," her mother said quietly. "You think I didn't think of it before he'd finished saying her name. I'm the Speaker. There is nothing in the Articles I haven't read. There is no petition, no appeal, no procedure, no favour I could call in, that I have not thought of in the last six hours." She spread her hands flat on the table. They were shaking slightly; Juno had never seen them shake. "And she doesn't want any of them."

"She's nineteen."

"She's nineteen," her mother agreed. "And she's my daughter, and she believes in it, and she wants to go. Have you talked to her?"

Juno had not. She had come home and gone to the window.

She went to Pell's room. The door was open. Pell was sitting on the floor among her canvases, with a duffel half-packed beside her, holding a painting on her knees and looking at it. The room smelled of oil and turpentine and the faint sweet resin of the varnish she used. Every wall was covered. Pell painted the Needle. She had painted nothing else since she was fourteen: the Needle from Hollin's surface, a smear of wrong stars over the salt pans; the Needle from the Anchorhold, huge and bruise-dark; the Needle as nobody had ever seen it, from inside, from the throat, in colours that did not exist — a long tunnel of folded light opening and opening into something that was not quite white. Juno had never known what to say about them. They frightened her a little.

"Hey," Pell said, without looking up.

"Hey."

"I can't take all of them. They said one bag." Pell turned the painting on her knees toward the light. It was one of the throat ones. "I thought maybe this one. Do you think this one?"

"Pell."

"I know." Pell put the painting down very carefully, face up, on the floor. "I know what you're going to say. You're going to say you'll put in for substitution."

"Yes."

"Please don't."

"Pell —"

"Please don't, Juno." Her sister looked up at her, finally, and her eyes were red and perfectly calm. "I know you think it's — I know what you think. You've been out there for six years taking names off Shoals kids. You think it's a machine for killing people. I know." She took a breath. "But it's not, to me. It's never been. Ever since I was little I've — you remember when we went up to the Anchorhold for your commissioning, and they let us into the Lectern, and I heard the harp? I was eight. I heard it and I thought, *that's it, that's the sound, that's what everything is.* And I've been painting it ever since and I can never get it right. I can never get the inside of it right." She gestured at the walls, at the tunnels of impossible light. "And now I'm going to see it. Me. Out of everyone. The Wheel chose me." Her voice shook for the first time. "Do you know what that's like? To be chosen? To have your name come out of the Wheel and know that it means something, that you mean something, that the whole Reach is going to stand in the Hall and sing for you? I'm not afraid, Juno. I keep waiting to be afraid and I'm not."

Juno knelt down among the canvases, because her legs were not reliable, and put her arms round her sister, and Pell let her, and they stayed like that for a long time without speaking. Pell smelled of turpentine. Through the wall, from the window, came the far, faint sound of someone singing in the light-well below: the Hunger Tone, held and held, as it would be held in every house in Tallow Under tonight.

When Juno came back out, her mother was standing at the window where Juno had stood.

"Well?" said Efua.

"She wants to go."

"Yes."

"I'm putting in for substitution anyway."

"I know you are." Her mother did not turn round. In the glass Juno could see her reflection, faint, laid over the well of lights below; the civic face was back on, perfectly composed. "Juno. When he read the name. Did you see his face?"

Juno was quiet for a moment.

"He looked at our terrace," she said. "Before he read it."

"Yes," said her mother. "He did."

Neither of them said anything else. Below them, in the well, the singing went on, and on, and on.
