## Petition
[
  Place: #The archive
  Characters: @@Adaeze Quill @@Juno Asante
  Relationships: ~Adaeze Quill/Juno Asante:Archivist and petitioner
  When: 18 October 2608
]
The petitions for substitution came to Adaeze Quill because nobody else wanted them, and because she was the archivist, and the archivist was where things went when the order did not know what to do with them.

There had been eleven so far, in the ten days since the Draw. There were always petitions. A mother for her son, a husband for his wife, a woman of thirty-nine for her nephew of eighteen; they came up from Tallow Under on the supply shuttle in envelopes of good paper, or in person, in their best clothes, and sat in the chair across Adaeze's desk in the little office off the archive and explained, very carefully, as if she might not have understood the Articles, that they were of age and willing and asked only to take the place of the one they loved. Adaeze listened to every one. She wrote them out fair, in the old hand, on the order's vellum, and sealed them with her own seal, and sent them up to the High Warden, and the High Warden declined them. He had declined every one for nineteen years. Four had been granted in the whole history of the Compact. It was not a door, the substitution; it was a painting of a door on a wall. Everyone knew it. They came anyway. Adaeze thought that the coming was the point — that people needed to have knocked.

The twelfth was in naval greys, and sat down across the desk without being asked.

"Commander Asante," said Adaeze.

"You know me."

"Everyone in the Anchorhold knows your sister's name." Adaeze folded her hands. "And I saw you, once, at your commissioning. You were nineteen. You had your hair much shorter. Your sister was very small and kept wandering off into the harp hall."

Juno Asante looked at her properly for the first time. Adaeze saw herself being looked at: a woman of forty-four in Warden's grey, round-faced, with ink on her fingers and her hair tied back in the plain knot of the order and a pair of reader's spectacles on a cord round her neck. Nobody remarkable. She had never minded being nobody remarkable.

"I've come to petition," said the Commander.

"I know."

"I'm thirty-one. I'm of Draw age. I'm a volunteer. Under the Articles, Section Nine — "

"I know the Articles, Commander." Adaeze said it gently. "I'll write it out. I'll write it out as well as anybody has ever written one, and I'll seal it, and I'll take it up to the High Warden myself, which is more than I do for most of them. I want you to know that before I tell you the rest."

"Which is?"

"That he will decline it."

The Commander's jaw set. "You don't know that."

"He has declined every petition for substitution since he was invested. Two hundred and six of them, at the last three Draws. I've written every one." Adaeze pulled a sheet of vellum toward her and uncapped her pen. "I've read the reasons too. They're always the same. The Needle chose; it is not for the Wardens to correct the Needle. It's theologically sound. I'm afraid it's also unanswerable."

She began to write. *To the High Warden of the Throat, from Juno Efua Asante, Lieutenant Commander of the Compact Navy, of the Speaker's house, being of age and of sound mind — .* She wrote in the old hand, the slanting formal script the order had used since before the Compact, and she could feel the Commander watching the pen move.

"You believe that," Juno Asante said. "That the Needle chooses."

Adaeze kept writing. "I believe the Wheel is a lottery and the Needle is a wormhole," she said, "and that between them, every four thousand days, nine people are asked to do something very terrible on behalf of everyone else. I believe it has kept the door open for three hundred years. Whether the Needle *chooses* — " She dipped the pen. "I'm the archivist, Commander. I believe in records. The records say that every time we have kept it, it has opened. That's what I believe."

"And the people who don't come back?"

Adaeze stopped writing.

"I have their names," she said. "Every one, every Keeping. A hundred and twelve. They're in the archive behind that door. I read them, on the anniversaries." She looked up. "I'm not a fool, Commander. I'm not a fanatic. I didn't take the grey because I think it's beautiful. I took it because somebody has to keep the records honestly, and I thought it might as well be someone who could bear to read them."

The Commander was quiet. Adaeze finished the petition and sanded it and folded it and dripped the grey wax and pressed her seal into it, and held it up.

"Would you like to see the harp?" she said. "While you're here. Your sister did, when she was eight. She stood in front of it for an hour. We had to carry her out."

They went through the archive — a long vaulted room, cold, lined floor to ceiling with racks of harp-records on spools and crystal and the oldest of them on paper, three hundred years of the Needle's voice written down — and through the iron door at its end into the harp hall.

It had been the Lectern once, in the Concession's time. The dome was the same dome, a hemisphere of old glass looking out on the mouth, and the floor was the same floor, worn into hollows by three centuries of feet. The harp stood in the middle of it. Adaeze had served here for twenty years and it still stopped her breath, sometimes, coming through the door. A frame of fused silica taller than a man, gone faintly gold with age, strung with nine courses of resonator wire that caught the grey light of the mouth and held it. It was singing. It was always singing. The long, wide, many-stranded drone of the throat, low and soft, filling the hall, so that coming into it was like walking into the inside of a held breath.

"The frame is original," Adaeze said quietly. "The First Warden's. The wires have been restrung many times. We tune them every morning to the Hunger Tone." She nodded to a small alcove off the hall, behind a grille of black iron, where a lamp burned. "The Fork is kept there, between Draws. It's struck once, at every Keeping, here, at the moment the Keepers go in. That's all. It's never used for tuning. It's too precious."

"What's it for, then? A tuning fork you don't tune with."

Adaeze smiled. "That's the sort of question your sister would ask."

The Commander did not smile. She stood in the middle of the hall with her hands at her sides and looked at the harp, and then up through the dome at the mouth, the bruise-dark wound with its rim of bent light, and Adaeze watched her face and saw on it something that she had seen on many faces in twenty years and had never been able to name. Not fear. Something more like grief, arriving early.

"She'll be up here," the Commander said. "Training. For seven weeks."

"Yes."

"Will you — " She stopped.

"I'll look in on her," said Adaeze. "I look in on all of them."

## The High Warden
[
  Place: #The Director's Gallery
  Characters: @@Juno Asante @@Cassius Mor
  Items: ^The Fork:#The reliquary
  When: 19 October 2608
]
The High Warden received her the next morning, which was itself, Juno understood, a kind of answer.

His rooms were at the very top of the Anchorhold's spindle, at the Needle-ward end: a long low gallery with a curved window filling one wall, so that the mouth hung outside it vast and dark, like a painting of nothing. It was almost bare. A desk. Two chairs. A cabinet of old black wood with a lock. On the floor a carpet of some deep red stuff so old that its pattern had worn to the weft in a path from the door to the desk, as if the same feet had walked that line for centuries. Cassius Mor stood at the window with his back to her when the novice showed her in, and did not turn round at once.

"Commander Asante," he said to the glass. "Sit down. Please."

She did not sit. After a moment he turned, and smiled at her — a warm, tired, genuinely kind smile, the smile of a man who has been expecting a difficult conversation and is sorry it has to happen — and came and sat behind the desk himself, and gestured again at the chair, and this time she sat, because standing had become ridiculous.

Her petition lay on the desk between them, its grey seal broken.

"Sister Adaeze writes a beautiful hand," Mor said. "And she has put your case as well as it could be put. Better. I've read it twice." He laid his palm on the vellum, gently, as if it were a living thing. "I'm going to decline it, Commander. I expect you know that. I wanted to tell you so myself, rather than send it down with a seal on it, because I think you are owed that, and because I wanted you to understand why."

"The Needle chose," Juno said.

"The Needle chose." He nodded slowly. "You think that's a formula. A thing we say. It is a thing we say. But it is also true, and I would like you to try, just for a moment, to hear it as true." He leaned forward. "The Draw is the only thing in the Reach that is perfectly fair. Do you understand? The only thing. The Speaker's daughter and the dock clerk and the Shoals girl in a rock with no name — every one of them a slip of foil in the same drum. No money buys you out of it. No family. No office. It is the one place where we are all the same. That is why it works, Commander. That is why the Reach has borne it for three hundred years. Because it is fair." He spread his hands. "And if I let the Speaker's daughter out, because the Speaker's other daughter is a decorated officer who has come in her greys to ask me — then it is not fair any more. Then it is a thing the powerful can get their children out of. And the next time the Wheel turns, every mother in the Fourth Terrace will know it. And the Draw will be broken. And the Keeping with it."

It was a good speech. Juno had heard admirals give worse. She sat and let it go past her and watched his face.

"There are four substitutions in the record," she said.

"There are. In three hundred years. Each of them for reasons which I would not wish on your family." He sighed. "Commander. I know what you are. I know what you've done in this war. I know you've been out in the Shoals for six years taking names off registries for the Draw — " a small, delicate pause — "and I know what that has cost you, because I have read your reports, and you write them very honestly. You of all people know what the Draw costs. You of all people know what it would mean if it were seen to bend."

She did not answer.

"How is your mother?" Mor said.

The change of direction was so smooth that it took her a second to feel it.

"She's well."

"I'm glad. She carries a great deal. I know it." He looked at her with what seemed to be real concern. "The appropriation for the Shoals campaign comes before the Assembly next week. I understand she hasn't yet said how she'll vote."

"I don't discuss my mother's votes, High Warden."

"No. Quite right." He smiled again. "I mention it only because the Keeping cannot be flown if the Shoals can raid the glass stores at will. You know better than I how close they came last month. The Keeping must be protected, this time of all times. The Thirtieth is deep. Deeper than anything in two hundred years. If the escort is not funded — " He let it trail away, and shook his head slightly, as if at the size of the thought. "Well. I hope she will remember that her daughter flies in it. I'm sure she will."

Juno looked at him. Outside the window, beyond his shoulder, the mouth of the Needle hung huge and patient, and she thought of the Hall, ten days ago: the seventh slip of foil held up to the light; the High Warden's eyes lifting from it, before he read, across the floor to the Speaker's terrace. Only for a moment.

"Thank you for seeing me, High Warden," she said, and stood.

"Commander." He stood too, courteous. "Your sister is extraordinary. I've met her. She has a gift for this that I have rarely seen. She is not afraid. You should be very proud."

"I am."

"Then let her go," said Cassius Mor gently. "It is the last thing you can give her."

## The Nine
[

They gave the nine a dormitory on the second wheel, nine narrow cells off a common room with a window on the mouth, and they gave them each a bead.

It was the first thing, the night they arrived: a Warden going round the common room with a little wooden box, and each of them taking one out, a nub of cloudy brine-glass the size of a pea on a black cord. You wore it from the night you came up until the night you flew. Pell put hers on at once and felt it settle into the hollow of her throat, cool, and lie there, very slightly to the left. Toward the mouth. The Warden said it always would.

"It's to remind you," the Warden said, "that you are already part of it."

Ezra Lind, sitting on the bench beside her, turned his bead over in his ink-stained fingers and said, under his breath, "Part of what, though," and then looked horrified at himself, and put it on very fast.

By the fourth day Pell liked him best of all of them. There was Tavish Orme, who was thirty-eight and had three children and did not speak to anyone; there was Abbe Sorrell, the Shoals girl they had brought in on a Navy ship with her hands cuffed, who had stopped fighting on the second day and stopped eating on the third; there were five others whose faces Pell was learning, a baker and a lift-mechanic and a woman who had been a Warden novice once and left. And there was Ezra, who was twenty-two and a clerk in the dock office and had never flown anything bigger than a cargo sled across a hangar floor, and who was so frightened, all the time, that it came out of him as jokes.

"The thing is," he said to her in the simulator bay on the fourth day, strapped into the mock cockpit of a Keeper's skiff with the screens full of grey-gold throat and his hands white on the stick, "the thing is that I failed my sled licence twice. Twice. They told me I didn't have the spatial reasoning. And now the Needle has looked into my soul and said, *Ezra Lind, you are the man to fly into my throat.* I think the Needle's made a clerical error. I think I should file a complaint."

"File it with Sister Quill," Pell said. "She does the paperwork."

"I tried. She said the Needle doesn't accept complaints. She said it very kindly." He banked, too hard, and the simulator screamed at him and the screens went red. "Oh, for — that's the fourth time I've died today. Do you think it counts? Do you think if you die enough times in the simulator the Needle lets you off the real one?"

"I don't think it works like that."

"No," said Ezra, and looked at the red screen, and his face changed for a second, and he said in a different voice, "No, I don't suppose it does," and then he reset the simulator and made a joke about the instructor's moustache.

The skiffs were very simple. That was what surprised Pell. She had imagined something grand, something like the ships in the old paintings in the Compact Hall; but a Keeper's skiff was twelve metres of narrow hull with a single seat and a cradle under the belly for the glass, and an engine, and a release lever painted white. You flew it into the mouth at the start of the Long Night, when the throat was tightening, following the reader's line, and you flew it down the throat as far as the waist, and at the waist you pulled the white lever and the glass went, and then you turned round and flew back the way you came. That was all. The instructors went over it and over it. The line, the waist, the lever, the turn. The line, the waist, the lever, the turn.

They did not talk about how many turned and did not come back. Nobody did. Pell knew the number anyway, the way everybody in the Reach knew it: a little under half. A hundred and twelve names in Sister Quill's archive. She thought about it sometimes, at night, in her cell, with the bead lying cool against her throat. She waited to be frightened. She was not frightened. She was something else that she did not have a word for, something that hummed in her like the harp.

She went to the harp hall on the sixth night, very late, because she could not sleep and because it was the only place in the Anchorhold she wanted to be.

Sister Quill was there. She was sitting on the floor with her back against the wall under the dome, in the dark, with her spectacles in her lap, listening. She looked up when Pell came in and did not seem surprised.

"I come here too," she said. "When I can't sleep. Sit down, if you like."

Pell sat down beside her. The harp sang in front of them, gold-wired, the long low many-stranded drone of the throat, and above them in the dome the mouth hung, huge, its rim of light trembling very faintly. It had begun to tremble on the fourth day. The readers said it was the Narrowing beginning in earnest. You could see it if you watched for long enough: a slow shiver at the edges, like heat over a road.

"Can I ask you something?" Pell said.

"You can ask."

"What does it look like? The waist. Inside."

Adaeze Quill was quiet for a while.

"I don't know," she said. "Nobody does. The ones who come back don't speak of it. It's a rule of the order — they're asked not to, and they don't. I've asked some of them myself, privately, when I was younger and more curious. They wouldn't tell me." She turned the spectacles over in her lap. "One of them — an old man, he'd flown the Twenty-Sixth — told me that it wasn't a thing that had words. That's all he'd say."

"I've painted it," Pell said.

The Warden turned her head.

"I've been painting it since I was eight. Since I heard this." Pell nodded at the harp. "I never get it right. I brought one with me. They said one bag." She did not know why she was saying this. "Do you want to see it?"

She went and got it from her cell. When she came back Sister Quill had lit the little lamp in the alcove beside the reliquary grille, and Pell held the canvas up in its light. It was the throat one, the one she had chosen on the floor of her room in Tallow Under while Juno knelt among the canvases: the long tunnel of folded light, gold and grey and a colour that was not quite either, opening and opening down its length toward a place at the centre where everything drew in, tight as the iris of an eye, and in the middle of that, very small, something that was not quite white.

Sister Quill looked at it and did not speak.

"Where did you see that?" she said at last. Her voice was odd.

"I didn't see it. I heard it. I just — that's what the harp sounds like. To me. That's what it sounds like inside."

"The iris," said the Warden softly. "In the middle. Drawn in."

"That's the waist. I think. That's where it goes tight." Pell looked at her. "Why? What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong." Sister Quill took off her spectacles, which she had put on to look, and folded them carefully. "There's a description in the archive. Very old — one of the oldest things we have. An account of the throat in a Narrowing, written by a reader, from before the Compact. Most of it's lost. But there's a passage — " She stopped. "It says almost exactly that. *Drawn in like an iris. And at the centre, a light I have no name for.*" She gave a small, uncertain laugh. "I've read it a hundred times. I never saw it until now."

They sat together in the lamplight in front of the singing harp, the painting propped against Pell's knees, and neither of them said anything for a long time. Pell's bead lay against her throat, cool, leaning very slightly toward the mouth.

"I'm not afraid," Pell said finally. "Everyone keeps waiting for me to be afraid."

"I know," said Adaeze Quill. "I've noticed." She put a hand, briefly, on the back of Pell's neck, the way you would with a child. "It's all right not to be. And it will be all right, if you are."

## A Question

The order came through the day after she got back aboard: the *Vigil* was detached from the Shoals patrol and assigned to the close escort of the Thirtieth Keeping, with station at the Anchorhold from the twentieth of November until the Narrowing.

"They're giving us the Keeping," said Marsh, reading it off the slate in her day cabin with his eyebrows up. "That's — Captain, that's an honour. That's a flag posting. Six weeks parked at the Anchorhold guarding the glass stores and then we fly the Keepers to the mouth." He looked up and saw her face, and his own changed. "Oh. Oh, Captain. I didn't think. I'm sorry."

"Don't be. It's an honour." Juno took the slate off him. "Somebody pulled a string for it. I'd like to know whose."

"The Admiral, probably."

"Probably," Juno said, and did not believe it.

Mor, she thought. Or her mother. Either of them could have done it, for opposite reasons. She did not know which would be worse.

The other message came in on the evening watch, on a channel it should not have been able to use: a Shoals relay, bounced off three rocks and an abandoned ore station, unencrypted, plain text, addressed to her by name. Marsh brought it to her himself, with the door shut behind him.

"It came in on the old Academy frequency," he said. "The cadet band. Nobody's used it in years. Comms only caught it because Siddiq's a sentimental idiot and still listens to it." He put the slate down in front of her. "Captain, if this gets logged — "

"It's logged," Juno said. "Everything's logged. Leave it with me."

He left it with her. She read it once.

*JUNO. I HEARD ABOUT PELL. I'M SORRY. YOU KNOW I AM. ASK THEM HOW THE NAMES ARE CHOSEN. — R.*

She sat with the slate in her hands until the watch changed.

Rhee Calder had been in her year at the Academy. They had shared a simulator pod for two years and a bed for six months of the third, and he had been the best pilot in their intake and the worst at doing as he was told, and she had loved him in the uncomplicated way you love someone at twenty. He had a younger brother, Callum. At the Twenty-Ninth Draw, eleven years ago, when Juno was twenty and Rhee was twenty and Callum was eighteen, the Wheel had turned and Callum Calder's name had come out of it. He had flown. He had not come back. Rhee had gone very quiet, and stayed quiet through their last year, and two days after commissioning he had taken a Compact patrol cutter out on a routine flight to the Shoals and not brought it back either. Six years ago, when the Shoals refused their quota, the Free League's first fleet commander had been a man the Compact papers called the Saltjack, and the face in the grainy picture had been his.

She had fired on his ships. He had fired on hers. They had never spoken since.

*Ask them how the names are chosen.*

She thought of the Hall. The seventh slip of foil. The High Warden's eyes lifting to the Speaker's terrace before he read. *I hope she will remember that her daughter flies in it.* She thought of Sister Quill at her desk: *two hundred and six petitions. He has declined every one.* The one place in the Reach where everyone was the same.

She logged the message, as regulations required, with a note — *unsolicited enemy contact, no reply sent* — and sent it up the chain to Squadron. Then she sat for a while longer in the dark of her cabin, with the *Vigil*'s engines thrumming through the deck and the slate's afterglow fading on her palm, and found that she had memorised it anyway.
