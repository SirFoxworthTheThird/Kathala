It was the archivist's duty, at every Keeping, to read the First.

Not the whole of it. Nobody had the whole of it. But on the night of the Long Night, while the Keepers flew and the Reach sang, the archivist stood in the harp hall beside the High Warden and read aloud from the harp records of the First Keeping — the waist's narrowing and its holding and its opening again, three hundred and seventeen years ago, in the numbers the First Warden's own readers had written down — so that the Reach would hear that it had been done before and had worked. Adaeze had read them at the Twenty-Ninth, eleven years ago, her first Keeping as archivist, with her voice shaking. She meant to read them better this time. So on the twenty-eighth of October, with thirty-eight days to go, she went down into the deep stacks to find them and copy them out fair.

The First Keeping's records were where they had always been: in the oldest rack, at the far end of the archive, in the cold, on spools of a crystal so old it had gone faintly yellow. The first spool's label was in the slanting hand of the order. *Harp. 2 May 2291. First Day After the Keeping.* She took it down and fed it into the old reader and listened to it while she copied: the throat's chord, steadying, opening; the readers' voices, flat with exhaustion, calling numbers. *Waist twenty-four. Twenty-six. Holding.* She knew it by heart.

It was only when she went to the rack for the spool before it — the Long Night itself, the night of the Keeping — that she found there was not one.

She stood in front of the rack for some time. Then she went and got a lamp.

The spool for the second of May was the first spool in the rack. She had always known that, the way you know the first page of a book is the first page, without ever thinking about it. There was nothing before it. No Long Night. No first of May. No April, no March, none of the readings from the weeks before the First Keeping, when the waist was narrowing and the First Warden heard its hunger and the Reach answered. The archive began on the morning after. It began, Adaeze realised, standing in the cold with the lamp in her hand, as if the Keeping had worked and somebody had then started writing it down.

But the Concession had had readers. The liturgy said so. *The readers of the Concession heard it narrow.* There had been a harp — this harp, the First Warden's harp, singing in the hall above her head. There had been forty years of records before the First Keeping. Where were they?

She went to the catalogue. The catalogue of the archive was itself a historical document, three hundred years of archivists' entries in a dozen hands, and its first page was older than the Compact. She found the entry she wanted in the very first column. *Concession harp records, 2247 to 1 May 2291: transferred to the keeping of the First Warden.*

And beside it, in a different and later hand, very small: *Not returned.*

That was all.

She went back to the rack with the lamp and looked at it properly, as an archivist looks, and saw what she had walked past for twenty years. At the end of the oldest rack, before the first spool, there was a space a hand's breadth wide where no spools were; and on the rack's old steel, in that space, there were marks. Bright scratches, long since dulled, where something had been pulled out hard. Clamps, sheared. The bolt-holes for a drawer that was not there.

The older archivists had a joke, when she was a novice. When you could not find a thing, they said it was in the Blank Year. It meant the time before the First Keeping, about which the order had nothing: nothing in the stacks, nothing in the catalogue, nothing anywhere but the liturgy and a single loose sheet in the reliquary inventory, a reader's description of the throat that someone had once found tucked into a book and kept. *Drawn in like an iris.* Adaeze had laughed at the joke, at twenty-four. She had never once thought to ask why there should be a Blank Year at all, in an order whose whole purpose was to keep the record.

She did not go back up to the harp hall until evening. When she did, it was to clean the Fork.

That was her duty too. Once a month, and before every Draw and every Keeping, the archivist took the Founder's Fork from its cushion in the reliquary and cleaned it with a soft cloth and a little oil, and laid it back. She had done it two hundred times. She went into the alcove behind the iron grille and lifted it from the grey velvet and held it to the lamp, as she always did, and turned it: fifteen centimetres of old brass, the tines worn bright, the stem's engraving worn to ghosts. FOR CL. She had always loved that. *For Clarity.* As if the First Warden had cut the order's whole purpose into the instrument with which the hunger was first heard.

She cleaned it. The harp sang behind her in the hall, its long wide drone. And when she had finished, standing at the grille with the oiled Fork in her hand and the drone in her ears, Adaeze Quill did a thing that no archivist had done in her lifetime, and very likely in anyone's. She struck it.

Just lightly. Against the heel of her hand, the way you would strike any fork. It was a tuning fork. It had been made to be struck.

The note came up out of it, pure and thin, and went out into the hall, and met the harp.

They did not agree.

It was not much. A hair. A quarter of a tone, perhaps less. But the Fork's note and the harp's drone were not the same note, and where they met they fought: a slow wavering throb in the air, *wah, wah, wah*, the sound two strings make when they are almost but not quite in tune, beating against each other. She stood in the alcove and listened to it beat until the Fork's note died away in her hand.

The harp was tuned every morning to the Hunger Tone. The Hunger Tone was the note the First Warden had heard the Needle's hunger in. The Fork was the instrument with which the First Warden had heard it.

And they were not the same note.

She laid the Fork back on its cushion with great care. Her hands were steady; she noted that, distantly, the way she would have noted the condition of a spool. She went back to her little office off the archive and closed the door and sat down at her desk and opened, not the order's daybook, but the small private notebook that she had kept since she was a novice, in which she wrote the things she did not know what to do with. She wrote the date. She wrote:

*No harp records before 2 May 2291. Forty-four years transferred to the First Warden. Not returned. Rack forced.*

*The Fork is not tuned to the Hunger Tone. I struck it. They beat.*

She looked at the two lines. Then she wrote a third, and looked at it, and did not cross it out:

*Why would anyone tune the harp away from the Fork?*

## Teague

The runner was a fast little packet out of Corrie Rock, and it tried to outrun the *Vigil* across the Shoals approaches, and it very nearly did.

"She's good," said Marsh at tactical, with reluctant admiration, as the packet jinked behind a tumbling ice-rock and came out the other side on a vector nobody had predicted. "She's really good, Captain. That's a Navy pilot. That's somebody we trained."

"Then she knows what happens next," said Juno. "Forward battery. Across her bow. Close."

The battery fired. The packet's pilot knew what happened next. She killed her drive and put her hands up, as far as a ship can, and the *Vigil* came alongside.

There were three aboard. Two were Shoals smugglers, sullen and scared, who sat where they were told. The third was a woman of fifty in a grey cardigan, who came out of the packet's tiny hold with her hands raised and a data slate held up in one of them, high, so that the boarding party could see it, the way you would hold up a white flag.

"Commander Asante," she said, as soon as Juno came through the lock. "I asked them to find me your ship. I've been trying to reach you for nine days. My name is Mara Teague. I was a lay clerk in the Draw office at the Compact Hall for nineteen years. I need to show you something."

They put her in Juno's day cabin, because it was the only room on the *Vigil* with a door that locked from the outside. Juno sat across the little table from her with Marsh standing at the door. Teague put the slate on the table between them and pushed it across with one finger.

"That's every name drawn in the last four Draws," she said. "The Twenty-Sixth to the Twenty-Ninth. And beside each one, how it was drawn."

"Drawn from the Wheel," Juno said. "That's how they're drawn."

"Some of them." Teague's voice was steady, low, a clerk's voice, precise. She had the hands of someone who had spent her life with paper. "Commander, I prepared the foils for the Wheel. That was my job. Every name in the Reach between eighteen and forty, on a strip of steel foil, cut and stamped and counted and fed into the drum. Every name the same weight, every foil the same size, so that the Wheel is fair. That's what the Articles say." She leaned forward. "The Shoals foils were cut at half-thickness. For the last four Draws. On written instruction from the High Warden's office. Thinner foil rides higher in the drum when it turns. Every clerk in the office knew. We were told it was a matter of security — the Shoals had to be seen to bear their share. Look at the numbers. The Shoals are a sixth of the Reach. Of the last thirty-six Keepers, nineteen were Shoals names."

Juno looked at the slate. Nineteen of thirty-six names had a small mark beside them.

"That's the first thing," said Teague. "The second thing is worse."

"Go on."

"At every Draw," said Mara Teague, "there is one foil that is not fed into the Wheel by the clerks. It is given to the High Warden in an envelope, by his secretary, on the morning of the Draw. He carries it in his sleeve. And when the Wheel stops and he reaches in, one of the times he reaches in, he does not take a foil from the drum." She was watching Juno's face. "We called it the sleeve name. Every clerk knew that too. Nobody spoke of it. It was — it was the price of the Draw, we told each other. The Wheel was fair, nearly. The High Warden just needed to be sure of one name. For the good of the Reach." She put one fingertip on the slate. "Those are the sleeve names. One a Draw. I copied them from the secretary's file before I ran, two years ago. Look at who they are."

Juno looked.

*Twenty-Sixth: Orla Venning, daughter of Assembly Member Tomas Venning (Treasury Committee; voted against the Wardens' appropriation, 2573).*

*Twenty-Seventh: Hal Brisk, son of the Shoals delegate Ana Brisk (moved the motion for Shoals quota relief, 2584).*

*Twenty-Eighth: Imre Saltash, brother of Commodore Lise Saltash (refused escort duty for the Twenty-Seventh Keeping on grounds of conscience).*

*Twenty-Ninth: Callum Calder, brother of Lieutenant Rhee Calder (Academy record: formal complaint to the Wardens' liaison regarding the treatment of Shoals cadets, 2596).*

She read the last line three times. Her mouth had gone dry.

"Rhee filed a complaint," she said. "In our third year. About the Shoals cadets. They were being given the worst postings. I — I signed it. Half our year signed it." She looked up. "Rhee's was the name on top."

"Yes," said Teague quietly. "That's how it works. It's never you. It's always the one you love."

The cabin was very quiet. Marsh, at the door, had gone pale.

"Why are you showing me this?" Juno said.

"Because I heard who was drawn seventh at the Thirtieth." Teague held her gaze. "I wasn't there. I don't have this year's file. I can't prove it. But I know how the Wheel works, Commander, and I know who your mother is, and I know what vote is before the Assembly this week. And I thought — " For the first time her voice wavered. "I thought, if I could get it to one person who could do something. Just one. Not the League. Not the papers. Somebody inside. Somebody who'd have a reason."

The comm on Juno's desk chimed. Squadron. Admiral Greaves.

She took it with the slate face-down under her hand. Greaves had been told about the runner. He had been told who was on it — the clerk's name was on a Navy watch-list as a League courier — and he was very clear. Mara Teague was a defector to the enemy in time of war and a carrier of enemy materials. She was to be transferred, with all materials, to the Wardens' guard at the Anchorhold, who had jurisdiction over offences against the Draw. The *Vigil* would make the transfer on her way in to her station. He was very clear about the materials.

"Understood, sir," said Juno.

When the screen went dark she sat for a moment with her hand flat on the slate. Then she said, without looking round, "Danny. Go and get me a clean slate from stores. A blank one. Take your time."

Marsh looked at her. Then he went, and took his time.

She copied it. All of it. It took four minutes. When Marsh came back with the blank slate she gave him the copy and said, "Put that somewhere it won't be logged. Not your quarters. Somewhere stupid," and he went white and nodded and put it inside his jacket, and she gave the original back to Teague.

"They're going to take this from you," she said. "At the Anchorhold. I can't stop that."

"I know," said Teague. She looked at the slate in her hands. "I always knew. Thank you for reading it."

The *Vigil* docked at the Anchorhold the next morning. Juno walked Mara Teague down the gangway herself, and handed her and her slate to a Warden guard-captain with a polite, bored face, and signed the transfer, and watched them walk her away across the hangar between two guards, small in her grey cardigan, not looking back.

Two days later, on the evening watch, a routine notice came through the Navy channel from the Wardens' office: *Mara Teague, held for offences against the Draw, died in custody of a heart condition on 31 October. Next of kin notified.*

Juno read it and logged it and went to her cabin and was sick in the little steel basin, quietly, so that no one would hear.

## Call

Pell called on the first of November, on the Anchorhold link, at the hour the nine were allowed calls.

She looked well. That was the first thing. Her face filled the little screen in Juno's cabin, flushed and bright-eyed, her hair cut short now in the Keepers' fashion, the bead at her throat on its black cord, and behind her a common room with a window on the mouth and somebody laughing off-screen.

"You look terrible," Pell said cheerfully. "Are you eating?"

"I'm eating."

"You're not. You've got your not-eating face. Mum's got it too, I saw her on the Assembly broadcast. You've both got it." Pell grinned. "I'm eating enough for all three of us. They feed us like we're being fattened for something."

"Pell."

"It's a joke, Juno." Pell's grin softened. "Sorry. It's what we do up here. You have to. Ezra says if you don't laugh about it you have to think about it, and if you think about it you'll go out of the airlock without a suit." She glanced off-screen. "He's very funny. He does an impression of the flight instructor that makes Tavish laugh, and Tavish doesn't laugh at anything. You'd like him."

They talked for twenty minutes. Pell did most of the talking. The simulators, which she was good at, it turned out — she had a feel for the throat's line, the instructors said; she had the second-best scores in the nine. Abbe Sorrell, the Shoals girl, who had started eating again and had told Pell, at three in the morning, about her mother's ice-farm on Corrie Rock. Sister Quill, who came to the harp hall most nights and sat with Pell under the dome and talked to her about the records, and had looked at the painting for a long time and said it matched something old. The harp itself. *I go and sit with it every night. I can't sleep anyway. I just sit and listen. It's like being inside it already.*

Juno listened, and nodded, and said the things you say, and the slate lay face-down on the desk beside the screen where Pell could not see it, with its list of names in a dead clerk's careful hand.

"You sound strange," Pell said, near the end.

"I'm tired."

"No. Strange. You keep looking at something." Pell peered at the screen as if she could see round its edge. "What's wrong?"

*They chose you,* Juno thought. *The Needle didn't. A man chose you, with a foil in his sleeve, to make our mother vote for a war. And I can't prove it, and if I tell you, you'll either not believe me or you'll believe me, and I don't know which is worse.*

"Nothing's wrong," she said. "The war's wrong. I'm tired. That's all."

Pell looked at her through the screen, with the mouth of the Needle behind her in the common-room window, huge and dark. Then she smiled, the listening smile.

"Thirty-four days," she said. "Then you'll be at the Anchorhold. On escort. You'll be right there when I go in. Sister Quill says the escort ships are close enough to see the skiffs go into the mouth. You'll see me go." Her voice was very gentle. "I'm glad it's you. I'm glad you'll be the one watching."

The link closed. Juno sat in the dark of her cabin until the watch changed.

Then she turned the slate over, and read the list again — all four Draws, all four sleeve names, the four careful lines of who each one had belonged to and why — and then, because she could not help it, she read the last line again, the one about Rhee's brother, and the complaint that half their year had signed, and she put her hand over her mouth.

*It's never you,* the clerk had said. *It's always the one you love.*

She thought of Rhee's message, sent on the old cadet band. *Ask them how the names are chosen.* He had known. Perhaps not this, not the sleeve, not the foils. But he had known something, for eleven years, and he had tried to tell her in the only way he could.

She did not answer him. Not yet. But she took a blank sheet of paper from her desk drawer — paper, not a slate, nothing that could be logged — and wrote on it in pencil the frequency of the old cadet band, and folded it small, and put it in the breast pocket of her uniform, over her heart, where she would know it was there.
