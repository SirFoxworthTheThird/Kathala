## The Penitents' Cells
[
  Place: #The cells
  Characters: @@Juno Asante @@Adaeze Quill @@Danny Marsh @@Nan Hollis
  When: 27 November 2608
]
The message came to the *Vigil* folded inside a ration wrapper, in the hand of a novice of sixteen who had run the length of the Anchorhold's spindle to bring it and stood in the airlock afterwards shaking so hard he could not speak.

*Sister Quill taken to the penitents' cells on the 25th by order of the H.W. Charge: heresy. No visitors. They took her notebooks. Please.*

There was no signature. The novice would not give his name. He went back up the spindle at a run before anyone could stop him, and Juno stood in the airlock with the ration wrapper in her hand and read it twice, and then went to find Nan Hollis.

The Chief was in the engine room with her arms in a coolant manifold to the elbow. She heard Juno out without taking them out.

"The penitents' cells," Hollis said. "The old Concession lock-up. Bottom of the spindle, Needle-ward end, under the docks. Six cells and a guardroom. Built for drunks and strikers." She pulled her arms out and wiped them on a rag. "I did a refit on the life support down there when I was a rating. Twenty years ago. The ventilation's on the same bus as the dock lighting. You kill the dock lighting, you kill the air in the cells, the doors open on a failsafe so nobody suffocates. They've never fixed it. Nobody fixes anything on that station that the Wardens can't see from the chapel."

Juno looked at her.

"You're asking me if I'll do it," said Hollis. "I'm fifty. I've been in this ship six years. I pulled seven of our people out of the starboard plating after the raid in pieces." She threw the rag down. "I'll do it."

They did it on the evening of the twenty-seventh, eight days before the Narrowing, while the High Warden was in Tallow Under at the Compact Hall for the blessing of the four new Keepers — four new names, Juno did not doubt, and one of them from a sleeve. There were five of them: Juno, Hollis, Marsh, and two ratings from the engine room whom Hollis vouched for and who did not ask what they were doing. They went down to the docks in damage-control suits with a work order for a coolant leak that Hollis had written herself. At nineteen hundred Hollis opened a panel under Dock Two and did something inside it with a pair of insulated pliers, and every light on the Anchorhold's docking level went out at once.

In the dark the cell doors sighed open, one after another, down the corridor: six soft pneumatic breaths.

There were two guards. They were Warden lay-brothers, not soldiers, and they had no idea what was happening, and Marsh put one of them on the floor with a hand over his mouth while the rating sat on the other, and Juno went down the dark corridor with her suit lamp on, looking into each open door.

Adaeze was in the fourth.

She was sitting on the bunk with her back against the wall and her knees drawn up, in her grey, with no spectacles. Her face was swollen down one side. Her bandaged hands were in her lap, and the bandages were dirty, and one of them had been unwrapped and wrapped again badly. She looked up into Juno's lamp and blinked, and did not seem surprised.

"Commander," she said. "They told me you'd come. I said you wouldn't be so stupid."

"I'm very stupid," said Juno. "Can you walk?"

"I can walk." She got up, slowly. "They didn't hurt me much. One of the lay-brothers hit me, on the first night. The High Warden was very angry with him afterwards. He doesn't like that sort of thing." She swayed, and Juno caught her. "He came every evening. Sat on the end of the bunk. Talked. He wanted me to understand, he said. He was very — patient."

"Did they find the crystals?"

Something moved in the archivist's swollen face that might have been a smile. "Two," she said. "Not the third."

"Where's the third?"

"Under the Fork," said Adaeze. "Inside the cushion. I unpicked a seam on the night I gave it back to him." She leaned on Juno's arm. "He'd never think to look there. Nobody would. It's the one thing in the Anchorhold nobody touches."

They got her out into the dark corridor. Marsh had the guards tied with their own belts. Hollis was at the far end with the panel open, waiting.

"Commander," said Adaeze. She had stopped, with her hand on the cell door. "There's something else. He told me, in there. On the third night. I think he wanted somebody to know. I think he's wanted somebody to know for a very long time." She was breathing hard. "There's a book. In his cabinet, in the gallery. The black one with the lock. They call it the Inheritance. Every High Warden is given it on the night of investiture, by the one before, and reads it alone, and keeps it. The First Warden wrote it." She held Juno's arm tightly. "He said that when he read it, at forty-one, he went out on the gallery afterwards and stood at the window for the whole night and thought about stepping off. And then in the morning he didn't. And then he was High Warden for twenty-five years." She looked up at Juno in the dark. "He said everyone who's read it has stood at that window."

"Where is he now?"

"At the Hall. Till midnight. The blessing." Adaeze's grip did not loosen. "Commander. If we're going to do this — whatever we're doing — I want to read it. I want it in my hands when I stand in that harp hall. Not his word. Not my traces. *Theirs.*"

Juno looked at the dark corridor, the open cells, the two lay-brothers tied on the floor, Hollis at the panel, Marsh with his sidearm drawn and his face very white. Five hours till midnight. The High Warden's gallery was at the top of the spindle, the far end of the station.

"Hollis," she said. "How long can you keep the docks dark?"

## The Inheritance

The cabinet was black wood, old beyond anything in the Anchorhold but the harp, and its lock was brass, and Nan Hollis looked at it for about four seconds and then opened it with a cutter's bar and a word Adaeze had not heard since she was a child on the Dockside.

Inside, on a shelf, there was a book.

That was all. Not a reliquary, not a casket. A book, bound in black leather gone brown at the corners, the size of a daybook, tied with a cord. Adaeze took it out with her bandaged hands and held it, and the leather was soft with handling, the way the Founders' Tally was soft, the way anything is that has been held by a great many hands for a very long time.

She had already been to the reliquary. They had gone that way, through the harp hall, in the dark, with the harp singing its false drone above them; and she had unlocked the grille with her archivist's key, which they had not thought to take from her, and lifted the Fork from its cushion, and felt inside the unpicked seam, and the third crystal had been there. She had put both in her robe. The Fork lay against her ribs now, cold and heavy. She had not quite meant to take it. She had found that she could not leave it.

"Read it," said Juno. "Quickly. We've got an hour, maybe less."

Adaeze sat down in the High Warden's chair, at the High Warden's desk, in the long low gallery with the mouth hanging outside the window like a painting of nothing, and untied the cord, and opened the book.

The first page said, in a small upright hand that she knew — she had seen it ten days ago at the head of a scorched daybook, *henceforth the harp is to be tuned to the H.T. and not otherwise* — the first page said: *To my successor.*

She read.

She read aloud, because Juno asked her to, and because she found she could not read it silently; her lips moved anyway. Juno stood behind her. Marsh was at the door with his sidearm. Hollis was at the window, watching the docks below, where the lights were still out.

*To my successor. You will have been told by now that the Narrowing of the Needle is a hunger, and that the Keeping feeds it, and that the door stays open because the Reach gives. None of this is true. I want you to know it from me, in my own hand, so that you need never doubt it, and never hope otherwise.*

*The Narrowing is a natural event. The Needle breathes. It has two breaths, a short and a long, and when they fall together it goes deep, and then it comes back. It has always come back. It will always come back. Nothing that is done to it alters this in the least. I had this from the Concession's own reader, who heard it first, before the First Keeping, and proved it, and whom I could not persuade to keep it to himself.*

Adaeze stopped. Behind her, Juno had gone very still.

"Go on," said Juno.

*I instituted the Keeping not to keep the door but to keep the Reach. A people who believe that their survival depends upon a sacrifice, which only one authority knows how to make, will keep that authority. They will keep it through any hardship and any change. They will keep it after everyone who began it is dead. That is what I have made, and what I leave to you: not a rite, but a government. You are its head. You will find it heavy. Carry it.*

*To that end I have taken the Concession's harp records into my own keeping and destroyed them, so that no reader after me can lay the old traces beside the new and see the breath in them. To that end I have ordered the harp tuned to a note of my own choosing, which I have called the Hunger Tone, and which is so chosen that a harp tuned to it can hear the waist narrow and the waist widen, but cannot hear why. To that end I have had the Keepers flown at the hour the waist begins to turn, and not before; and the readers will tell you the hour, and you will tell them it is the hour of the Keeping, and they will believe that it is because it is.*

"Oh," said Adaeze, very quietly, to the page.

*You will be tempted, as I was, to think the Keepers a price too high. Consider what else would hold the Reach together, with the Inward a door away and nobody on this side of it who owes anyone anything. Consider what the alternative is. I considered it for a long time. I believe it is fire. You may believe otherwise. If you do, you may break the covenant, and you will find out.*

*And let the High Warden hold one name in every giving. Not by lot. So that the Reach is reminded, though it never knows it, who keeps it.*

Juno made a sound. Adaeze did not look round.

There was more. Pages of it, in the same small upright hand. Instructions. How to choose readers who would not ask. How to keep the order's books so that the gap at the beginning would look like nothing but a gap. How to manage an Assembly, if there came to be one. How to speak to the families of Keepers who did not come back. It was all very clear and very calm and very well written, and none of it was cruel, exactly; it was simply perfectly certain, all the way through, the way a proof is certain. And at the end of it, on a last leaf folded over and sealed with a disc of grey wax the size of a coin, there was a line on the outside of the fold, in the same hand:

*My name, for my successor's eyes. It is not to be spoken. The order has none but the one I gave it.*

Adaeze put her bandaged thumb on the grey wax.

"Don't," said a voice from the door.

Cassius Mor stood in it. He was in his grey with the silver at the cuffs, and the four lay-brothers behind him in the passage had sidearms, which Adaeze had never in her life seen a Warden carry, and Marsh's own sidearm was up and pointing at the High Warden's chest, and his hand was quite steady.

"Don't break the seal, Sister," said the High Warden. "Not like this. Not in a hurry, in the dark, with a gun at the door." He looked at her, sitting in his chair with the book open in front of her, and his face was very tired and very kind. "I came back early. I had a feeling. When the docks went dark." He looked at the book. "You've read it, then."

"Yes."

"All of it?"

"All but the name."

He nodded slowly. "I stood at that window," he said, "for nine hours, the night I read it. Did you know that? I expect I told you, in the cell. I'm sorry. I talk too much to people I've locked up." He came one step into the room. Marsh's gun followed him. "Adaeze. Listen to me. You think the book is a confession. It isn't. It's a gift. It's the hardest gift anyone has ever been given, and every one of us who has carried it has carried it because the alternative was worse. You've read what the alternative is."

"Fire," said Adaeze.

"Fire." He held out his hand. "Give it to me. And the Fork. And go back to your cell, and on the sixth of December, when the door has opened and the Keepers have come home, we'll talk about what you've read, the two of us, as long as you like. I'll answer every question. I promise you that. I've wanted to, for twenty-five years."

"And Pell?" said Juno.

He turned his head and looked at her for the first time.

"Your sister," said Cassius Mor, "will fly into a door that is already opening, and turn, and come home. As most of them do. As she is very likely to, with the best escort in the Navy." His voice was gentle. "I chose her because your mother needed choosing, Commander. I'm sorry. I'm sorrier than you will ever believe. I chose her also because she is the best of the nine, and because I knew you would be there, and because I thought — I truly thought — that of all of them she was the most likely to come back. I've done that every Draw. Chosen someone who'd come back." He looked at the book. "That's not in there. That's mine."

Juno did not answer. Her face was like stone.

"Give me the book, Sister," said the High Warden, and took one more step.

"Sir — " said Marsh. "Sir, don't — "

One of the lay-brothers in the passage, a boy, nineteen, frightened, who had never held a sidearm before that night and had been told the people in the High Warden's gallery were Shoals agents, saw the High Warden move toward the gun and fired.

He missed the High Warden. He hit Danny Marsh in the chest.

Afterward Adaeze would remember it in pieces. Marsh sitting down suddenly against the door frame with a surprised look. Mor turning, crying out *No* — a real cry, an old man's cry, anguished. The boy in the passage dropping his gun as if it had burned him. Hollis coming across the room from the window like a ship under full burn and taking the High Warden by the collar and the arm and putting him face first onto his own red carpet. Juno on her knees by Marsh with her hands on his chest and his blood coming up between her fingers.

"Captain," said Marsh. He looked puzzled. "Captain, I think — "

"I know. Don't talk. Hollis, *medical* — "

"Galley flour bin," said Marsh, and smiled, a little, as if it were a joke between them, and stopped breathing.

Juno knelt there with her hands on him. Then she took them away and stood up. Her face had not changed at all.

"Bring the book," she said to Adaeze. "Bring the Fork. Hollis. Leave him."

Hollis let go of the High Warden. Mor did not get up. He lay on his face on the red carpet in his grey, where the path was worn to the weft, and he did not get up, and he did not say anything, and as Adaeze went past him with the Inheritance against her chest she saw that his shoulders were shaking.

## Mutiny

Pell's door was on the way.

It was not, really. It was two passages out of the way, on the guest level of the spindle, where the five surviving Keepers and the four new ones were sequestered in the High Warden's own rooms; and the docks were still dark, and the station's alarm had begun, a long slow pulse through every bulkhead, and there were lay-brothers with sidearms somewhere behind them. Juno went anyway. Nobody tried to stop her. Hollis took Adaeze on toward Dock Four with the book and the Fork, and Juno ran.

Her sister opened the door before she knocked. She had heard the alarm. She was dressed, in the plain white of the Keepers, with the bead at her throat, and her hair cut short, and her face very calm.

She looked at Juno's hands.

"Whose is that?"

"Danny's." Juno heard her own voice from a long way off. "Danny Marsh. My XO. A Warden shot him. Ten minutes ago. In the High Warden's gallery." She took a breath. "Pell. Get your coat. You're coming with me. Now."

Pell did not move.

"Pell, listen to me. It's a lie. All of it. The Keeping. It doesn't do anything. It never has. The archivist — Sister Quill — she proved it, she tuned the harp to the Fork and it *breathes*, Pell, it opens by itself, it always has. And the Draw's rigged. Mor chose you. He told me so. To my face. He had your name in his sleeve, he picked you to make Mum vote for the war, and he's got a book in his cabinet that the First Warden wrote, that says all of it, every word — we've got it, Adaeze has it, she's on the way to the *Vigil* with it now. It's a *lie*. You don't have to go. Nobody has to go. Get your coat."

Her sister looked at her. Behind Pell, in the common room, the other Keepers had come to their doors and were standing in them in their white, listening. Abbe Sorrell. The lift-mechanic. Four new faces Juno did not know.

"Is that true?" said Pell.

"Yes."

"All of it?"

"Every word."

Pell was quiet. Then she said, very gently, "Then say it where everyone can hear it."

"What?"

"Say it in the hall. On the Long Night. On the harp. When everyone's listening." Pell's eyes were wet and steady. "Not to me, in a corridor, with blood on your hands, in the middle of an alarm. You're my sister. I'd believe anything you told me in a corridor. That's why I can't." She put her hand on Juno's arm, on the dirty sleeve of the damage-control suit. "If it's true, Juno, it's true for all of us. For Abbe. For Ezra. For every one of the hundred and twelve. Not just for me because I'm the Speaker's daughter and my sister came to get me." She swallowed. "I won't run. If it's a lie, I want to hear it said out loud, to everyone, at once. And then I'll know what to do."

"Pell — "

"Go," said her sister. "They're coming. I can hear them. Go."

Juno went.

The *Vigil* left the Anchorhold at forty minutes past midnight with her docking clamps cut instead of released, which tore four metres of plating off the Anchorhold's Dock Four and was entered in the Compact Navy's log, the next morning, as an act of mutiny. She went out on full burn with her running lights dark, past the *Ardour*, whose captain was a fat frightened decent man called Pryce who had been ordered to stop her and who, for reasons he never afterwards explained to the board of inquiry, did not.

Juno stood on her bridge with Marsh's blood dried brown on her hands and her crew at their stations. She had told them, in the airlock, all of it, in four sentences, and said that the shuttle was there for anyone who wanted it. Six had taken it. Forty-one had not. Hollis was in the engine room. Adaeze was in the wardroom with the book open on the table in front of her and the Fork beside it on a folded napkin, not reading, only sitting with her bandaged hands flat on the pages.

The command channel was screaming. Squadron. Admiral Greaves, his square grey face on the screen, red with fury, ordering her to heave to and surrender her ship. She let it scream for a while. Then she switched it off.

She took from the breast pocket of her uniform a small square of folded paper, soft with handling, and unfolded it, and looked at the frequency written on it in pencil. She did not need to; she had known it by heart for a month. She dialled it in.

"*Callum*," she said, on the old cadet band. "This is the *Vigil*. Juno Asante." She found that her voice was quite steady. "I'm coming over. I've got the archivist, and I've got something for her to read. Seven days, Rhee. On the night of the fifth. I'm going to need you."

There was a long silence on the band. Static. The hiss of the Shoals.

Then his voice, very close, as if he had been sitting with his hand on the set for a month waiting for it. "*Vigil, Callum. I hear you.*" A pause. "*Come home, Juno.*"
