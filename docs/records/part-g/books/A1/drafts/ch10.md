## The Trial
[
  Place: #The Needle
  Characters: @@Teo Kesh @@Corrigan Dace:dead @@Ambrose Lyle:remote @@Hesper Vane:remote
  Items: ^Brannock:lost ^Small Mercy:@Teo Kesh
  When: 2 April 2291

They gave Corrigan a speech, and a band, and a blessing from a Concession chaplain nobody had ever seen before, and a sunburst pin for his collar, and they gave Teo the inner markers.

She had asked for it. Hesper Vane's office had said that the trial would go with one escort to the edge of the shear — a pathfinder, to fly the line ahead of the Brannock as far as the markers, so that Corrigan would not have to read the outer waves and the throat both — and the Concession throat-haulers had all found reasons to be in the yards that day, and Teo had gone up the spindle to the Director's outer office and put her name down in pencil on the form before she could think about it. Nobody had argued. Nobody else had wanted it.

So at eleven in the morning on the second of April she sat in the Small Mercy at the last beacon of the in-lane, with her mains feathering and the Needle filling the canopy, and waited for him.

The mouth had changed since she had last been out here with Ambrose. She could see it from the beacon, before the shear even began: the wall of light at the rim was brighter, tighter, the coils of starlight around it wound so close that they had blurred into a single band of grey-gold fire, and the bruise at the centre was smaller and darker and deeper, like a pupil in a bright room. It looked, she thought, like an eye that was squinting. Like an eye in pain.

"Mercy, Lectern." Ambrose's voice in her ear, very calm. He had asked to read the line himself. "The waist is at thirty-four. Shear is heavy from two to five o'clock, lighter from the left. I'll call you in. Is the Brannock with you?"

"Coming up now."

He came up on her starboard side, the Brannock, ugly and familiar, her new cradle slung under her belly like a pregnancy, packed with two tonnes of the best glass Shaft Nine had ever cut. Teo could see the cradle-frame through the gap in the hull, and the dull blue gleam of the crated glass inside it, and the white release lever painted on the frame where Corrigan would be able to see it through the floor-port of his cockpit. They had made him practise pulling it. He had told her so the night before, laughing, in the Lantern, with Lulu asleep against him. *Thirty times. Like I might forget which way's down.*

"Morning," he said on the private channel.

"Morning."

"Lovely day for it."

"Shut up, Corr."

"I've got a pin," he said. "Did you see? They gave me a pin. I've never had a pin." A pause. "Lulu's on the gallery with Mother Salome. She's got her bolts. She said she'd bring them for luck."

Teo did not trust her voice. She ran the mains up.

"Lectern, Mercy. Brannock's with me. Going in."

They went in.

It was worse than before. She had known it would be. The first shear-waves came at them two kilometres past the beacon and they were not waves, they were walls, coming one on top of the other with no space between, and the Small Mercy bucked and shuddered and screamed in every seam, and twice Teo felt the tow-hook in the stern tear half out of its mount and hang there by a bolt. Ambrose called them. His voice in her ear never changed. *Two o'clock, high, thirty seconds. Left, now, now. Hold her. Big one from behind in fifty, turn into it, Teo, turn into it.* She turned into them. Behind her on her stern camera the Brannock came on in her wake, heavier, slower, lurching under the weight of the cradle, Corrigan following her line so close that she could see the scratch on his canopy where Lulu had once dropped a spanner.

At the inner markers she stopped. Those were the orders. The two dead beacons hung in the fire on either side of her like drowned men, and she brought the Mercy round between them and held her there, nose into the mouth, mains screaming, and the Brannock came up beside her and slowed and hung there too, for a moment, side by side, the way they had hung at a hundred markers in twenty years waiting for Approach to wave them through.

"Well," said Corrigan.

"Corr."

"Don't." He said it quite gently. "I know. Me too." She could hear him breathing. "Look after her."

"I will."

"If."

"If," Teo said. "Just if."

The Brannock went forward.

She watched it go. She would watch it go for the rest of her life; she would see it every time she closed her eyes in the dark for the rest of her life. A small ugly tug with a cradle under its belly, going away from her into the fire. It got smaller. It did not get smaller the way things get smaller when they go away from you; it got smaller *wrong* — stretched, pulled long, thinned to a line of light and then not even that, as the throat took it, until it was a bright scratch on the face of the dark and then a point and then nothing she could see at all.

"I'm in," said Corrigan's voice in her ear. It was faint, and it had begun to waver, rising and falling as if he were speaking from the bottom of a well. "I'm in the throat. Lectern, are you getting me?"

"We have you, Brannock." Ambrose. "You're on the line. Hold your heading. The waist is nine kilometres ahead."

"It's — " A rush of static. "It's not like anything. Teo. Are you there?"

"I'm here."

"It's not like anything. The walls are — it's folded. It's all folded light. Like the inside of a shell. And it goes in. It goes in and in." His voice went away and came back. "There's a — it's narrowing. Up ahead. It's drawing in. Like an iris. Like an eye shutting." A long breath. "And there's a light in the middle of it, Teo. In the middle where it shuts. A light. I've got no name for it."

"Three kilometres," said Ambrose. "Two. Brannock, you're at the waist. Release when you're ready."

"Releasing."

Teo did not hear the release. But she saw it.

The mouth flared. Not the rim — the dark at the centre, the bruised pupil, which had never in her life shown anything but dark; it went white, for an instant, a hard bright white like a cutter's arc, and then a sickly blue, and then dark again, and a shockwave came out of it that hit the Small Mercy like a thrown rock and threw her back on her tail between the two dead beacons and set every alarm on the console screaming at once. Through the channel, through the static, she heard the harp in the Lectern — Ambrose had his line open to it — let out a sound she had never heard an instrument make, a scream, high and thin and long, all its strings at once.

"Brannock," said Ambrose. "Brannock, report."

Static.

"Brannock, Lectern. Corrigan. Report."

"Corr," said Teo. "Corr, talk to me."

Static. And under the static, very faint, faint as something heard through a wall — she would never be sure, afterward, that she had heard it at all — a voice, not saying anything. Breathing. Or laughing. Or something else.

Then nothing.

She waited at the inner markers for three hours. The orders were twenty minutes. At thirty minutes Approach told her to return. At an hour the Director's office told her to return. At two hours Ambrose said, very quietly, on the private channel, *Teo. Come home. Please,* and she switched him off. She sat between the two dead beacons with the Mercy screaming and shuddering under her, the tow-hook hanging by a single bolt, every alarm on the console red, and watched the dark at the centre of the mouth for a small ugly tug with a scratch on its canopy coming back out of it.

At three hours the Mercy's port attitude jet failed, and she had to come home, because if she did not she would go in herself.

## The First Keeper
[
  Place: #The Lectern
  Characters: @@Ambrose Lyle @@Bettany Shore @@Hesper Vane:remote
  Items: ^The Fork:@Ambrose Lyle ^The harp:#The Lectern
  When: same day
]
The waist came up two points at a quarter past two.

Ambrose watched it happen. He had been watching the trace since the Brannock's signal died, sitting at his console with his headphones on and the harp's scream still in his ears like tinnitus, and at a quarter past two the line that had been creeping down all morning — thirty-four, thirty-three and a half, thirty-three — stopped, and hesitated, and came back up. Thirty-four. Thirty-five. Thirty-five and a half. And held.

On the other console Bettany made a small sound.

"It's holding," she said. "Ambrose. It's holding. It's *up*."

He did not answer. He was looking at the clock.

A quarter past two. He had not given Hesper Vane the date of the next pause; he had walked out of her office and down the spindle and sat in the Lectern for six days refusing to think about it. But it was not hard to find. Anyone who could read a trace could find it, from his own short-period figures, which were on the Lectern's own system, in its own logs, in the files he had not thought to lock because they were only the ordinary daily numbers. Anyone he had trained. He looked at Bettany. She was staring at the trace with her face lit up, and she had not yet understood what he was looking at her for, and then she did, and the light went out of her face very slowly, and she looked down at her console.

"She asked me for the short-term projections," Bettany said, very low. "On the twenty-seventh. After you'd been up there. She said you'd said I could do them. She said you were tired."

"Yes," said Ambrose.

"I didn't — she just asked when the next pause was. That's all. I didn't know what it was for. I thought it was for the — for safety. So he'd go in when it was easiest." Her voice cracked. "Was it? Ambrose? Was it for that?"

He could not answer her.

The screens over the consoles flicked to the sunburst. It was not twenty hundred, and it was not the evening broadcast; it was an interruption, all channels, all screens on Anchorage and Hollin, and then Hesper Vane was there, in the Gallery, with the mouth in the window behind her, its bruised centre dark again, and its rim of fire very slightly — you could see it if you knew how to look — less tight than it had been that morning.

She told them the trial had succeeded.

She told them in numbers, as she told them everything. The waist at thirty-three at the moment of release. Thirty-five and a half now. Holding. She said that the Concession's readers had observed, following the release of brine-glass at the waist, a measurable recovery of the throat. She said that this confirmed the Concession's analysis, and that the Keeping would therefore proceed as planned at the deepest point of the Narrowing, on or about the first of May, with as many vessels as could be fitted and crewed. She said that the cost of the trial had been one vessel and one pilot. She said his name.

"Corrigan Dace," said Hesper Vane, "of the Brannock, gave his life this morning so that the door would stay open. His debts, and his daughter's, and his father's, have been struck from the books of the Concern. I have done it myself. I will see his name honoured in the Reach for as long as there is a Reach." She paused. "He is the first Keeper. He will not be the last. But he went first, alone, into the dark, not knowing whether it would answer. It answered. Remember him."

The screens went back to the sunburst.

Ambrose took his headphones off and put them down on the console very carefully, as if they might break.

"Ambrose," said Bettany.

"Go home, Bettany."

"I didn't know. I swear I didn't — "

"I know you didn't. Go home." He did not look at her. "Please."

She went. He heard her crying in the passage outside, and then the lift, and then nothing but the harp. It had stopped screaming. It was singing again, the thin high strained chord of the Narrowing, a hair less strained than that morning, as the waist held at its pause, exactly as the short-period figures had said it would — exactly as it would have held if the Brannock had never left the dock.

He put the headphones back on, and listened to the buoy.

He had been listening to it all morning, under the harp: the buoy at the inner markers, two million kilometres closer, hearing what the harp smoothed away. And when the glass went in, the buoy had heard something the harp had not. He played it back now. The release — the flare, the shockwave, a great white roar in the headphones. And then, after the roar, when the throat's chord came back: a new note in it. Very small. Very high. Hard and bright and steady, like a pin struck against a glass and left ringing. It had not been there before the release. It was there still, six hours later, in the live feed from the buoy, not fading. A small hard ringing at the waist, where nothing had rung before.

*Something stayed,* he thought. *She put glass into it, and something stayed.*

He did not know what it meant. He wrote it down. Then he took a clean sheet of paper from his drawer and wrote at the top of it, carefully, in his small neat hand, *Brannock, at the waist, 2.4.91, last transmission*, and under it, from memory, every word of what Corrigan Dace had said in the throat. *It's all folded light. Like the inside of a shell. It's drawing in. Like an iris. Like an eye shutting. And there's a light in the middle of it. In the middle where it shuts. A light. I've got no name for it.* He read it over. Then he folded it and put it between the pages of the logbook he kept in his drawer, the private one, so that somebody would have it, so that it would be written down somewhere that it had been a man who said it, and not a Keeper.

## Dock Four, Night
[
  Place: #Dock Four
  Characters: @@Teo Kesh @@Ambrose Lyle @@Lulu Dace
  When: same day
]
She was sitting on the dock floor with her back against the Mercy's landing strut and a bottle of Fat Diz's worst between her knees when Ambrose found her, a little after midnight.

Lulu was asleep in the Mercy's cockpit above them, curled up in the pilot's seat under Teo's flight jacket with her bag of bolts in her arms. Mother Salome had brought her down from the gallery that afternoon, after, and stood with her on the dock while the Mercy limped in on one attitude jet with her tow-hook hanging off, and Lulu had walked across the dock to Teo before the engines had even spun down and said, *Where's the Brannock?* — just that, with her bolts in her arms — and Teo had knelt down on the dock and told her. Lulu had not cried. She had listened, and nodded, and asked if she could sit in the Mercy, and had climbed up into the pilot's seat and sat there looking out of the canopy at the empty cradle where the Brannock should have been, until she fell asleep.

Ambrose sat down on the dock floor beside Teo without asking. He did it stiffly, an old man getting down onto steel, and when he was down he sat for a while with his long arms round his knees, looking at the empty cradle.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Everyone's sorry." She drank. "She's sorry. She said so. On all the screens. *Remember him.*" She laughed, a bad sound. "They struck his debts. Lulu's free. She's nine and she's free. Isn't that wonderful."

He did not say anything.

"It worked, though." She turned the bottle in her hands. "Didn't it. I heard the broadcast. The waist came up. He went in and he did it and it came up. He didn't die for nothing. That's something. Isn't it? Tell me that's something."

"It didn't help," said Ambrose.

She went still.

"It didn't help," he said again, looking at the cradle. "The waist would have come up at a quarter past two if he had stayed in the Lantern. There's a short rhythm in it — a pause every few days. I told her about it. She found out when the next one was. She timed the trial to it." He was quiet for a moment. "And there's something else. The buoy heard it. When the glass went in — something stayed. At the waist. A sort of ringing. It's still there. It wasn't there before. I don't know what it is. But the glass didn't — feed anything, Teo. It didn't splint anything. It went in, and it burned, and something stayed, and that's all."

Teo put the bottle down on the dock floor, very carefully, so it would not fall over.

"Then say so," she said.

"To whom?"

"To *everyone*. Get on the screens. Get in the Assembly Hall and stand on the platform and tell them what you just told me. Tell them about the pause. Tell them she timed it. Tell them about your ringing." She was on her knees now, facing him, and she did not remember getting there. "You're the harp man. They'll believe you. They believe your charts, every pilot on the station flies by your charts. Tell them."

"I can't prove it."

"You just — "

"I can't *prove* it, Teo." For the first time his voice cracked. "I have a hypothesis. I have four paper traces and a sum on an envelope and a ringing I can't explain. If I stand up in the Assembly Hall and say that the Director is lying, she will stand up after me and say, in numbers, that the waist came up after the glass went in, and every person in that hall saw it come up. And they'll believe her. Of course they will. She has the numbers. All I've got is *why*." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "And I sail in twenty-six days. I have a berth. Cabin twelve. I have a daughter I haven't seen since she was four. If I stand up in that hall — "

"You'll lose your berth."

"I'll lose my berth," he said, behind his hands. "At least."

Teo looked at him for a long time: an old man in a cardigan, sitting on the floor of a dock in the middle of the night with his hands over his face.

Then she spat. On the dock, between them. She had not known she was going to do it.

"He went in for twenty-six days of your life," she said. "That's all he cost. Twenty-six days and a cabin." She stood up. She was not quite steady. "Go home, harp man. Go home to your daughter. I hope she's worth it."

She climbed up the Mercy's strut into the cockpit, and lifted Lulu out of the pilot's seat in her flight jacket, bolts and all — the girl did not wake, only muttered something and put her arms round Teo's neck — and climbed down again with her, and walked away across the dock toward the Lantern Row lift without looking back.

When she reached the lift and turned round to press the button, he was still sitting where she had left him, on the floor, under the Mercy's broken tow-hook, in the dark.

## Down Tools
[
  Place: #Shaft Nine
  Characters: @@Nico Kesh @@Salome Okafor @@Teo Kesh @@Lulu Dace @@Okoro Brandt
  Items: ^The Tally:@Salome Okafor
  When: 4 April 2291
]
The Keeping glass shipment was due up from Shaft Nine at six in the morning on the fourth, and at five minutes to six every cutter in the shaft put down their tools and sat down.

Nico had been afraid it would be a few. It was not a few. It was all of them, all three shifts, two hundred people sitting down in the galleries and the cage-head and the canteen in the dust, with their cutters lying beside them on the salt, not saying anything. The overseers went up and down shouting. Nobody got up. At a quarter past six the overseers stopped shouting and went up in the cage to report, and at seven Marshal Brandt came down the access road from Tallow with forty Security men in grey and stopped at the shaft-head gate and looked at it.

Nico stood on top of the cage-head housing, where everyone in the yard could see him, with a borrowed megaphone he did not need.

"We'll cut for the Inward," he told Brandt, and the yard, and the forty men in grey. "We'll cut quota. We'll cut what pays the debts. We're not cutting for the dark." He held up his right hand. "Not one more hand for the dark. You heard what happened two days ago. A man flew into the Needle with our glass and didn't come back. That's what it's for. That's what it costs. We're not paying it."

"The Director says the trial worked," said Brandt mildly, from the gate.

"Then let the Director fly the next one."

There was a roar from the yard behind him that went up and up and echoed off the salt walls of the shaft-head, and Brandt waited for it to finish, the way his Director waited for silences, and then nodded, as if Nico had given him a perfectly reasonable answer, and said something to the man beside him, and the forty men in grey spread out along the fence and stood there. They did not come in. They did not go away.

Mother Salome came up the access road at noon, past the men in grey, who let her through, because nobody ever stopped Mother Salome going anywhere. She had the Tally under her arm in its blanket. She had Teo with her, grey-faced, and between them, holding Salome's hand, a small girl in an oversized flight jacket with a bag of bolts over her shoulder.

They came into the yard. Salome set the Tally down on the cage-head step, and unwrapped it, and the cutters who were sitting nearest got up without being asked and came and stood round it.

"He wasn't a shaft man," Salome said. "Corrigan Dace. He was a pilot. He never cut a gram in his life." She looked round at them. "But he died of the glass. Same as the thirty-one at the top of this plate. Same as all of them. The glass killed him and the Concern sent him to it. That makes him ours." She held out the stylus to the girl. "Go on, love."

Lulu took the stylus. She knelt down on the step in front of the steel plate, below the rows of names, and the yard went silent, two hundred cutters and forty men in grey at the fence, and a small girl's hand scratched letters into the steel, slowly, carefully, the way she had been taught at the shaft school: C. DACE. When she had finished she looked at it for a while. Then, as an afterthought, she scratched underneath it, smaller, in a hand that wavered: PILOT.

Not Keeper. Nico noticed. Everyone noticed. Nobody said anything.

Teo, standing behind the girl, looked up across the yard at her brother on the cage-head housing, and their eyes met. Hers were red. She did not look away. After a moment she lifted her hand, and put it flat over the bead at her throat, the way she always did, and held it there; and Nico understood that she had come to tell him something, and that she would tell him later; and that whatever it was, she was not going to tell him to keep his head down any more.
