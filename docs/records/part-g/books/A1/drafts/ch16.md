Bettany brought him the memorandum on the twenty-second, at two in the morning, in the Lectern, with her hands shaking so badly that she dropped the crystal twice before she got it into his palm.

"She gave me her key," she said. "On the twenty-seventh. To file the short-term projections to her channel. She said it would save time. She never asked for it back." She was white to the lips. "I've been sitting on it for three weeks. I didn't — I thought if I didn't look I wouldn't have to know. And then he died. And then you went to the shaft-head and I heard — the guards were talking about it, in the canteen, about the breath — and I thought, I looked her in the face and told her when the pause was, and Corrigan Dace — " She stopped. "So I looked. Tonight. It's all there. All her files. That's the one you want. I think that's the one."

He fed it into his reader. It was the one.

*To the Board of the Ostrander Concern, from H. Vane, Director, Reach Concession. 20 March 2291. Confidential. Subject: Continuity of brine-glass revenue following the Withdrawal: a proposal for governance by covenant.*

He read it through twice. It was nine pages long, and it was very good, and it said exactly what she had told him across her desk, and more, in numbered paragraphs with headings: that the Narrowing was almost certainly natural; that the Concession's senior reader could find no mechanism by which brine-glass could affect it; that this was immaterial. That a population which believes its survival depends on a periodic sacrifice, administered by an authority which alone can read the conditions for it, will maintain that authority indefinitely and at negligible cost. That the sacrifice should be voluntary, honoured, and rewarded, so as to be sought rather than resented. That the authority should be local, permanent, and quasi-religious in character, so as to survive changes of personnel. *Paragraph 14: It is recommended that the undersigned remain in the Reach as its first head.* And at the bottom, in a different typeface, a single line: *Approved by the Board, 1 April 2291. Proceed.*

He took the crystal out of the reader and held it for a while.

"Go to bed, Bettany," he said. "Don't come in tomorrow. Tell them you're ill. Tell them anything." He looked at her. "And thank you."

She went. He sat alone under the dome with the memorandum on its crystal in one hand and the fork in the other, and the buoy's feed in his ears, the small hard ringing at the waist that had begun on the second of April when the Brannock's glass went in and had not stopped since.

He had spent a week on the ringing. He knew what it was now. He had built it, in the end, out of nothing but listening: the note of the thing, its pitch, the way it answered the throat's noise when the noise went over it, how big a thing would have to be to ring like that and where it would have to sit. It was glass. Corrigan's glass. Two tonnes of it, released at the waist on the second of April. It had not burned. It had not dispersed. It had not braced or fed or splinted anything. It had gone, on release, into the one place in the universe where everything was already bent toward it — and it had *stuck*. Settled onto the throat's wall at the narrowest point, the way frost settles on the coldest pane, and stayed there, a little hard crystalline scar, ringing.

So he had done the other sum. The one he had not wanted to do.

Nine ships at every Narrowing, the memorandum said. Two tonnes a ship. Every four thousand days, for as long as the covenant lasted, which was meant to be forever. Each Keeping laying down its scar on the last. He did it carefully, conservatively, three times. It came out the same each time, give or take a few Keepings either way. At the Concession's proposed rate, the throat would begin to fail — truly fail, narrow and not recover — at around the fiftieth Keeping. It would close, for good, at around the sixtieth.

Six hundred and fifty years.

He copied the sum onto the spool beside the swell and the prediction and the sound. He copied the memorandum onto it. Then, at nine in the morning, because he was a fool, because he could not help believing that somewhere under the steady grey eyes there was a person who did arithmetic and would care about this one, he went up to the Gallery for the last time.

She heard him out. She always did.

He told her about the scar. He did not tell her about the memorandum; he was not quite that much of a fool. He laid out the ringing, the pitch, the mass, and then the sum, and he watched her follow it, step by step, as she had followed the swell; and he saw her reach the end of it, and saw her understand it exactly.

"Sixty Keepings," she said.

"Give or take."

"Six hundred and fifty years."

"And then the door shuts, Director. For good. Not because it was starving. Because you choked it. With the glass you said would feed it." He leaned on her desk. "Your Board wants the glass to come through that door forever. That's what this is for. All of it. Well — it won't. Not if you do this. You'll be closing the door you're selling. Slowly. But you'll be closing it."

Hesper Vane was quiet for a long moment.

"Six hundred years, Ambrose," she said at last.

He stared at her.

"Six hundred and fifty, you said. Give or take." She folded her hands. "I'm forty-seven. The Board's average age is seventy-one. The Concern has existed for two hundred and twelve years and has had four names. The Reach has had people in it for fifty." She looked at him almost gently. "In six hundred years there will be no Concern, and no Board, and very possibly no Ardent, and certainly nobody alive who has ever heard of either of us. There will be no one to blame. There will barely be anyone to remember." She shrugged, very slightly. "Whoever is sitting in this room in six hundred years will have to find their own answer. I expect they'll find the same one I have. People usually do."

"They'll be dying," he said. "The people in six hundred years. Because of this. Because of you."

"Everyone in six hundred years will be dying," said Hesper Vane. "That isn't a moral argument, Ambrose. It's a description of time." She held out her hand across the desk, palm up. "Give me the spool."

He did not move.

"You have it on a spool," she said. "Of course you do. The swell, the prediction, the sound. And now this. Give it to me. You can sail on the twenty-eighth with your berth and your fork and your daughter's letters, all of it, I've said so. But the spool stays here."

"No," said Ambrose.

She looked at him for a long moment with her hand still out. Then she drew it back and laid it on the desk, folded over the other, exactly as before.

"Very well," she said. "Thank you for coming up. Go back to the Lectern, Ambrose. Read the throat. Give me the numbers."

He went. At the door he looked back. She had not moved. She was sitting behind the desk with her hands folded, looking at the mouth through the window, and he thought — he would think it all that day, and he would think it at the end — that she looked, for the first time in eleven years, tired.

He did not hear her call Marshal Brandt. But he knew, walking down the long curve of the spindle with the spool in his pocket, that she would.

## The Spool

He sent for her on the twenty-fourth, at midnight, with a note under the door of her bunk-cube in Ambrose's small neat hand: *Lectern. Now. Come the back way, through the archive. Please.* She did not know there was a back way. She found it: a service crawl from the pilots' dorm level up the inside of the spindle that came out behind the steel drawers of the Lectern's archive, among the old paper rolls. He was waiting there. He had not slept; she could see that. He had a spool in his hand.

"Take this," he said.

"What is it?"

"Everything." He put it into her palm and closed her fingers round it with both of his hands. "The swell. The prediction. The sound — you heard the sound. And two other things. Listen to me, Teo. I don't have long."

He told her. The ringing at the waist, that had begun when the Brannock's glass went in. What it was. The sum. *Sixty Keepings. Six hundred and fifty years. And then it closes.* And then, in a lower voice, the memorandum: *governance by covenant*. *Paragraph fourteen.* *Approved by the Board, first of April. Proceed.* The day before Corrigan flew.

She stood among the paper rolls with the spool in her fist and listened, and she did not interrupt, and when he had finished she did not say anything for a long time.

"The Board knew," she said at last.

"The Board approved it."

"So Ardent's no good. Your plan. The *Providence*, the newspapers, the inspectors — "

"Some of them might listen." He did not sound as though he believed it. "Some. The courts, perhaps. But the Board knows, Teo. The Board wrote *proceed*. Whatever I put in front of anyone in Ardent, the Board will have been there first, saying it's a scientist's hobby and has no bearing on policy." He let go of her hand. "So it has to be here. It has to be the Reach. Not Ardent. The pilots. The cutters. The people who'll be asked to fly and cut and give, every eleven years, forever. They have to hear it. All of it. The breath and the scar and the paper with *proceed* on it. And they have to hear it from somewhere she can't talk over."

"Then broadcast it. Tonight. From here. You've got the harp's transmitter, you've got every channel — "

"She's cut it," said Ambrose. "This afternoon. The Lectern's transmitter goes to the Director's channel and nowhere else, as of three o'clock." He smiled slightly. "She's very thorough."

"Then I'll do it. The Mercy's got a set. I'll take it out to the lanes and — "

"The Mercy's impounded. Since this evening. There are four of Brandt's men on Dock Four." He saw her face and put a hand on her arm. "I'm sorry. I checked before I sent for you. It's why I sent for you. Because they'll be coming here next, and I wanted it in somebody's hands who isn't me before they do."

She stared at him. "Coming for you."

"Yes."

"Then *come with me*. Now. The crawl. We'll go down to the Pans, we'll get you to the shaft-head, there's two hundred cutters there who'll — "

"Who'll be broken tomorrow morning," said Ambrose gently. "She told me. Not in so many words. Marshal Brandt has orders to clear the shaft-head at dawn. If I'm there, they'll have a reason to be rough about it." He shook his head. "No. You go. You go now, with that, and you keep it, and you find a way. I don't know what way. I'm a harp man, Teo, I've never in my life known what to *do*." He laughed, a little, and it cracked in the middle. "But you have. I saw you, at the inner markers. You turned into the waves before I'd finished calling them."

Somewhere out in the dome, beyond the archive's steel drawers, the door slid open. They heard it. Footsteps, heavy, several. A voice, mild, unhurried: "Mr Lyle? Marshal Brandt. The Director would like a word."

Ambrose looked at her. He put his hand in his jacket pocket. For a moment she thought he was going to give her the fork as well, and she saw him think it, too — saw his hand close round it in the pocket, and his face change, and the decision go through it.

He did not. He left his hand where it was, round the fork, in his pocket.

"For Clare," he said, very low. "If you ever — no. Never mind. It doesn't matter." He pushed her toward the crawl. "Go. *Go*, Teo."

She went. She went into the crawl on her hands and knees with the spool in her fist, and the panel slid shut behind her, and she lay in the dark in the narrow tube with her face against cold steel and listened.

She heard Ambrose go out of the archive into the dome. She heard him say, quite calmly, "Good evening, Marshal." She heard Brandt say something she could not catch. She heard Ambrose say, "No, I don't think I will," and then a silence; and then, faintly, the sound of the harp. He had struck something, or touched it. The whole dome rang with it, the thin high strained chord of the Narrowing, and under it, so faint she might have imagined it, a single clear note, pure and thin, laid across the chord like a ruler.

Then voices, raised. Then something heavy. Then the harp again, all its strings at once, a long discord, as though somebody had fallen against it.

Then nothing.

She lay in the crawl until her arms stopped shaking. Then she went down through the spindle in the dark, the way she had come, with the spool inside her coveralls against her skin, next to the bead.

The notice went out on every screen at six in the morning on the twenty-fifth, between the throat report and the quota boards, in the same plain type as both.

*The Concession regrets to announce the death of Senior Throat-Reader Ambrose Lyle, who fell from the maintenance gallery of the Lectern late last night while making adjustments to the instrument. Mr Lyle served the Concession for twenty-three years. His work will be continued by Miss B. Shore.*

Teo read it on a screen on the Pans shuttle, halfway down to Hollin, with her helmet on her knees and the spool inside her coveralls. She read it three times. *Fell.* *Making adjustments.* She thought of the harp's long discord in the dark, all its strings at once. She put her hand flat over the place on her chest where the spool lay and did not take it away until the shuttle landed.

She went to the shaft-head because there was nowhere else to go, and because Ambrose had said *at dawn*, and it was dawn.

It was over by the time she got there.

She came up the access road from the Pans at a run and stopped at the top of it, where you could see down into the yard, and stood. The fence was down. The gate was off its hinges. There were Security men everywhere, sixty, eighty, in grey, moving through the yard in twos with their batons, unhurried, the way they did everything; and the cutters were sitting on the salt in long rows with their hands on their heads. The canteen door was broken in. There was smoke coming out of it, not much. On the cage-head step, where Lulu had scratched Corrigan's name, three shapes lay under sheets.

She found Mother Salome by the cage-head, kneeling beside the sheets with the Tally across her knees.

"They came at five," said Salome, without looking up. Her voice was perfectly flat. "Through the fence. A hundred of them. They said we'd killed a Security man in the night. We hadn't. Nobody had been near the fence all night. They said we had." She lifted the edge of the nearest sheet and looked under it and put it down again. "Fennick tried to stop them coming into the canteen. With his one hand. They hit him. He didn't get up. And then the others tried to help him and they hit them." She closed her eyes. "Three. Fennick and the Okonjo boy and old Ruth from the cage."

"Nico," said Teo.

"They took him." Salome opened her eyes. "First. Before anyone. They knew exactly which one he was. They put him in the wagon in chains and Brandt read him a charge off a slate. The murder of a Security officer." She looked at Teo at last. "He'll hang, Teodora. That's what Brandt said. To his face. Under the Concession's articles, a cutter who kills a Security man in a strike hangs. On the Pans. In front of everybody."

Teo sat down on the salt beside her. She did not decide to. Her legs went.

"Ambrose is dead," she said.

"I saw."

"He didn't fall."

"No," said Mother Salome. "I didn't suppose he did."

She held out the stylus. It took Teo a long moment to understand. Then she took it, and Salome tilted the Tally on her knees, and Teo looked down at the plate of old steel, the thirty-one names at the top in Salome's careful capitals, the rows below in a dozen hands, and at the bottom, small and wavering, C. DACE — PILOT.

"He wasn't a shaft man either," said Salome. "Lyle. He was a Concern man. He read their harp for twenty-three years." She looked at the sheets on the cage-head step. "But he came here in the dark and played us the breath. And they killed him for it. That makes him ours."

Teo scratched it. A. LYLE. Her hand was not steady. When she had finished she looked at it for a while, and then, under it, smaller, she scratched: HARP.

Then she sat beside Mother Salome on the salt while the men in grey moved through the yard, and Fennick and the Okonjo boy and old Ruth from the cage lay under their sheets, and somewhere in Tallow her brother sat in chains; and she held the spool through her coveralls with one hand and the bead with the other, and did not cry, and did not know what to do.

## The Offer
[
  Place: #The Director's Gallery
  Characters: @@Teo Kesh @@Hesper Vane @@Okoro Brandt

Brandt came for her on the twenty-seventh. Not with men; alone. He found her in the pilots' dorm, in her bunk-cube, where she had been for two days with the door locked, and he knocked politely and waited, and when she opened the door he said, "The Director would like to see you, Miss Kesh. I'm to bring you up. Will you come, or shall I fetch help?"

She went.

Hesper Vane was standing at the window when Teo came in, and this time she turned round at once.

Teo had never been in the Gallery. She had seen it on the screens behind the Director's head a hundred times — the curved window, the mouth hanging in it like a painting — and it was smaller than she had thought, and emptier, and the rug on the floor was the colour of old blood. Hesper Vane came across it to her and stopped and looked at her, a slight straight woman with grey cropped hair, a head shorter than Teo; and she was holding something in her right hand, turning it slowly in her fingers, the way you might turn a pen while you think.

It was a tuning fork. Brass. Fifteen centimetres long. Its tines worn bright.

Teo looked at it, and something inside her went very cold and very still.

"Mr Lyle's effects," said Hesper Vane, following her look. "He had no family here. They came to me." She turned the fork once more and set it down on the desk, carefully, on a sheet of paper, as if it might mark the wood. "Sit down, Miss Kesh."

"I'll stand."

"As you like." The Director went back behind the desk and sat, and folded her hands. "I'll be brief. Your brother is charged with the murder of a Security officer during an illegal strike. The penalty under the Concession's articles is death. The Marshal has a witness."

"He didn't do it."

"I know he didn't," said Hesper Vane. "The Marshal's witness is lying, and the Marshal knows he is lying, and so do I. That's rather the point." She did not look away. "Your brother will hang on the Pans on the fourth of May. Unless."

Teo waited.

"The Keeping flies on the first," said the Director. "I have eight pilots. I need nine. I would like the ninth to be the pilot who flew the trial escort to the inner markers and held there three hours under fire. The best tug pilot on the station, Mr Lyle said. He was usually right about people." She paused. "Fly the Keeping. Go to the waist with a cradle of glass and release it. And the charge against your brother is withdrawn on the morning of the second, and he walks out of the cells, and his debts are struck from the books, and yours. Every mark. Today. I'll do it in front of you."

"And if I don't come back?"

"Then he walks out anyway, free and clear, and you'll be remembered." A small pause. "The Keepers of the First Keeping will be remembered for a very long time. I've seen to that."

Teo looked at the fork on the desk.

"Did Mr Lyle give you anything?" said Hesper Vane. "Before he died?"

"No."

The grey eyes rested on her for a long moment. Then the Director nodded, very slightly, as she had nodded at Ambrose, as if Teo had given her an answer.

"No," she said. "Well. If he had, it wouldn't matter. I want you to understand that, Miss Kesh. Whatever he might have given anyone — it wouldn't matter at all." She unfolded her hands. "Yes or no."

Teo stood on the blood-coloured rug and thought of Nico in chains on the Pans. She thought of Corrigan going away from her into the fire, getting smaller wrong. She thought of an old man in a cardigan on the dock floor with his hands over his face, and of a single clear note laid across the harp's chord in the dark, and then the discord. She thought of the spool, which was not on her; which was, at this moment, sealed inside the casing of the Small Mercy's flight recorder on Dock Four, under the noses of four of Brandt's men, where she had put it at three in the morning by going in through the tug's belly hatch from the maintenance trench underneath, where no one had thought to look. She thought of the throat. Nine hours to Ardent, in an ordinary week.

"Yes," she said.

"Thank you," said Hesper Vane. She picked up a slate and touched it and turned it round so that Teo could see. *KESH, T. — 40,712 M. — CANCELLED. KESH, N. — 18,900 M. — CANCELLED.* She touched it again and the figures were gone. "There."

Teo looked at the empty screen. Forty thousand seven hundred and twelve marks. She had carried them for twenty years. She felt nothing at all.

The Director held out her hand. In it, on a black cord, there was a bead. A nub of cloudy brine-glass the size of a pea.

"We're giving one to each of the Keepers," she said. "So that you'll carry a piece of it in with you. So that you'll be part of it." Her mouth moved very slightly. "It was my idea. I thought it would be remembered."

Teo looked at the bead in the Director's palm. Then she put her own hand up, slowly, and touched the one at her throat. Nico's. Cut from his first seam when he was sixteen. Lying, as it had lain all her life, a little to the left. A little toward the mouth.

"I've got one," she said.

She took the Director's anyway, because Brandt was standing at the door, and put it in her pocket. Then she turned and walked out across the red rug, past Brandt, and the door slid shut behind her, and she did not look back.

The *Ostrander Providence* sailed at noon on the twenty-eighth.

Teo watched her go from the window of the pilots' dorm: three hundred metres of hull with the company sunburst on her flank, backing out of her dock at the pace a big ship did everything, turning, lining up on the green in-lane, the last of the beacon strings that would be lit for a week. She went into the mouth an hour later. The throat was so narrow by then that the readers had given her a single window of forty minutes, and a throat-hauler's reader aboard, and she went in on it. Teo watched her get smaller. Get smaller wrong. Stretched, pulled long, thinned to a line of light, and gone.

Cabin twelve was empty. Senior grade. Teo did not know how she knew that, but she did.

She stood at the window for a long time after the *Providence* had gone, looking at the mouth: the rim of grey-gold fire wound tight, tighter than she had ever seen it; the bruise at the centre shrunk to a dark pin. Three days. Three days to the bottom. She thought of the spool in the Mercy's flight recorder. She thought, *Nine hours, in an ordinary week.* She thought, *Nobody has ever flown it in a Narrowing. Nobody has ever been to the waist and come back. Nobody has ever gone through.*

She put her hand over Nico's bead, and held it, and felt it lean, very gently, insistently, against her palm. Toward the mouth.
