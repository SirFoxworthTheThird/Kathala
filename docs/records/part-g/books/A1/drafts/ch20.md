They came out of the shear at three in the morning on the fifth of December, the *Vigil* and nineteen ships of the Free League, riding the waves down off the rim of the mouth the way the raiders had a month before, with a League reader in the *Callum* calling the line on the open band so that every ship could hear it.

Juno had never flown the shear. She flew it now. She stood on the *Vigil*'s bridge with her hands on the back of the helm's chair, and Linnet at the helm turned into each wave as the reader called it, and the corvette shuddered and bucked and came down off the fire into the grey-gold dark with the Anchorhold ahead of her and nineteen Shoals ships strung out behind; and Juno found that she was thinking, absurdly, of Pell at eight, in the harp hall, standing in front of the harp for an hour until they had to carry her out.

"Home Squadron," said the tactical rating who had Marsh's chair. His voice was steady. "Bearing one-eight-zero, coming up from Tallow. The *Constant*, the *Resolute*, the *Ardour*, six frigates. Admiral's flag on the *Constant*."

"They knew we'd come," said Juno.

"Everyone knew we'd come, Captain."

On the main display the Anchorhold hung against the mouth, its broken wheel still slewed against its spindle where the dead wheel had struck it, its docks lit. And beyond it, at the edge of the shear, at the mouth itself, a line of small lights. Nine. The Keepers' skiffs, in their cradles on the Keeping barge, waiting for the hour.

The command channel opened without her touching it. Greaves.

"*Asante.*" His square grey face, very tired. "*Asante, I'm going to say this once. You are a mutineer in time of war in company with the enemy. The Keeping flies in four hours. If you or any ship with you comes within ten kilometres of the Keeping barge I will fire on you. I don't want to. I've known you since you were a cadet. But I will.*"

"Understood, Admiral," said Juno. "We're not going near the barge."

He frowned. "*Then what the hell are you doing here?*"

"Holding a door," said Juno, and closed the channel.

The plan was very simple, the way the plans you make with nothing are always simple. The League's nineteen would come in between the Anchorhold and the Home Squadron and hold there, and not fire unless fired on, for as long as it took. The *Vigil* would dock at the Anchorhold — at Dock Four, which was still torn open where she had left it, and which nobody would be guarding, because nobody would think anyone would try to dock there — and put Adaeze aboard with Hollis and six volunteers. Adaeze would go up the spindle to the harp hall. And at the hour, when the Keepers went in and the whole Reach was listening to the harp on every channel, she would tune it true, and read.

"Thirty minutes," Juno had said to her, in the wardroom, the night before. "That's what Rhee thinks he can hold. Maybe less. Thirty minutes from when you start."

"It'll take me twenty to tune it," Adaeze had said. "If my hands hold." She had looked down at them, still bandaged. "I'll read while I tune. I'll start reading as soon as I'm in the hall. They'll hear it go true while I read. That's better, I think. They'll hear the difference." She had put her hand on the book, which had not left her for seven days. "And the seal. I'll break the seal at the end. On the air."

"You haven't read it."

"No." Adaeze had looked at the grey disc of wax for a long time. "I want to read it once. With everyone."

The Home Squadron came on. The League's nineteen spread out across its path in a long thin line, between it and the station, and stopped, and hung there. Juno watched it on the display. She saw the *Callum* at the line's centre, her forward guns still dark from the raid, her running lights lit. She saw the squadron's frigates slow. She saw, for a long held minute, nobody fire.

"*Juno*," said Rhee's voice on the cadet band. Very calm. "*I've got them. For now. Go.*"

"Linnet," she said. "Dock Four."

## Broadcast

The harp hall was full when Adaeze came into it, and singing.

She had forgotten that it would be. She had thought of it, all the way up the spindle with Hollis and the six beside her, as she had always known it at night: empty, cold, the harp alone under the dome. But it was the Long Night. Every Warden in the Anchorhold was here, a hundred of them, in their grey, in rows on the worn floor before the harp, and the novices along the walls, and the readers at their consoles, and they were singing the Hunger Tone, all of them, held and held, so that the whole dome hummed with it; and over their heads the mouth hung huge and dark with its rim of fire wound tight, and in the middle of the floor, before the harp, stood the High Warden.

The broadcast lamp above the reader's console was lit. Red. Every channel in the Reach.

Nobody stopped her. That was the thing she would have remembered, if there had been time to remember anything. She came through the iron door in her grey with her bandaged hands and the black book against her chest, and the singing faltered, near the door, and then all the way along the rows, voice by voice, until it died; and a hundred Wardens turned and looked at her, the archivist, who had been taken for heresy, who everyone said had stolen the Inheritance and fled to the Shoals; and nobody moved.

Except the High Warden's guard-captain, who put his hand on his sidearm.

"No," said Cassius Mor.

He did not raise his voice. He did not need to. The guard-captain took his hand away.

Mor stood in the middle of the floor in his grey with the silver at the cuffs, very straight, and looked at her across the hall. He looked, she thought, as if he had not slept since the night in the gallery. He looked a hundred years old.

"Sister," he said.

"High Warden."

"You've come to read it."

"Yes."

He looked at the book against her chest. Then at the broadcast lamp, red, over the console. Then up through the dome at the mouth. For a long moment he did not say anything at all, and the hall was so silent that she could hear the harp's drone, the false one, the Hunger Tone, going out over every channel in the Reach into every house.

"She wrote that the alternative was fire," said Cassius Mor, very quietly. "I've believed that for twenty-five years. I've chosen a child in every Draw because I believed it." He looked at her. "I don't know if I believe it any more. I haven't known since the boy in my doorway." He stepped aside. "Read it, Adaeze. Let them choose."

She went past him to the harp.

She took the Fork out of her robe. A sound went through the hall — a hundred people drawing breath — and she did not look round. She struck it against her bandaged palm. It hurt. The note went up into the dome, thin and pure, and met the Hunger Tone, and fought it. *Wah. Wah. Wah.* Over every channel in the Reach.

She knelt at the base of the frame and began to tune.

And she began to read.

She did not remember, afterward, the order she did it in — there was no afterward, as it turned out, or not much of one — but the people who heard it remembered, for the rest of their lives. They remembered the archivist's voice, low, steady, a little hoarse, reading from a book into the open broadcast in the order's old formal cadence, *To my successor*; and under the voice, the harp, changing. Course by course. Peg by peg. The long false drone of the Hunger Tone, which every one of them had heard every four thousand days of their lives and sung in the Hall since they could stand, beginning to shift, and slide, and fight itself, and then — wire by wire, as she read — to lie down into something else. Something wider. Something that breathed.

*The Narrowing is a natural event. The Needle breathes.*

In Tallow Under, in the light-wells, in a thousand houses with the Long Night on, people stopped singing.

*I instituted the Keeping not to keep the door but to keep the Reach.*

In the Shoals, in the habs, in the ice-farms on Corrie Rock, people turned their receivers up.

*I have ordered the harp tuned to a note of my own choosing, which I have called the Hunger Tone, and which is so chosen that a harp tuned to it can hear the waist narrow and the waist widen, but cannot hear why.*

On the bridge of the *Constant*, at the head of the Home Squadron, Admiral Tobiah Greaves sat in his command chair with his hand over his mouth.

*And let the High Warden hold one name in every giving. Not by lot.*

At the edge of the mouth, in nine small skiffs in their cradles on the Keeping barge, nine Keepers sat with their hands on their yokes and their beads at their throats and listened.

It took her twenty-two minutes to tune the nine courses and read the book. Her hands bled through the bandages by the seventh course. She did not stop. At the end of it the harp was singing its true chord over every channel in the Reach — the great slow breathing chord, the long tide under the Narrowing, turning; anyone could hear it; a child could hear it — and she had read every page of the Testament but the last, and she sat back on her heels on the floor of the harp hall in front of the harp, with the book on her knees, and the last leaf folded and sealed in grey wax, and put her thumb on the seal.

"*My name,*" she read aloud, from the outside of the fold, "*for my successor's eyes. It is not to be spoken. The order has none but the one I gave it.*"

She broke the seal.

The guard-captain had been watching the High Warden for twenty-two minutes. He had watched the High Warden stand aside, and stand still, and say nothing, while the archivist knelt at the First Warden's harp and unmade it, wire by wire, over every channel in the Reach. He had watched a hundred Wardens on their knees on the floor, some of them weeping, some of them with their hands over their ears. He was a simple man, the guard-captain, and a devout one, and he had been told since he was a boy that the name of the First Warden was not to be spoken. When she broke the seal, he drew his sidearm and shot her.

He shot her in the back. She fell forward over the book.

The hall screamed.

Hollis was across the floor before the echo died, and the six behind her, and the guard-captain went down under them; and Cassius Mor was on his knees beside the archivist, with his hands on her, turning her over, saying her name. *Adaeze. Adaeze.* There was a great deal of blood. Her spectacles had come off. She looked up at him, puzzled, the way Danny Marsh had looked up at Juno in the gallery doorway, and then past him, at the harp, singing its true chord above her into the dome.

"It breathes," she said. "Can you hear it?"

"I can hear it."

"I want to read it," she said. "The name. I want to read it with everyone."

He looked at the leaf, open now, on the floor beside her, in a spreading pool. He picked it up. His hands were shaking. He held it where she could see it, and she looked at it, and her lips moved; and she could not; and so the High Warden of the Throat, on his knees on the floor of the harp hall with the broadcast lamp burning red above him, read it aloud for her, into every house in the Reach.

"*My name is Hesper Vane,*" read Cassius Mor. "*I was Director of the Reach Concession of the Ostrander Concern, and on the second of May, twenty-two ninety-one, I made myself First Warden of the Throat. The fork on the cushion in the Lectern was not mine. It belonged to Ambrose Lyle, the Concession's reader, who heard the breath first, and proved it, and died for it in the Lectern at the hand of my Marshal, on my order, on the twenty-fourth of April. It is engraved for his daughter Clare. I have let the order read it otherwise. I find I would rather not be thanked for it.*"

He stopped. He read the last line to himself, and then aloud.

"*If you are reading this, you have kept it. I am sorry. I did not think anyone would mind for six hundred years.*"

Adaeze Quill was looking up at the harp. She did not hear the end. Her face, under the dome, under the true chord, was quite peaceful.

Mor knelt beside her for a long time with the leaf in his hand.

Then he got up. He laid the leaf on the open book on the floor beside her, and took the Fork out of her bloody hand, gently, and laid it on the floor of the harp hall in front of the harp, on the worn stone, not on its cushion. And he went to the iron door at the side of the hall that led into what was left of the archive — the racks, the crystal, the singed daybooks and the bound volumes Adaeze had carried out of the fire a month ago and carried back in again — and he stood in it, and looked back once at the hundred Wardens on the floor of the hall, and at the red broadcast lamp, still burning.

"She wrote that the alternative was fire," said Cassius Mor, to all of them, to every house in the Reach. "Let's see."

He went into the archive and closed the iron door behind him. They heard him bar it from the inside.

The smoke came under the door eleven minutes later. By then nobody in the harp hall was trying to open it.

## The Mouth

Juno heard it from the mouth.

She had put Adaeze and Hollis and the six aboard at Dock Four in four minutes, under the torn plating, with nobody watching, as they had planned; and then she had not done what she had planned, which was to hold the *Vigil* off the dock and guard it. She had taken the *Vigil* to the mouth. To the Keeping barge, at the edge of the shear, where nine small skiffs sat in their cradles with their engines warm, waiting for the hour, and the *Ardour* sat beside them as their escort with her forward guns out. She had not been able not to. She had stood on her bridge and listened to Adaeze's voice begin, *To my successor*, and to the harp begin to change under it; and she had said *Linnet, take us to the barge,* and Linnet had.

Greaves had said he would fire on any ship within ten kilometres of the barge. He did not fire. She did not know if he had heard her coming. She thought perhaps he had, and was listening too.

The *Vigil* came to rest four hundred metres off the barge, between it and the *Ardour*, and hung there.

On the open channel the harp was singing its true chord. You could hear it in every speaker on the bridge, in every cabin, in the passages; the crew had stopped where they stood, all through the ship, and were listening. Juno stood on the bridge with her hands on the back of the helm's chair and listened to the Needle breathe, and to the archivist's voice reading over it, *I have had the Keepers flown at the hour the waist begins to turn*, and thought that she had never in her life heard anything so beautiful or so terrible, and that Pell was hearing it, four hundred metres away, in a skiff with a cradle of glass under her and a bead at her throat.

She heard the shot.

It came over the open channel, small and flat. A scream, a hundred voices. A man's voice saying a name. Then, after a long time — very long — the man's voice again, an old man's voice, shaking, reading.

*My name is Hesper Vane.*

She did not know the name. Nobody in the Reach knew the name. It meant nothing. It was a name from before the Compact, from the Blank Year, a Concession name, a company name. And then the voice went on — *the fork on the cushion was not mine; it belonged to Ambrose Lyle, the Concession's reader, who heard the breath first* — and Juno found that she had sat down, without meaning to, on the edge of the tactical console, with her hand over her mouth, the way she had seen the Admiral sit on the screen.

*It is engraved for his daughter Clare. I have let the order read it otherwise.*

FOR CL.

Not *for clarity*. *For Clare.*

"Captain," said the rating at tactical, very quietly. "Captain, the readers are calling the hour. Waist's turning. On the Wardens' channel. They're calling the Keepers in."

She stood up.

On the display the nine skiffs on the barge were lighting their engines, one after another, the way they had been trained to, at the call. The line, the waist, the lever, the turn. The Wardens' readers in the Lectern had called it, as they had called it at every Keeping for three hundred years, at the hour the waist began to turn; and some of them had called it weeping, and one had called it and then begun, on the open channel, to laugh, horribly, and been cut off. And the skiffs were lifting out of their cradles, one by one, and turning their noses to the mouth.

"Get me the barge channel," Juno said. "The Keepers' channel. All nine."

"Captain, I don't know if — "

"*Get it.*"

He got it. She stood on her bridge and looked at the nine small lights against the fire at the rim of the mouth, and opened her mouth to speak, and did not know what she was going to say.

"*Juno,*" said Pell's voice, before she could say anything.

It was very calm. It came over the Keepers' channel, small and close, from the third skiff in the line.

"*Juno, are you there? Is that the* Vigil?"

"I'm here."

"*I can see you.*" A pause. "*I heard it. All of it. The harp.*" Another pause, longer. "*It's the sound, Juno. That I painted. When I was eight. That's it. That's what I heard. I always thought it was a hunger. I thought it was asking.*" Her voice shook for the first time. "*It isn't asking for anything. It's just breathing.*"

"I know," said Juno. "Pell. I know."

"*Sister Quill's dead, isn't she.*"

"I think so. Yes."

On the display the skiffs were moving. Slowly. Out from the barge, toward the mouth, toward the shear, one after another, in their line. The first. The second. Pell's, the third, its engine lit, drifting forward. On the *Ardour*, four hundred metres beyond them, the forward guns were out, and Juno knew, the way you know a thing you have known all your life without being told it, what the *Ardour*'s standing orders for the Keeping escort were. What every escort's had always been. *A Keeper who turns back with the glass is to be prevented.*

"*Juno,*" said Pell. "*What do I do?*"

And Juno, who had been trying to find words for this moment since a slip of steel foil came out of a brass drum in the Compact Hall fifty-eight days ago, found that she had none at all. That there were no words. That she had come four hundred metres from the barge to stand between her sister and a gun, and there was nothing to say.

"Whatever you want," she said. "Pell. Whatever you want. It's yours. It's always been yours."

There was silence on the channel.

Then, in the line of skiffs at the edge of the shear, the third light turned round.

It did it slowly, the way they had been trained not to, laboriously, a narrow little ship pulling its nose round against the pull of the mouth, until it was facing out. Facing the barge, and the *Vigil*, and the *Ardour*'s guns. And it stopped there.

"*I don't want to,*" said Pell Asante on the Keepers' channel, very clearly, so that the barge and the escort and the *Vigil* and the *Ardour* could all hear it. "*I'm sorry. I don't want to.*"

Juno did not breathe.

On the *Ardour*, four hundred metres away, the forward guns moved. She saw them move. She saw them come round, the long barrels swinging slowly on their mounts toward the small turned skiff at the edge of the shear, and stop, and hold, and she put her hand on the back of the helm's chair and said, very quietly, "Linnet," and Linnet put the *Vigil* forward, in between.

The *Ardour*'s guns held on the *Vigil*'s flank for a long moment.

Then, very slowly, they swung back, and went in.

"*Vigil, Ardour,*" said a voice on the escort channel. A fat, frightened, decent man called Pryce, whom Juno had known for years. "*I'm not doing it, Asante. You can tell the board of inquiry. I'm not doing it. Not after that. Not after — *" His voice cracked. "*Let them come home. Let them all come home.*"

On the display, at the edge of the shear, a fourth light turned. Abbe Sorrell. Then a fifth. Then a sixth.

Three did not. Three skiffs went on, out into the shear and down toward the dark at the centre of the mouth, in their line, the way they had been trained: the lift-mechanic and two of the new names, drawn eight days ago from the Wheel, believers. Juno watched them go. She watched them get smaller wrong. She did not try to stop them. She did not know how she would have; and she thought, watching, that they had the same right to it as Pell had to turn, and that for three hundred years nobody in the Reach had had either.

The sixth skiff came in alongside the *Vigil*, and then the fifth and the fourth, and last of all the third, very slowly, as if its pilot were not quite sure how to fly it in this direction. It came to rest so close to the corvette's flank that Juno, at the bridge's side port, could see through the two canopies her sister's face. Pell had taken off her helmet. Her hair was cut short. She was crying, and she was looking at the bead at her own throat, lying on its cord against her white Keeper's shirt, quite still.

On the open channel the harp went on breathing, over every house in the Reach, under a red broadcast lamp in a hall where nobody was singing any more, beside an iron door with smoke coming out from under it.

## The Founders' Tally
[
  Place: #The Assembly Hall
  Characters: @@Juno Asante @@Pell Asante @@Efua Asante
  Items: ^The Tally:#The Assembly Hall
  Facts: ?Teo flew to cross, not to keep:@Juno Asante ?Teo flew to cross, not to keep:@Pell Asante

The door opened at thirty-one minutes past seven on the morning of the sixth of December, Anchorhold time, exactly as the archivist had said on the air that it would, two hours after the last three Keepers went in. One of them came out. The other two did not.

By noon the Reach was arguing about it.

It argued in the Assembly, which met in emergency session and adjourned in disorder and met again. It argued on every channel. It argued in the light-wells of Tallow Under and the habs of the Shoals and on the docks, and in the Compact Hall, where the Wardens who were left — there were sixty-odd of them now, without a High Warden, with their archive burned to the bare steel and their harp tuned to a note that half of them refused to sing — stood on their bench beneath the Founders' Tally and said that three had gone in, and the door had opened, and that the covenant was kept. And the other half stood beside them and said nothing. And the Hall said many things.

*It breathes,* some of them said. *You heard it. Anyone could hear it.*

*It opened because three went in,* said others. *It has always opened because they went in. Three were enough. The faithful are always enough.*

*There's no record,* said a great many. *There's no archive. The High Warden burned it. There's nothing left but a broadcast and an old woman's voice and a name nobody's ever heard of. Who was Hesper Vane? Who was Ambrose Lyle? Where's the proof?*

Juno heard it all from the *Vigil*'s bridge, at anchor off Corrie Rock under the League's flag, with a Navy warrant for her arrest on every channel and Admiral Greaves, in a long private message that she did not reply to, asking her as a personal favour not to come back to Tallow Under for a year or two. She heard it with her sister sitting on the floor of her day cabin, in her white Keeper's shirt, painting. Pell had not stopped painting since they brought her aboard. She painted the harp hall from memory. She painted it over and over: the dome, the mouth, the gold frame of the harp, and on the floor before it, very small, a figure in grey.

On the ninth, they went to Tallow Under anyway.

They went at night, in a League shuttle with its transponder off, the two of them, with Rhee at the controls saying nothing at all. Their mother met them at the back of the Compact Hall, at the low end under the gallery, where the ceiling came down and the original salt wall showed through with the marks of the cutting machines on it in long curved grooves like the inside of a shell. She had the keys. She was still the Speaker, for the moment. She did not ask why they had come. She embraced Pell for a long time without speaking, and then Juno, and then she let them in and stood back by the door and waited.

The Hall was empty and dark. Caul was down. Only the lamps along the Wardens' bench were lit, low, and in their light, on the original salt wall behind the bench, in its frame of polished brass far too fine for it, the Founders' Tally.

Juno went up to it. She had not touched it since her Naming Day.

The steel was cold. Three hundred years of hands had worn it smooth, as she remembered; at the top the names were gone entirely, only a faint unevenness, like the surface of water. She ran her fingers down it, slowly, row by row. The scratches deepened as she went down. Lower on the plate, where fewer hands had reached, they were sharper — still worn, still soft at their edges, but there. Shallow grooves under her fingertips, like braille in a language nobody spoke any more.

"Pell," she said. "Bring a lamp."

Pell brought one from the bench. Juno held it close to the steel, low, so the light lay across the plate sideways and every scratch threw a shadow.

At the very bottom of the plate, below everything, where the steel was least worn because it was hardest to reach — where you would have to kneel to touch it, and nobody had knelt in three hundred years — there were four lines. They were not like the others. The others above were in a dozen hands, deep and careful, rows of them. These four were small and scratched and uneven, as if made by a child and by people in a hurry; and they had a second word after each name, which none of the rows above had.

The first was in a hand that wavered, like a child's.

C. DACE — PILOT

Under it, in a different hand, harder, quicker:

A. LYLE — HARP

Juno's breath stopped.

Under that, in a third hand, steady and deep:

T. KESH — PILOT

And under all three, very small, almost too small to read, in a fourth hand — small and careful and upright, nothing like the others — as if it had been added later, by somebody who had come back to the plate alone:

FOR CLARE

Juno knelt on the stone floor of the Compact Hall in front of the Founders' Tally with the lamp in her hand. Pell knelt beside her. Neither of them said anything for a long time.

"Lyle," said Pell finally. "The reader. The one she said. Whose fork it was."

"Yes."

"And *pilot*." Pell put her fingertip, very gently, on the last name but one. "They didn't put *Keeper*. Whoever scratched these. They had the word. Everyone above them had it, all the way up, the old Wardens say — *Keeper*, *Keeper*, *Keeper*. And they put *pilot*." She looked at Juno. "Why would you do that? Why would you scratch somebody's name on the Founders' Tally and not call them a Keeper?"

"Because they weren't," said Juno.

She did not know how she knew. She knew. She knelt with her fingers on the three short words in their three different hands, at the bottom of the oldest thing in the Reach, where nobody had knelt to read them in three hundred years, and she knew that somebody had known. In the year of the First Keeping. The year of the Withdrawal. In the Blank Year, before the records began. Somebody had known that it breathed, and had knelt here, or somewhere, and scratched it into steel the only way they could, in one word, three times, so that it would be there for anyone who ever knelt down far enough to read it.

*Pilot. Harp. Pilot.*

*Not Keeper.*

Behind them, at the low end of the Hall, under the gallery, their mother stood by the door in the dark with her hands folded, waiting, and did not come closer.

Pell took a stylus out of the pocket of her coat. Juno did not know where she had got it; from the bench, perhaps, where the Wardens kept them for the Naming Day children. Her sister looked at the plate for a long moment. Then she leaned in, below the four small lines, where there was a little bare steel left at the very edge, and scratched, slowly and very carefully, in a painter's steady hand:

A. QUILL — ARCHIVIST

She sat back on her heels and looked at it.

"She'd have wanted it right," Pell said. "The word. She'd have checked."

Juno put her arm round her. They knelt together on the stone in front of the old steel plate with the lamp between them, the Speaker's daughters, a mutineer and a Keeper who had turned round, in the dark of the Compact Hall, while all through the Reach above and around them people argued and wept and sang and stopped singing; and Juno thought of the Needle, two million kilometres away, open, breathing, as it always had and always would whatever anyone sang to it; and she did not know what would happen to the Reach now. Nobody did. That was the thing that had changed. For the first time in three hundred and seventeen years, nobody knew.

She found that she could bear it.
