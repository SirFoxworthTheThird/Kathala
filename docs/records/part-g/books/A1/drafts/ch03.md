## The Ossuary
[
  Place: #The Ossuary
  Characters: @@Isra Venn @@Ostap Pike
  Items: ^Gristle:@Isra Venn
  When: 24 April 2937
]
The new one had come out of the mouth sometime in the night, and by the time Isra Venn got the Gristle out to it there were already scratches on its hull where somebody else's grapple had tried and slipped.

"Coopers," said Pike, at the back of the cockpit, looking at the marks. "Or the Mallon girls. Mallon girls, I'd say. They always come in too fast."

"They didn't get it."

"They didn't get it," Pike agreed, and coughed, a long wet rattle that went on for some time, and spat into the rag he kept for it, and folded the rag away. "You want me on the arm?"

"I want you on the arm."

The Ossuary lay in the lee of the Needle's mouth, two hours out from the Pans at the Gristle's best speed, which was not a speed anybody would boast about. Nobody had planned it. Things that came out of the mouth came out slow, tumbling, and the mouth's own pull caught them and held them there in a long loose drift a few thousand kilometres across, like leaves caught in an eddy at the edge of a stream. Forty-odd hulls, the last time Isra had counted. More every month. They hung in the grey light at odd angles to each other, dark and still, some of them turning very slowly, all of them furred over with the pale stuff that made them worth the trip — glass, throat-grown glass, the best there was, crusted on the old metal as thick as a hand. You could live a season on what you could cut off one Return. You could live a year, if you were careful and the Lantern did not take it.

Behind them, filling the whole of the canopy's upper arc, the Needle hung.

Isra did not look at it much. Hardly anybody did, now. When her grandmother was a girl it had been a bright thing, Hanne said — a ring of light, the Eye, ships going in and out of it like bees at a hive. Isra had never seen a ship go into it. She had never seen the Eye. What hung over the Ossuary now was a dull bruise, a wound in the sky the colour of old blood, with a sick flicker at its rim that came and went like a lamp on a bad circuit. Sometimes you would catch it out of the corner of your eye, flickering, and feel your stomach turn over. That was all. It did not go anywhere. Nothing went through it. It was where things came out.

"Coming round," she said, and brought the Gristle in under the new one's flank.

It was a skiff. A Keeper's skiff, the narrow kind, a single seat and a cargo cradle and an engine and nothing else — the kind they had flown in the Keepings before the Quiet, Hanne said, though nobody alive had seen one flown. Twelve metres nose to tail. It had come out of the mouth tail-first and was turning end over end, very slowly, once every four minutes or so, and every time it came round the grey light slid across its hull and lit up the glass on it, and Isra caught her breath, which she did not often do.

She had seen a lot of Returns. She had never seen glass like this. It did not lie on the hull in a crust. It *grew* from it, out and out, in long branching fronds and plates and needles, as if the skiff had lain for a hundred years at the bottom of some sea that made glass the way the sea on Old Earth was supposed to have made coral. It was clear as water at the tips and milky at the root, and all of it, every frond, every needle, leaned the same way, swept back along the hull from the nose like hair in a wind.

"Look at that," said Pike softly.

"I'm looking."

"It's grown backwards."

"It's grown out. That's all." But she was looking at it the way he was. "Get the arm on her. Midships, behind the cradle, where it's thin."

The Gristle's grapple arm was older than the Gristle and had been Pike's before it was hers, and he worked it the way a surgeon works, slow and exact, his big ruined hands quiet on the controls. The claw went out on its cable and closed on the skiff's flank where the glass was thinnest, and bit, and held, and Pike let out a long breath that became a cough. They matched spin. It took twenty minutes. Then Isra put on her suit and went over.

Inside the glass, the skiff was cold in the way only things from the mouth were cold, a cold that came through the gloves and into the bones of your hands. The canopy was frosted white. She knocked a window through it with the butt of her cutter and put her helmet lamp to the hole and looked in.

He was still in his seat. They always were. Strapped in, hands on the yoke, head a little forward as if he had been leaning to see something ahead of him. He was young — younger than her, she thought, though it was hard to tell with the frost on his face; a boy with a soft jaw and a lot of dark hair. His eyes were open. They were always open. At his throat, on a cord gone black, there was a small cloudy bead of glass.

Isra looked at him for a while. Then she reached into the thigh pocket of her suit and took out one of the strips of grey cloth she kept there, and reached in through the hole in the canopy, and laid it over his face, and tucked the ends in under the edge of his helmet ring so it would stay.

"Isra," said Pike in her ear.

"I know."

"Clock's running."

"I *know*, Pike."

She cut for two hours. The cutter's blade went into the glass with a high clean whine that she could feel through her feet on the hull, and the fronds came away in long crystalline pieces that she bagged and clipped to the cable for Pike to reel in. It was good glass. It was very good glass, dense and sweet and clean, the kind that would make a Pans buyer go quiet and stop haggling. She took the fronds off the starboard side and the long plates off the cradle and started on the nose, where the growth was thickest, and somewhere in the second hour she stopped thinking and only cut, the way you do.

That was why she nearly missed it.

She had her lamp down in the root of the growth at the nose, where the glass met the metal, and the light went into the glass and came back to her strangely — not reflected but bent, sent off sideways into the crystal and back out again somewhere else, so that for a second there seemed to be a lamp shining up at her from inside the hull. She stopped cutting. She put her helmet close.

The glass at the root was not milky. It was layered. Fine bands of it, clear and cloudy, clear and cloudy, packed thin as pages, all the way down to the metal, so that looking into it was like looking down into the side of a cut cliff. And the bands did not lie flat to the hull. They rose up from it, every one of them, in a long sweep, like ripples frozen on water. Like something that had been pushed.

"Pike," she said.

"What?"

She did not know what. She cut a piece of the root out — a fist-sized chunk with the bands in it — and bagged it, and clipped it to her own belt rather than the cable.

## The Lantern Comes
[
  Place: #The Ossuary
  Characters: @@Isra Venn @@Ostap Pike @@Deacon Rusk @@Mercy Oduya:remote
  Items: ^The glass shard:@Isra Venn
  When: same day

The Lantern cutter came out of the drift twenty minutes after she got back aboard, which meant they had been watching.

It was a long black ship with a lamp painted on its bow — a real lamp, an old hanging lantern, done in yellow with a little red flame in it — and it came in close and slow and stopped fifty metres off the Gristle's nose and sat there with its forward guns showing, the way you would let a dog see your stick. Isra watched it come from the pilot's seat with her helmet still on her lap. Behind her, Pike had gone very still.

"Gristle," said the radio. A man's voice, light, pleasant, almost apologetic. "This is the Lantern cutter *Patience*, Deacon Rusk speaking. Good morning to you."

"Morning, Deacon."

"Is that Isra Venn? Old Hanne's girl?"

She did not answer that. "You want something, Deacon?"

"I'm afraid I do. I'm afraid I want the Return you're tied to, and whatever you've cut off her, and I'm afraid I'm going to have to ask you to let go of her now." A pause. "Please."

"She's ours. We had the arm on her first. It's on the log."

"I'm sure it is. But there's been a proclamation, you see. As of this morning." The voice was regretful, patient, as if he were explaining to a child why it could not have a sweet. "You'll hear it in a moment, I expect. All glass is tithe. All Returns are the Lantern's. You can keep your arm and your log. It's the glass we need. Every gram."

"For what?"

"For the Needle," said Deacon Rusk.

Isra looked over her shoulder at Pike. He was sitting at the arm controls with his hands flat on his thighs, looking at the black ship in the canopy, and his face had gone a strange grey colour under the beard. He met her eyes and shook his head. *Don't.*

She thought about the glass in the bags in the Gristle's hold, two hours of it, the best she had ever cut. A year's living. Hanne's medicine. The heater. She thought about the guns.

"Cut the arm loose," she said to Pike.

He did. The claw opened and the skiff drifted free, turning, its torn glass sparkling where she had cut it, the grey rag a small dark square in the white of its canopy. The *Patience* put out a grapple of its own and took it in.

"And the hold, if you would," said Deacon Rusk. "I'll send a boy across."

He sent two boys across. They were young and polite and very thorough, in black coveralls with the lamp stitched on the shoulder, and they emptied the Gristle's hold of every bag and checked the lockers and the crawlspace behind the heater, and one of them found the little cache in the floor under Pike's seat and looked at Pike and said nothing and took that too. They did not search Isra. She sat in the pilot's seat with her helmet on her lap and the fist of banded root glass on her belt, under her hand, where she had put it when the *Patience* came out of the drift, and they did not search her, because she was a girl sitting in a seat and they were boys being thorough about lockers.

When they had gone, the radio clicked, and changed, and every channel in the Reach began to speak at once in the same voice.

Isra had heard the Lamplighter before. Everybody had. Mercy Oduya had been preaching from the old Assembly steps in Tallow for twenty-five years and from the Lantern House for five, and her voice came out of every speaker in the Gutter twice a week, warm and low and certain, a voice that sounded as if it was speaking only to you. Isra had never heard it like this. It filled the Gristle's little cockpit to the walls. It sounded like it was coming from inside the hull.

"Children of the Reach," said the Lamplighter. "Listen to me now. The Sixtieth Narrowing is sixty days away.

"You know what the Wardens have told us. You have seen it yourselves. Since the Fifty-Eighth the door has not opened as it should. Since the Fifty-Ninth no ship has passed. The Inward is silent. And the Needle, which we were taught from our cradles would keep what we gave it — the Needle is giving it back. You have all seen the Returns. You have all seen what comes drifting out of the mouth with the dead in it. I have stood in the Ossuary and seen it with my own eyes. I have seen my own son's face in a dream every night for eleven years, and I tell you that the Needle is not giving back the dead to mock us. It is giving them back because it is starving. Because we have fed it less and less, generation after generation, a skiff here and three there, as if the covenant were a tax to be dodged. Because we stopped believing that it was hungry.

"It is hungry. It is dying of hunger. And when it dies, the door closes, and the Reach is alone in the dark forever.

"So hear me. At the Sixtieth Narrowing, the Reach will keep the Needle as it has never been kept. Not nine Keepers. A hundred. Not a cradle each. Every gram of glass that lies in every hold and every cache and every Return in the Reach. All glass is tithe from this day. All Returns belong to the Keeping. Those who give will be blessed. Those who hoard" — the warm voice did not change, not by a hair — "will be found.

"A Great Keeping, children. The greatest since the First. We will feed the door that it may stay a door.

"What the Needle keeps, it keeps for us."

The voice stopped. The channels went back to static.

In the canopy, the *Patience* was turning away, with the stripped Return held in its grapple like a cat with a bird, and the little rag of grey cloth was still there in the window, a dark square, very small.

"Pike," Isra said.

He did not answer. He had his rag out and was coughing into it, a long fit, worse than before, bent double over the arm controls. When he came up out of it his eyes were wet, and he would not look at her.

"You all right?"

"Fine."

"A hundred Keepers," she said. "Where's she going to get a hundred people who want to fly into that?" She jerked her chin at the bruise in the sky.

"She'll get them," Pike said. He folded the rag away. His voice was flat. "She got them last time."

## Hanne
[
  Place: #The Gutter
  Characters: @@Isra Venn @@Hanne Venn
  Items: ^Hanne's cittern:@Hanne Venn ^The glass shard:@Isra Venn
  When: same day
]
The Gutter was the outermost ring of old Tallow, the oldest part of the town, where the first tunnels had been cut down into the salt by the first machines before anybody living could remember. It had been abandoned once, Hanne said, in the good times, when Tallow had spread itself up into terraces and light-wells and gardens. Now the terraces were cracked and dark and most of the light-wells had fallen in, and the Lantern held what was left of the upper town, and the Gutter was full again: bone-pickers, Shoals families who had come in from the rocks when the rocks ran out of air, people who could not pay for heat higher up. The tunnels were narrow and low and smelled of salt and cooking and cold, and everything in them was the same grey-white as the walls, and the only colour anywhere was what people had painted.

Hanne's room was at the end of a dead-end tunnel off Rope Street, behind a door that had once been part of a ship. It was warm, because Isra paid for it to be warm. It was full of things: rags and pots and bundles of dried salt-weed and a thousand scraps of paper and plastic and foil, all of them covered in Hanne's writing, the songs and the scraps of songs and the stories she had collected for sixty years, and the sayings, and the lists of names. It smelled of her, and of the weed she smoked, and of the old wood of the cittern.

She was sitting on the bed with the cittern on her knee when Isra came in, tuning it.

"You're late," she said. "And you've got nothing. I can tell by your face. They took it."

"They took it."

"The Lantern."

"Deacon Rusk." Isra sat down on the floor with her back against the bed and her head against her grandmother's knee, which she had not done since she was small, and Hanne put a hand on her hair and left it there, and went on tuning with the other.

"Rusk," said Hanne, "is a little shit."

"Yes."

"His mother was a little shit before him. I knew her. She sang flat." Hanne plucked a string, frowned, turned a peg. "I heard her, you know. Mercy. On the speakers. A hundred Keepers."

"Pike says she'll get them."

"Pike would know." Hanne's hand moved on her hair. "He flew escort for her, at the Fifty-Ninth. Did you know that? Before you took him on. He was one of her boys, then. He doesn't talk about it." She plucked the string again. "Nobody comes back from the Lantern the same."

Isra had not known that. She sat with it for a while.

"Sing me something," she said.

"What do you want?"

"I don't care. Something old."

Hanne thought. She was seventy-three and looked older, small and brown and folded, with a face like a dried apple and eyes like a bird's, and she had been a teller all her life, like her mother before her and her mother's father. A teller kept things. That was all it meant. In the Quiet, Hanne said, when the machines that kept things had all broken and the Compact had broken and nobody could read anything older than their grandmother's handwriting, the tellers had kept what there was to keep, in their heads and their mouths and their hands. Most of it was wrong. Hanne said that too. *Most of it's wrong, girl, but it's all we've got, and wrong is better than nothing, because you can fix wrong.*

She played a few bars, slowly, and then she sang.

It was the one everybody knew, or knew the start of: the Long Night. The tune was the Hunger Tone underneath, the long drone on the low string that you heard in the Lantern House and in the Wardens' chapel at the Anchor, but Hanne's version had words over it, old words, worn smooth.

*The door grew narrow in the dark,
the Reach was cold and small,
the First Warden heard it hunger
and the Reach gave of its all.*

*They loaded glass in little ships
and flew them through the night,
and what the Needle keeps, it keeps,
and in the morning, light.*

There were more verses. There were always more verses; Hanne had collected forty of them, from forty different tellers, and they contradicted one another on almost every point. Isra had heard them all. She listened anyway, with her eyes closed, her head on her grandmother's knee and the warm drone of the low string going through her.

"Is it true?" she said, when it was finished.

"Which bit?"

"Any of it. That it's hungry. That feeding it works. That there was a First Warden who heard it." She opened her eyes. "Gran, everybody on the speakers is going to be saying it's true for sixty days. I want to know if it's true."

Hanne was quiet.

"I don't know," she said. "I'll tell you what I know, and you can do what you like with it. There was a Concession, before the Compact. That's true. A company from the Inward that owned everything and everybody, and dug the salt, and shipped the glass out through the door. There was a first Narrowing, a bad one, and there was a first Keeping, and the door opened. That's true too, or it's what everybody says, which isn't the same, but it's what we've got. Then there was the Compact, for three hundred years, and the Wardens, and the Draw, and they kept the door every four thousand days, and it opened. Then there was the Severance, and then there was the Quiet, and everything broke, and when it came back together nobody could remember properly how anything had been done. The Wardens kept on keeping. Then they kept less. Then for a while, my gran said, hardly anybody kept at all, and then the Lantern came and started again." She shrugged. "That's it. That's what I know. Whether it's hungry I couldn't tell you. Nobody's ever asked it."

"The Lamplighter says the Returns mean it's starving."

"The Lamplighter would. She has to say something, poor cow. She put her own boy in it." Hanne set the cittern upright against her knee. "The Wardens say it's dying. That's all I've heard from them, the last ten years. *It's dying.* Never why."

Isra was quiet. Then, without quite knowing why she did it, she unclipped the fist of banded glass from her belt and held it up.

"I kept this back. From the Return. Look."

Hanne took it and turned it in the lamplight, and her eyebrows went up. "Well, that's a pretty thing. Layered. I've never seen glass layered like that." She held it closer to her face, squinting. "Like a cake."

"It was at the root. Where it grew out of the hull. It's all like that, all the way down, and it all leans the same way." Isra did not know how to say what she meant. "Like it was pushed. Like something pushed the ship out and the glass grew while it was being pushed."

Hanne looked at her, and then at the glass, and did not say anything. Then she did a strange thing: she held the chunk of glass out over the cittern, close to the strings, the way you might hold a cup under a tap, and with her other hand she plucked the low string. The drone sounded. And the glass — Isra heard it, she would have sworn she heard it — the glass hummed back. Very faint. A note under the note, sweet and thin, like a fingertip on the rim of a wineglass, there and then gone as the string died.

They both sat very still.

"Huh," said Hanne.

"What was that?"

"I don't know." Her grandmother's voice was odd. She plucked the string again, and again they heard it, the faint sweet answering hum from the glass in her hand. "My gran used to say — this was her gran's, I think, or some teller before — she used to say that glass sang, back when the door was well. That the cutters used to hear it singing in the seams, down in the salt, like it was answering something. I always thought it was one of those things people say." She looked down at the glass. "I've never heard it. Not once in my life."

"It's not singing now. It's — answering the string. That's all."

"Is it?" said Hanne.

She held the glass out to Isra, and Isra took it. It was cold in her hand, from the mouth, the deep cold that never quite went out of Return glass. She held it, and Hanne plucked the low string one more time, and Isra felt it, through the palm of her hand, tiny, unmistakable: the glass shivering against her skin, like something alive. Like something trying to tell her a thing in a language she did not have.

Outside in the tunnel somebody's speaker was playing the Lamplighter's proclamation again, from the beginning, the warm low voice coming small and tinny through the ship-door. *Children of the Reach. Listen to me now.*

Isra put the glass inside her shirt, against her skin, where she could feel it, and sat with her head against her grandmother's knee, and listened.
