## The Run In
[
  Place: #Dock Four
  Characters: @@Teo Kesh @@Corrigan Dace:phone
  Items: ^Small Mercy:@Teo Kesh ^Teo's bead:@Teo Kesh ^Debt chit:@Teo Kesh
  When: 2 March 2291
]
The sled was riding high again, and Teo Kesh had been cursing it for an hour.

Forty tonnes of crated brine-glass on a tow-frame half a kilometre long, and the loaders at the Pans had stacked it heavy at the back the way they always did when the shift was ending and nobody cared, so that every time she fired the Small Mercy's mains the whole frame wanted to swing round and come and look at her. She had trimmed it twice. She had told Pans Loading what she thought of their mothers. Now she sat with her boots up on the console and her hands loose on the yoke, feeling the tow through the seat of her coveralls, and let the tug do what tugs were for, which was pull.

The Small Mercy was forty-one years old. She had been Teo's mother's before she was Teo's, and somebody else's before that, and she had been re-hulled twice and re-engined once and patched more times than anybody had logged. Her cockpit smelled of coolant and of the oranges Teo bought at a ruinous price from the hydroponics barge and ate in the long middle of the run, and of Teo, and of forty years of other pilots under that. There was a crack in the forward canopy shaped like a river delta. There was a clip on the console where the debt chit lived. Teo did not look at the chit. She knew what it said. It said 41,212 marks, and on the line under that, in smaller figures, what she had paid off this month, which was less than what had been added to it, which was the joke.

Ahead of her, over the curve of the console and through the delta crack, the Needle was coming up.

You did not see it so much as see what it did. A patch of sky the width of a thumbnail at arm's length where the stars were wrong: pulled long, smeared into arcs, crowded at the rim like fish against a net. At the centre of the wrongness there was a darkness that was not black but a deep bruised colour no one had a good name for, and around it a faint circle of light that the old pilots called the Eye. Anchorage Station hung off to the side of it, a spindle and two wheels lit up like a party, and the traffic lanes ran in toward the mouth in strings of beacon lights, green in and amber out, the way they had for fifty years.

Teo had been looking at the Needle every working day of her life. It had stopped being strange when she was nine. It had not stopped being the only door.

"Mercy, Brannock." The radio, Corrigan's voice, too cheerful for the hour. "You still dragging that cow?"

"She's dragging me."

"Told you. Load your own. Never let Pans load you on a shift change."

"I was asleep on a shift change, Corr, that's the point of a shift change."

"I'm three hundred back of you. I can see your sled swinging from here. It's beautiful. It's like watching a cathedral fall over very slowly."

"Go and fly into something."

He laughed. Corrigan Dace had a laugh that had got her into trouble more than once, a long time ago, before Lulu, before he had sold the half-share in the Brannock to cover his wife's medical and then lost the wife anyway. They had stopped sleeping together about the time they had both started owing more than they could ever pay, which Teo privately thought was not a coincidence. Debt took up the room in you where other things went.

She touched the bead at her throat, a habit. It was a nub of brine-glass the size of a pea, cloudy, faintly blue, on a cord gone black with her sweat. Nico had cut it from his first seam in Shaft Nine and given it to her when he was sixteen and she was twenty-four and still thought she would get them both out. He had told her, very seriously, that glass always knew where the Needle was. It was the kind of thing cutters said. But she had noticed, over the years, that the bead lay a little to the left of where it should on her breastbone when she was out on the lanes, a little toward the mouth, and it had bothered her more than she would have admitted to Nico, who believed things.

The radio clicked over to the approach channel.

"All inbound traffic, Anchorage Approach." Not the usual controller, not the bored boy with the Ardent accent who flirted with the tug pilots. A woman, clipped. "All inbound traffic, hold at your current marker. I say again, hold at your current marker. Outbound traffic, cancel throat entry, return to Anchorage. Acknowledge."

Teo took her boots off the console.

Along the lanes the beacon strings were changing. The green in-lane lights were blinking to amber one marker at a time, rolling down the string toward her like a rumour. Far ahead, at the mouth, a long-hauler that had been lined up to go through — one of the big Ostrander freighters, three hundred metres of hull with the company sunburst on its flank — was firing its forward thrusters, slowly, the way a big ship did everything, backing away from the dark.

"Mercy acknowledges, holding at marker nine," she said, and cut the mains, and felt the sled overtake her a little on the tether and come up short with a jolt that rattled her teeth. She cursed it. She was looking at the Needle.

It was the same. That was what she would remember afterward: that it looked the same. The same bruised dark, the same smeared stars. And then, as she watched, the ring of light around the Eye flickered, as though something had passed across it, and the arcs of starlight at the rim shivered and drew in, very slightly, the way a pupil does in a bright room.

"Did you see that," said Corrigan on the private channel.

"I saw it."

"What was that."

"I don't know."

"It flinched," Corrigan said. "Teo. The bastard flinched."

The bead at her throat lay very still against her skin. She put her hand over it without thinking and held it there, and the two of them sat at their markers three hundred metres apart and said nothing at all for a while, and watched the long-hauler back away from the mouth like a man backing away from a dog.

They held for three hours. Approach would say nothing except *hold*, and then *stand by*, and then, at last, *inbound traffic, proceed to dock in sequence*, without explanation, as if the three hours had not happened. Teo brought the sled in to Dock Four on a bad angle because her hands were cold, and the dock crew swore at her, and she swore back, and signed for forty tonnes of glass on a slate that added eleven marks to her chit for the late docking. Eleven marks. She looked at the figure.

"Ask them what's going on," Corrigan said on the radio, still out at his marker, still waiting his turn.

"I'm a tug pilot," she said. "Nobody tells tug pilots anything. That's how you can tell we're tug pilots."

But she stood for a minute at the dock window after the crew had gone, with her helmet under her arm, looking up the long curve of the station toward the Needle-ward end where the Lectern was, the observatory, its dome lit up as she had never seen it lit at this hour of a shift. She could see figures moving behind the glass. She could not see what they were doing. She could guess that they were frightened, because she was.

## The Harp
[
  Place: #The Lectern
  Characters: @@Ambrose Lyle @@Bettany Shore @@Hesper Vane
  Items: ^The Fork:@Ambrose Lyle ^The harp:#The Lectern
  When: same day
]
The harp was singing in a key Ambrose Lyle had never heard it use.

He had built the thing. Twenty-three years ago, a younger man with a younger man's back, he had strung its first course of resonator wires across a frame of fused silica in a workshop in Port Ardent and shipped it through the Needle in crates and assembled it here under the dome, and he had played it — that was the word everyone used, though it was a matter of listening and not of playing — every working day since. He knew its voice the way you knew the voice of someone you had been married to. It hummed. In its ordinary moods it hummed a soft, wide, many-stranded chord, the throat's noise turned into sound, and a reader sat at the console and listened and watched the traces and drew the throat's shape for the pilots who would fly it. It had sung louder before, and softer. It had never sung like this: thin, high, drawn tight, the whole chord pulled up into its upper strings and straining there like a held breath.

"Again," he said.

Bettany Shore, at the second console, ran the sweep again. She was twenty-two and had been his junior for eight months, and she had the kind of face that showed everything, and what it showed now was that she wanted very much to be told that this was normal.

"Waist reading's at sixty-one per cent of nominal," she said. "It was seventy-four an hour ago. It was ninety-eight yesterday."

"And the rate?"

"It's slowing. I think it's slowing." She looked at him. "That's good, isn't it? If it's slowing?"

Ambrose did not answer. He took off his reading glasses, and polished them on his shirt, and put them back on, which was what he did instead of swearing. Then he reached into the pocket of his jacket and took out the fork.

It was brass, fifteen centimetres long, its two tines worn bright at the tips and dull at the root where his thumb went. On the stem, in a hand engraver's tidy capitals, it said A. L. — 440 — FOR CLARE. He had bought it in a music shop in Ardent the year his daughter was born, because she had been born in A, as he used to tell her — screaming on a concert pitch, the midwife said, you could have tuned an orchestra — and he had had it engraved for her as a joke, meaning to give it to her when she was old enough to think the joke was funny. She had been four when he took the Concern's posting. She was twenty-seven now. He had the fork still. Every instrument he had built since had been tuned to it, because there was a peculiar comfort in the thought that every measurement he made of the most extraordinary object in human space was referred, in the end, to a note his daughter had screamed in a delivery room. He had never told anyone that.

He struck the fork against the heel of his hand and touched its stem to the reference plate, and the harp's tuning monitor took the note and laid it across the traces like a ruler. He watched the strings settle against it. Drift was nothing. Drift was a hair. The instrument was true.

So the throat was doing this. Not the harp. The throat.

"It's a Narrowing," he said.

"There was a Narrowing in eighty," Bettany said. "I read the logs. It closed for six hours. Nobody died."

"There was a Narrowing in eighty," Ambrose agreed. "And sixty-nine, and fifty-eight, and forty-seven, every four thousand days, give or take, as long as there have been people here to watch. Six hours. Four hours. Nine. The waist went down to eighty per cent of nominal in eighty and everyone was very excited and wrote papers." He looked at the trace, the bright line crawling down the screen. "We are at sixty-one, and sixty days out from where the cycle says the trough should be. It is not going to be six hours."

"How long?"

"I don't know."

"Could it —" She stopped.

"Close?" He made himself say it, because someone was going to. "I don't know that either. Nobody knows. Nobody has ever seen one go this deep. We don't have a model for this. We have four data points and a lot of hope." He heard his voice and disliked it. "Bettany. Log the sweep. Log everything. Don't round anything."

The door at the back of the dome slid open, and the Director came in.

Hesper Vane did not hurry. Ambrose had known her for eleven years and had never seen her hurry; he had sometimes wondered whether she was physically capable of it, whether some childhood injury had removed the faculty. She was a slight, straight woman of forty-seven with grey cropped hair and the Concern's sunburst at her collar, and she came across the dome to his console at the pace of someone crossing a room to look at a painting, and stood at his shoulder, and looked at the trace.

"Mr Lyle," she said.

"Director."

"Tell me."

He told her. He told her the way he would have told a student, carefully and in order, because he had found that this was the only way to talk to Hesper Vane: she would follow you anywhere you could actually go and nowhere you could not. The waist. The rate. The four previous Narrowings and their depths. The sixty days. She listened without interrupting. When he had finished she was quiet for a while, looking at the line.

"Can it close?" she said. "Permanently."

"I don't know."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have. Nobody has a theory of the Needle, Director. We have observations. The observations say it has always opened again. The observations are fifty years long. The Needle is" — he lifted a hand toward the dome, where the bruise of the mouth hung huge and silent beyond the glass — "considerably older than fifty years."

"So it can."

"It might. It might not. If you want a number I will invent one for you, but I would be inventing it."

She nodded slowly, as if he had confirmed something. "Then nobody else knows either," she said. "Until I say."

Ambrose looked at her.

"I want this kept in this room," Hesper Vane said. "The readings, the projections, all of it. Miss Shore." Bettany jumped. "You will log everything Mr Lyle asks you to log, and you will log it to the Director's channel and to nowhere else, and you will speak to no one about it, including your mother, your friends, and the very attractive young man in Approach Control you spend your breaks with. Do you understand?"

Bettany went red to the ears and said that she did.

"There is going to be an announcement this evening," Hesper Vane said to Ambrose, more quietly. "About other matters. It would be unhelpful if this were the subject of conversation in every bar on Lantern Row before I had made it."

"Other matters," he said.

She looked at him for a moment, and something in her face that was not quite kindness decided to tell him. "The Board isn't renewing the Charter," she said. "We are withdrawing. All salaried staff, all equipment that pays for its passage. The *Providence* sails on the twenty-eighth of April and she will be the last Ostrander ship through the Needle. The Concession continues. The debts continue. But the Concern will manage it from Ardent, at a distance, through local administration." A small, dry pause. "Through me, in point of fact. I'm staying."

He had heard rumours. Everyone had heard rumours. He found that hearing it said made a hollow place open in his chest, very quickly, the way the floor of a lift drops.

"Your berth on the *Providence* is confirmed, of course," she said. "Senior grade. I put it through myself this morning." She glanced at the trace. "Assuming there's a Needle for her to sail through on the twenty-eighth. Which, if I understand you, is in fact a question."

"The trough is around the first of May," he said. "If the cycle holds. She'd sail three days before."

"Then let's hope it holds." Hesper Vane straightened. "Go home, Ambrose. You've earned it. Your daughter will be glad. Keep this room quiet until then, and give me the numbers every six hours, and go home."

She went out, at the same unhurried pace. The door slid shut behind her.

Bettany let out a breath that she seemed to have been holding since the Director came in. "Is that true? We're leaving? The whole Concern?"

"So it seems."

"But what happens to — everyone? Here?"

Ambrose turned the fork over in his fingers. Through the dome the Needle hung, vast and patient, its rim of bent stars drawn in just perceptibly tighter than it had been that morning. He thought of forty thousand people on Hollin, dug down into the salt in their towns, cutting glass to pay off debts that the Concern would go on owning from the far side of a door that might be closing. He thought of Clare, whose face he could not quite remember, whom he would see in fifty-seven days.

"Log the sweep," he said. "Don't round anything."

## Lantern Row
[
  Place: #The Lantern
  Characters: @@Teo Kesh @@Nico Kesh @@Corrigan Dace @@Salome Okafor @@Lulu Dace @@Hesper Vane:remote
  Items: ^The Tally:@Salome Okafor
  When: same day
]
The Lantern was the third bar on Lantern Row and the only one where the beer was made from anything you could identify, and on the night of the second of March it was full to the walls.

Teo came down the stair from the Pans lift still in her flight coveralls and found Nico at their usual table at the back, under the vent that dripped, with his cutter's goggles pushed up into his hair and the grey dust of Shaft Nine still in the creases of his knuckles, holding forth. He was always holding forth. He was twenty-six and had been holding forth since he could stand. Around him, five or six young cutters were listening with the expressions of people who had heard it before and liked it anyway.

"— because they need us more than we need them. That's the whole thing. That's all you have to understand. Who cuts the glass? We cut the glass. Who hauls it? Teo hauls it." He saw her and pointed. "There. A worker. Look at her. Look at the state of her. Teo, tell them who needs who."

"I need a drink," she said, and he laughed and pushed his across the table to her, and she drank half of it and sat down.

She was always surprised by how glad she was to see him. It caught her every time, under the breastbone where the bead lay. He had their mother's long face and their father's terrible laugh and none of the caution either of them had died of, and when he was talking he lit up like a beacon string, and she had spent twenty years trying to keep him from walking into things and loved him for walking into them anyway.

"They held the lanes three hours," she said, low, under the noise. "Something's wrong with the Needle."

The light went out of his face, a little. "What kind of wrong?"

"Corr says it flinched. I say I don't know. Approach wouldn't say anything. The Lectern's lit up like it's Founders' Day."

Corrigan came in an hour later with Lulu on his shoulders, because he had nobody to leave her with on a long shift and the Lantern's owner had a soft spot for her and fed her fried salt-eel from the kitchen. She was nine and had her father's laugh and a bag of bolts she was collecting for reasons she would not explain, and she climbed down onto the bench next to Teo and showed her the new ones, and Teo admired them, and Corrigan sat down heavily and drank Nico's second beer without asking.

"Eleven marks," he said. "Late docking. Eleven marks for holding where they told me to hold."

"I got eleven too."

"Everyone got eleven." He rubbed his face. "They're going to say something tonight. There's a broadcast at twenty hundred. All screens. Mandatory. Fat Diz behind the bar says the screens in the Pans dorms have been showing the sunburst all afternoon."

"Fat Diz says a lot of things."

"Fat Diz says the Concern's pulling out," Corrigan said.

Nobody said anything. The bar went on being loud around them.

Mother Salome came in a little before twenty hundred, the way she always did on the nights there was trouble, as if she could smell it in the ventilation. She was sixty-odd, tall and stooped, ordained by nobody, with a face like a cliff that had been rained on for a long time. She did not drink. She went from table to table with her hand on people's shoulders, and she carried under her arm, wrapped in an old blanket, the Tally.

Teo had seen it a hundred times and it still did something to her stomach. It was a plate of steel half a metre across, cut with a torch from one of the support beams they had dragged out of Shaft Nine after the collapse of eighty-six, and on it, in rows, scratched with a nail and a cutter's stylus and anything else to hand, were the names of the shaft's dead. Thirty-one from the collapse, at the top, in Salome's careful capitals. Below them the others, from bursts and glass-lung and falls and the slow ordinary ways, in a dozen different hands. Salome brought it out when somebody died and let the family scratch the name. She brought it out, too, on nights like this one, without explanation, and set it on the bar, and people touched it as they went past. Teo had never asked why.

The screens over the bar flicked to the sunburst at twenty hundred exactly, and the room went quiet in a ripple from the front to the back, and then Hesper Vane was there.

She was in her office, the Director's Gallery on Anchorage — Teo knew it from the window behind her, and the edge of the Needle in the window, bruise-coloured. She sat very straight and spoke without notes. She did not hurry.

She said that the Board of the Ostrander Concern had determined not to renew its Charter for the Reach. She said that the Concern was grateful. She said that all salaried staff of the Concern would be withdrawn, with such equipment as justified its passage, on the *Ostrander Providence*, departing the twenty-eighth of April, and that this would conclude the Concern's direct presence in the Reach. She said that the Concession continued; that all contracts, indentures and debts remained in force and remained the property of the Concern; that they would be administered locally, by an office of the Concession which she herself would head; and that the shipment of brine-glass to the Inward would continue at the current quotas. She said that she was confident that the people of the Reach would meet this change with the steadiness for which they were known.

Then she said, in exactly the same voice, that Anchorage had today observed the beginning of a Narrowing of the Needle — a natural event, previously observed on four occasions — and that this Narrowing was expected to be deeper and longer than those before it; that throat traffic would be restricted from time to time; and that the Concession's throat-readers were monitoring it closely. She said that there was no cause for alarm. She said goodnight.

The screens went back to the sunburst.

For a moment nobody in the Lantern moved at all. Then everybody did. Somebody threw a glass at the screen and missed. Somebody was crying. Fat Diz was shouting for order and getting none. At the next table a cutter Teo half knew was standing on the bench yelling *what does it mean, remain the property, what does that mean* at nobody, over and over, as if one of them might know.

Teo sat very still.

It meant what it said. That was the thing about Hesper Vane: everything she said meant exactly what it said, and you only had to listen. It meant that the Concern was leaving and taking its money and its people and its doctors and its ships, and leaving behind the paper. It meant that forty-one thousand two hundred and twenty-three marks — she had the new figure by heart already — were still owed, to a company that would be on the other side of the Needle, and would go on being owed, and would be collected by whoever sat in that office with the bruise in the window. It meant that she would haul glass for the Concession until she died, the way her mother had, and her mother's mother, and that the oranges would get more expensive, because everything would.

And it meant that the door — the one door, the only door, the door she had looked at every working day of her life and stopped finding strange at nine — was doing something nobody had seen it do before. *No cause for alarm.* Hesper Vane had said it in the same voice as everything else. Teo had heard the Lectern's lights in it.

"They can't," Nico was saying. He was on his feet. His face had gone white under the dust. "They can't just — keep the paper and leave. That's not — there are laws."

"Whose?" said Corrigan, quietly. He had Lulu pulled in against his side. "The laws are in Ardent, Nic. With the Concern."

"Then we don't pay." Nico looked round at the cutters, at the bar, at the whole loud frightened room, and Teo saw it come into his face, the thing she had spent twenty years keeping him from walking into, and saw that this time there was nothing she could do. "They're leaving. Who's going to make us? The Director and her Marshal and whoever's too poor to get on the *Providence*? They need the glass. They said so. *The shipment of brine-glass will continue.* Who cuts it?"

"Nico," she said.

"Who cuts it, Teo?"

The cutters around the table were looking at him. Some of them were nodding.

Across the room Mother Salome had set the Tally down on the bar. She stood beside it with one hand flat on the steel, the way you would put a hand on the neck of a frightened animal, and she was looking at nobody, or at all of them. As Teo watched, people began to move past the bar and touch it, as they always did, without being asked — a hand, a fingertip, a palm — and go on. A cutter with a burn scar across his jaw. A woman with a baby. Lulu, slipping out from under her father's arm, standing on tiptoe to reach. One by one, the whole time the shouting went on, they went up and touched the names.

Teo did not go. She sat with Nico's beer going warm in her hand and her other hand over the bead at her throat, which lay, she noticed — she could not help noticing — a little to the left. A little toward the mouth.

## Night Watch
[
  Place: #The Lectern
  Characters: @@Ambrose Lyle
  Items: ^The Fork:@Ambrose Lyle
  When: same day
]
At two in the morning, station time, Ambrose sent Bettany to bed and sat on alone at the harp.

The dome was dark except for the consoles. Beyond the glass the Needle's mouth filled half the sky, and in the dark it was easier to see the ring of light the pilots called the Eye, faint and grey and very slightly irregular, like a coin worn by thumbs. He had read somewhere, long ago, that the brain is built to see faces in things. He had never seen a face in the Needle. He had sometimes thought he saw attention.

He put on the old headphones, the ones with the cracked band, and listened.

The harp was quieter now. The waist had held at sixty for four hours. The high strained chord had relaxed a little, back down toward something like its old self, and under it — he had to close his eyes for this — under it there was the deep wide hum of the throat, the sound he had spent twenty-three years learning. He let it fill his head. He had told Hesper Vane the truth: nobody had a theory of the Needle. But he had sometimes thought, in the middle of the night, with the headphones on, that he was closer to one than anybody, and that it was not a theory he could write down. It was the sound. It was knowing the sound.

And there was something in it tonight that he did not know.

It was very faint. It was so far down that at first he thought it was the station: a pump somewhere, a ventilation fan, the long slow flex of the wheels. It was lower than the lowest course of the harp, below hearing really, a thing he felt in the bones of his face more than heard — a slow swell, rising and falling over a period of minutes, like a sea under a ship. It was not in the traces. He checked. The traces showed nothing but the waist and its strain. It was only in the sound, in the long wide chord, a kind of tide.

He sat with it for half an hour.

Then he took the fork out of his pocket and struck it against his hand and held it to the reference plate, and listened to the note lie down across the chord like a ruler, true; and the swell was still there underneath, rising and falling, rising and falling, patient as breathing.

Drift, he told himself. An artefact of the strain. A beat between strings pulled out of their ordinary tension. He wrote a note in the log — *low-frequency swell, period approx. 4 min, source unknown, probably instrumental* — and looked at the word *probably* for a long time, and did not cross it out.

Fifty-seven days. He put the fork back in his pocket and kept his hand on it, and sat in the dark with the Needle and listened to it breathe.
