## Hoarders
[

The Lamplighter called it the Cleansing, and it began at midnight on the twenty-sixth of May, twenty-eight days before the Sixtieth Narrowing, with a sermon.

Isra heard it from the Pans. She was on the bone-pickers' dock with a torch in her teeth, under the Gristle's belly, refitting the deck plates the Lantern had torn up the week before, and the speakers on the dock came on all at once the way they did, and that warm low voice filled the cold hangar like water filling a tank.

"Children," said the Lamplighter. "There are those among us who hoard.

"I have asked for the glass. I have asked gently. I have sent my Deacon to your doors with a lamp and a kind word. Most of you have given. I bless you. But there are those among us — in the Gutter, in the Pans, in the old Shoals holds — who have hidden what the Needle needs. Who have kept back glass in their walls and their floors and their mouths, while the door dies. Who have gone, some of them, in secret, to the Anchor, to whisper with the Wardens about things the Wardens have no business saying." A pause. "I know who you are. I have always known.

"Tonight my Lantern will go into the Gutter and cleanse it. Every room. Every wall. Every floor. What is hidden will be found, and what cannot be found will be burned out. Do not stand in their way. If you have nothing hidden, you have nothing to fear.

"What the Needle keeps, it keeps for us."

Isra dropped the torch.

It took her twenty minutes to get from the Pans to Rope Street. She ran the whole way, up through the cargo tunnels and the old shuttle ramp and the long dark gallery under the Third Terrace where the light-wells had fallen in, and the whole way she could smell it coming: smoke. Not salt-smoke, the dry grey breath of a cutting torch on rock; real smoke, black and greasy, the smoke of rags and oil and wood and plastic, of everything people had carried down into the Gutter because it was all they had. By the time she came out at the head of Rope Street the tunnel was full of it to the roof, rolling and orange-lit, and people were coming out of it the other way, crawling, coughing, carrying children, carrying nothing.

The Lantern had lit it from both ends.

They had not meant to, perhaps. Isra would think that later, much later, when she could think anything. They had come down Rope Street with their oil lamps on poles, room by room, tearing out walls and floors, and somewhere somebody had fought them, or dropped a lamp, or thrown one; and the Gutter was a hundred years of rags and bedding and dried salt-weed and paper packed into tunnels with no air, and it had gone up the way paper goes up. She could see them at the far end of the tunnel through the smoke, the black coats, lamps swinging, falling back. Coughing. Shouting at each other. Not coming forward.

Hanne's room was at the end of a dead-end off Rope Street. In the middle. Where the smoke was thickest.

Isra pulled her shirt up over her mouth and nose and went in.

She did not remember most of it. Heat, low down, along the floor. The walls invisible. Somebody's hand catching at her ankle — a child, a boy, she dragged him back to the street and went in again. The dead-end. The ship-door, which had been part of a ship, which was steel, which was standing open. And in the doorway, on his knees, a huge dark shape coughing so hard he could not stand, with something in his arms.

"Pike."

"Got her," Pike said. It came out of him in pieces. "Got her. Ribs. Can't — lift — "

Hanne was in his arms. Small, folded, wrapped in her shawl. Her face was black with soot and her eyes were shut and she was holding, against her chest, with both arms, the cittern.

Isra took her. She weighed nothing. Isra took her and put her over her shoulder the way you carry a sack of glass, and got her other arm under Pike's arm, and the three of them went back up Rope Street through the smoke on their knees, and their hands, and Pike's breath whistling and whistling, until the air got thinner and cooler and they came out into the junction under the old shuttle ramp and lay down on the salt.

Deacon Rusk was standing at the junction with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the smoke come out of the tunnel.

He looked down at them. At Pike, flat on his back with his strapped ribs heaving. At Isra on her knees. At Hanne, in her shawl, with the cittern in her arms.

"Old Hanne," he said. He sounded, for once, almost uncertain. "Is she — "

"Get away from her," said Isra.

He looked at her. His face, under the soot and the lamplight, was unreadable. Then he stepped back, one step, and turned his head and called to the black coats behind him: "Leave these. They've nothing. Go on." And he went away up the ramp without looking back.

Hanne opened her eyes.

"Girl," she said. Her voice was a thread.

"I'm here. Gran. I'm here. Don't talk."

"Don't be stupid. I've been talking for seventy-three years, I'm not stopping now." Her bird's eyes moved, found Isra's face, held it. "The cittern."

"You've got it. It's here. You've got it."

"Listen." The old woman's hand moved on the strings. "The low string. Do you hear me? The low string. Never retune it. It's never been retuned. Not by me. Not by my mother. My mother's gran set it, and her father before her, back and back. Every teller. We're told: you can tune the others how you like, but the low one stays. Always. It's the old note." She coughed. It went on a long time, and when it stopped there was blood on her lips. "I never knew why. Nobody knew why. We just kept it." She looked at Isra. "That's why your glass sings at it. Isn't it. That's what you found."

Isra could not speak. She nodded.

"Hah," said Hanne. Something like satisfaction moved in her face. "Wrong is better than nothing. Because you can fix wrong. Didn't I tell you." Her fingers found the low string and plucked it, very softly. It sounded under the roar of the burning tunnel: one deep note, old, true. Against Isra's breastbone, inside her shirt, the shard woke and hummed.

"Take it to your Warden," said Hanne. "The boy. Take it to him."

"Gran — "

"*The door grew narrow in the dark,*" sang Hanne Venn, in a voice so thin it was hardly there, plucking the low string, "*the Reach was cold and small — *" and stopped, and did not go on.

Isra knelt on the salt with her grandmother in her arms, and the cittern between them, and the low string still faintly humming. Beside her Pike lay with his face turned away. Behind them, all down Rope Street, the Gutter burned.

## The Old Note
[

She came in to Dock Four at noon the next day with Pike strapped into the jump-seat and her grandmother's body wrapped in a bone-picker's shroud in the Gristle's hold, and Lucan was on the dock, and when she climbed down out of the hatch and saw him she found that she could not let go of the ladder.

He came and took her hands off it, one finger at a time, and held them.

They laid Hanne out in the harp hall. That was Lucan's idea; Isra would not have thought of it. The Wardens had a rite for the dead, he said, an old one, older than the Quiet: the body lay for a night under the dome, in the sound of the harp, so that the Needle would know who was coming. He did not believe the last part. He said so. But he thought Hanne might have liked the music. So they carried her up through the dark spindle, the two of them, with Pike walking behind, slowly, one hand on the wall, and laid her on a pallet on the worn floor under the mouth with her shawl folded round her and the cittern beside her, and Lucan lit the little lamps that the rite required, nine of them, round the pallet, and they sat down on the floor.

The harp was singing the Hunger Tone again. Lucan had retuned it, as the Prior ordered, the night the Prior ordered it. He had sat up all night doing it, he told Isra, without once looking at the screen.

"I've been playing it like this for nine days," he said. "Listening to it lie to me." He looked at the harp, gold in the lamplight. "I've been thinking about your grandmother's cittern the whole time."

Isra said nothing. After a while she reached over and picked up the cittern from beside Hanne's body, and set it on her knee, the way Hanne had always set it, and held it out to him.

"The low string," she said. "She said it's never been retuned. Never. Every teller back and back. They're told the low one stays." Her voice did not quite hold. "She said it's the old note."

Lucan looked at the cittern. Then he got up without a word and went to the reliquary grille.

The hinge-pins were back in. The Prior had seen to it. Lucan did not bother with them. He took out of his sleeve, from somewhere inside his robe, a small brass object, and turned round, and Isra saw what it was.

"You — "

"I took it back the night after he took it," Lucan said. "Off his bedside table. He sleeps very deeply. He hasn't noticed yet. Or he has, and he hasn't said." He came back and knelt beside her. "I'm going to hell, I think. If there is one. I've stopped being sure there's anything to go to."

He struck the Fork against his palm. Its note went up, thin and pure, into the dome, and met the harp's drone and fought it, *wah, wah, wah*. And then, while it was still sounding, Isra plucked the low string of her grandmother's cittern.

The two notes met.

They did not fight. They lay down together in the air of the hall like two people who had been apart a long time, one inside the other, so exactly the same that for a moment Isra could not tell which she was hearing — the Fork or the string or both — only one note, round and clear, going up into the dome under the bruised dark mouth. Inside her shirt the shard rang against her breastbone like a struck bell.

Lucan let the Fork fall to his lap. His face was wet.

"The tellers," he said. "The tellers kept it. All through the Quiet. When the order lost the records, and the harp went to the Hunger Tone, and nobody remembered — they kept the old note. In a string. Not knowing why." He gave a short, cracked laugh. "Three hundred years. In a cittern in the Gutter."

Isra put the cittern back down beside Hanne, carefully, the low string still faintly humming against the old woman's shoulder. Then she lay down on the floor of the harp hall next to her grandmother's body with her face against the shawl, the way she had lain against Hanne's knee when she was small, and cried the way she had not cried on the salt at the junction or in the Gristle or on the dock — all of it at once, out loud, with nothing held back, while Lucan sat beside her with the Fork in his lap and his hand on her back, and Pike, against the wall, with his eyes closed, coughed softly into his rag.

## The Lamplighter's Chapel
[
  Place: #The Lectern

The Lamplighter came to take the Anchor the next morning, and found a dead woman in her chapel.

Lucan heard the ships before he saw them: the deep shudder through the station's frame as the black ore-hauler docked, and then another, and another. Eight ships. More than she had brought the first time. More than the Anchor had seen in a century. He was on his feet in the harp hall with the Fork still in his sleeve when the iron door opened and the black coats came in, two by two, with lamps, and spread along the walls in their silent double rank as they had before. Then the Prior, on his two sticks, his face like old paper. Then the Deacon. Then her.

She came into the middle of the hall and stopped.

The nine little lamps were still burning round the pallet. Hanne lay in her shawl under the mouth with the cittern at her side. Isra was sitting on the floor at her head, cross-legged, with her back straight and her face swollen from crying and her eyes perfectly dry; and she looked up at Mercy Oduya as the Lamplighter came in, and did not get up, and did not look away.

The Lamplighter looked at the body for a long moment.

"Who is this?" she said.

Nobody answered. Then the Deacon, behind her, said quietly, "Old Hanne Venn. The teller. From Rope Street."

Lucan saw it go into her. He saw it the way he had seen her face change the first time she stood under the dome: something going through the broad plain face and leaving it different. She stood very still.

"Hanne," she said. "From Rope Street." She took a step toward the pallet. "In the Cleansing?"

"In the smoke," said Isra from the floor. "Your smoke."

The Deacon moved. The Lamplighter lifted one hand without turning, and he stopped.

"She sang at my son's naming," said Mercy Oduya. She was looking at Hanne's face. "Did you know that? I was twenty-two. I lived on Rope Street then, four doors down from her. I had nothing. I had a baby and a room with no heat. And she came to his naming and sat on my floor with that — " she looked at the cittern — "with that thing on her knee, and sang the Long Night for him, all forty verses, every one of them different. She said he should hear all of them. Because they were all wrong, and he should know." Her voice was very low. "Tobin. She sang all forty for Tobin."

She knelt down. The black coats along the walls shifted, uneasily. She knelt on the worn floor beside the pallet in her black coat, across the body from Isra, and put out one cracked brown hand and laid it flat on the old woman's shawl, over her heart.

"I'm sorry," she said. To Hanne. "I'm so sorry."

Isra stared at her.

"You burned her," Isra said.

"Yes," said the Lamplighter. She did not take her hand away. "I did. I said cleanse the Gutter, and they cleansed it, and she was in it. I'll carry her." She looked up, across the body, into Isra's face. "I carry a great many. I'll carry her too. And I will go on, because the door is dying, girl, and if it dies every one of them was for nothing. Every one. Hanne and Tobin and all of them." Her dark eyes were wet and perfectly steady. "Do you understand that? It's the only way they mean anything. If it works."

Isra did not answer. Lucan, standing by the harp with the Fork heavy in his sleeve, watched the two of them kneeling on either side of the dead woman, the prophet and the bone-picker, and thought that he had never in his life seen two people understand each other so completely and hate each other so much.

The Lamplighter stood up.

"The old woman stays here tonight," she said. "With her lamps. Nobody touches her. Tomorrow the Wardens will give her the rite, in the grey, properly." She turned to the Prior. "And then, Prior, this hall is mine. My Keepers will come here to be blessed. My readers will sit at that harp. The Great Keeping will go from this station, from this dock, under this dome. Your Wardens will keep their cells and their greenhouse and their grey, and they will stand on the dock when my Keepers fly, and sing." She paused. "That is how it will be."

Prior Saar leaned on his two sticks.

"That is how it will be," he said.

She turned to go. At the iron door she stopped, and looked back once — at the harp, singing its long false drone; at the old woman on the floor in her ring of lamps; at Lucan beside the frame.

"Brother," she said. "You told me the truth, the first time. That it was dying. You were right." She looked at the mouth through the dome. "I mean to save it. I'd like you on my ship when I do. Giving me the line. Will you?"

Lucan felt the Fork in his sleeve, cold against his arm. He felt Isra's eyes on him from the floor.

"Yes, Lamplighter," he said.

She nodded, as if that were the answer she had expected, and went out, and her black coats went after her, and the Deacon last, who looked back from the door at Isra on the floor — a long look, Lucan thought, not pleasant and not unpleasant, the look of a man reading a list — and closed it.

Isra got up off the floor.

"*Yes, Lamplighter,*" she said.

"Isra — "

"You said *yes*."

"I said yes," said Lucan, "because she is going to fly a hundred skiffs into the throat in twenty-six days, and she wants somebody to give her the line, and if it isn't me it'll be one of her own, who'll give her whatever line the harp tells him with the harp tuned to a lie." He took the Fork out of his sleeve and held it, so she could see it, and put it back. "I'd rather it was me. I'd rather be on the ship. I'd rather be in the room when it happens, with this in my pocket, than anywhere else in the Reach. Wouldn't you?"

Isra looked at him for a long moment, there in the ring of lamps over her grandmother's body, with the harp droning its false note behind him and the mouth leaning over them both.

"Yes," she said at last. "I would."

She sat back down at Hanne's head. After a while Lucan sat down beside her, and they kept the vigil together, all that night, the two of them and the nine little lamps and the old woman, under the dome, while the harp sang the wrong note over and over and over into the dark.
