## Shaft Nine
[
  Place: #Shaft Nine
  Characters: @@Nico Kesh @@Teo Kesh @@Salome Okafor

  Relationships: ~Teo Kesh/Nico Kesh:Sister and brother
  When: 23 March 2291

At nine hundred metres the salt sweated.

Nico Kesh had been cutting in Shaft Nine for ten years and he still noticed it every shift, the moment the cage dropped past the eight-hundred mark and the air turned thick and warm and the walls began to glisten: a fine bright beading of brine on the grey-white rock, catching the lamps, running in slow threads down the cuts. The old cutters said the salt was crying. The older cutters said it was the glass breathing, down in the seams, and that if you put your ear to the wall in the deep galleries at the end of a shift when the machines were off you could hear it. Nico had tried, at sixteen. He had heard something. He had never been able to say what.

The seam they were working that week was the Lower Nine, a long low gallery where the glass ran in a vein as thick as a man's thigh between two beds of hard salt, and the Concession had put two shifts on it since the Director's address. Then three. Keeping glass, the overseers called it, with a kind of embarrassed reverence, as though it were for a church. The quota boards at the cage-head had been repainted with a new line at the bottom, in red: *KEEPING ALLOCATION*. The number on it went up every day.

Fennick lost his hand at the end of the second shift on the twenty-third.

Nico did not see it happen. He was twenty metres along the gallery with his own cutter in the vein, and he heard the burst — a flat hard crack like a pistol shot in a small room, which was the sound glass made when it let go — and then the scream, and by the time he got there Fennick was sitting in the dust with his back against the wall holding his right arm up in front of his face and looking at it with an expression of polite surprise, and there was nothing on the end of it below the wrist but a mess, and the vein beside him was a starred white crater the size of a dinner plate where the glass had shattered under his blade.

"Too fast," Fennick said, to nobody. "I was going too fast. They said go fast."

They got him up in the cage. They got a tourniquet on him. Nico rode up with him, holding his good hand, and Fennick talked the whole way up, very calmly, about his daughter's birthday, and passed out at the six-hundred mark.

The vote was that night, in the shaft canteen, with the dust still on everyone.

Nico stood on a table to call it. He had not planned what he was going to say. He did not need to. The canteen was full, three shifts' worth, a hundred and forty cutters and loaders and cage-men crammed in among the long tables in the bad light, and every one of them had heard the burst or heard about it, and the red line on the quota board was in every one of their heads, and Nico stood on the table and said Fennick's name and then said nothing at all for a while, and let them look at him.

"They want us to cut faster," he said at last. "So they can take what we cut and throw it into the dark."

Nobody said anything.

"That's what the Keeping is. You heard her. She's going to put our glass in little ships and send it out to the Needle and drop it down the hole. Our glass. That we cut. That Fennick cut. And the price of it — " He held up his own right hand and turned it, slowly, in the light. "The price of it's this. And she doesn't even know if it'll work. Nobody knows. She *said* nobody knows. She just — needs us to do something with our hands so we don't use them for anything else."

"What else would we use them for?" somebody called from the back.

"That's the question, isn't it," Nico said.

There was a ripple of laughter, rough, not quite comfortable. He waited for it to die.

"I'm not asking you to do anything stupid," he said. "I'm asking you to stop. That's all. When the Keeping shipment's due — when they come down to load the glass for the throat — we stop. We put down the cutters and we sit down in the galleries and we don't cut. Not for the Keeping. We'll cut for the Inward, we'll cut quota, we'll cut what pays the debts. But not for the dark. Not one more hand for the dark." He looked round at them. "That's it. That's the vote. Who's with me?"

They voted with their hands. It was not unanimous. It was not close.

Teo was waiting for him at the cage-head when he came up, sitting on an upturned ore bucket in her flight jacket with her arms folded, and he knew from her face that she had heard.

"You're out of your mind," she said.

"Hello, Teo."

"A *strike*. In the middle of the — Nico, do you know what Brandt's going to do?"

"Arrest me, probably." He sat down on the next bucket. He was very tired, suddenly; the whole day was in his shoulders. "Fennick lost his hand."

"I heard."

"They told him to go fast. For the Keeping."

"I *heard*, Nico." She rubbed her face. "Listen to me. Six weeks. That's all it is. Six weeks and the *Providence* sails and the Narrowing comes and goes and the whole thing's over one way or another. Keep your head down for six weeks. Please. For me."

"And then what? Then the Concern's gone and she's still here and the debts are still here and we're still cutting, and nothing's changed except we did what we were told one more time." He looked at her. "You know what she's doing. You know it better than me. You fly the lanes, you've seen the Needle. Does it look *hungry* to you?"

Teo did not answer. She was thinking, he could see, of something. Her hand had gone, the way it always did, to the bead at her throat.

Mother Salome came out of the canteen behind him with the Tally under her arm in its blanket. She stopped beside them, tall and stooped, and looked down at the two of them on their buckets for a long moment, the way she had looked at them when they were children and their mother was alive and they had been fighting over something in the passage.

"He's right," she said to Teo. "And you're right. That's the trouble with this family. You're both always right, and you never listen to each other." She shifted the Tally under her arm. "I'm going up to Fennick's wife. Somebody should tell her before the Concession does."

When she had gone, Teo said quietly, "I'm frightened for you, Nic."

"I know."

"No. You don't." She looked at the cage-head, at the quota board, at the red line. "I was at the inner markers ten days ago. In the Mercy. With the harp man — Lyle. Ambrose Lyle. He was putting a buoy out." She was quiet. "He doesn't know why it's narrowing, Nico. The man who reads it. He doesn't know. He said so. And she stood up in the Assembly two days later and told everybody it was starving."

Nico stared at her.

"I'm not saying you're right," Teo said. "I'm saying I don't know. And when I don't know, I keep my head down." She got up off the bucket. "Keep your head down. Please."

## Old Traces
[
  Place: #The Lectern
  Characters: @@Ambrose Lyle @@Bettany Shore
  Items: ^The Fork:@Ambrose Lyle ^The harp:#The Lectern
  When: 24 March 2291
]
The old traces were on paper.

The Lectern's archive was a narrow room behind the dome, lined with steel drawers, and in the bottom drawers, under fifty years of spools and crystals, were the harp records of the first Narrowings: long rolls of thermal paper from the days before the station had memory to spare, printed with the traces in faded violet ink. Ambrose had not looked at them since he wrote his paper on the Narrowing of fifty-eight, twenty years ago. Nobody had. He spent the night of the twenty-fourth on the floor of the archive with all four of them unrolled around him, weighted at the corners with coffee mugs, his reading glasses on and a lamp on the floor beside his knee, while Bettany slept at her console in the dome with her head on her arms.

He was looking for the swell.

He had listened to it every night since the buoy went out. It was there. It was not the instrument; the buoy heard it louder than the harp did, from two million kilometres closer, and the buoy and the harp were different instruments built ten years apart and agreed about nothing except what they were both hearing. A long slow rise and fall under the throat's chord, with a period of a little over four minutes. Not steady. That was the thing. The period was *drifting* — lengthening, very slightly, day by day, the way a pendulum's swing lengthens when you let out the string. He had a column of figures for it in his notebook. He did not like what the figures were starting to look like.

If it was real now, it had been real before.

The trouble with the old traces was that they were made on the first harp, the little one, the prototype he had built in Ardent and shipped through in crates before the real harp was finished; and the little harp had drifted badly. Its strings had crept, the way strings do. Every trace on those old rolls was a little out — sharp, flat, smeared — by an amount that changed from day to day. You could not lay them over each other and compare them. Everyone who had studied the old Narrowings had said so, including Ambrose, in print.

But he had tuned the little harp, every morning, to the fork.

He had forgotten that until three nights ago, lying awake in his bunk. He had tuned it every morning to the fork, and logged the tuning, and the tuning logs were on the same paper rolls, in the margins, in his own younger handwriting: *0600, ref 440, strings 3 and 7 flat 2c, corrected.* Every morning for five years. Which meant that every trace on those rolls could be referred back to the same note — to the fork, to a scream in a delivery room in Ardent — and corrected, day by day, for exactly how far the strings had drifted. It was tedious work. It took him all night. He did it with a pencil and a slide rule and the fork itself lying on the paper beside him, as if it might help. The two oldest rolls, from forty-seven and fifty-eight, had not been made on any harp of his at all; they came off the survey gravimeters the Concession had run before he arrived. But in sixty-nine the gravimeter and the little harp had recorded the same Narrowing side by side, and the gravimeter's drift could be read off against the harp, and the harp's against the fork. A chain of corrections three links long. He did that too.

At four in the morning he stopped and sat back on his heels and took his glasses off and looked at what he had done.

The swell was there. In all four.

Faint, under the drift; but there, once you had taken the drift away. The same slow rise and fall under the throat's chord. In forty-seven, in fifty-eight, in sixty-nine, in eighty. And in each of them it was at a different place in its own long cycle — he could see that now, laying the corrected traces side by side — rising in one, falling in another, near its crest in fifty-eight, near its trough in eighty. And the depth of each Narrowing followed it. Exactly. Where the swell had been high, the Narrowing had been shallow. Where it had been low, the Narrowing had been deep. Eighty, the deepest before this, had come with the swell near its lowest.

Two rhythms. That was what he was looking at. The breathing — the Narrowing, every four thousand days, regular as a clock. And under it something much slower, a long tide with a period of decades, rising and falling, so that sometimes the breath came at high water and was hardly a breath at all, and sometimes it came at low water and went deep. Two rhythms beating against each other, the way two strings a hair out of tune beat against each other, *wah-wah-wah*, louder and softer, louder and softer.

And if the long tide had been falling through eighty, it would be lower now. At its lowest. At the very bottom.

He did the sum on the back of an envelope with his hands not quite steady. He did it three times. The swell's drifting period, the slow lengthening he had been logging all week, was exactly what you would expect from a tide approaching its turn — slowing, as a pendulum slows at the top of its swing, before it comes back. If the pattern held — *if* — the long tide would turn somewhere in the last days of April. And the waist would go down, and down, to a little above twenty per cent, perhaps, on or about the first of May. And then, on its own, because the tide had turned, it would begin to come back.

He did not know that. He had four Narrowings and a hypothesis. He had a swell he could not explain and a sum on the back of an envelope. If he had seen it in a student's paper he would have written *suggestive* in the margin and sent it back. To know it he would have to watch: watch the swell's period, every night, for the next five weeks, and see whether it slowed and turned exactly when the sum said it would. If it did, he would know.

He sat on the archive floor in the lamplight among the paper rolls and the coffee mugs, and the fork lay on the paper beside his knee, and he thought: *nobody has to fly into it.* And then, more carefully: *nobody may have to.*

He picked up a clean sheet of paper and wrote at the top of it, *Dear Clare*. He had begun a great many letters that way over the years. He had sent very few of them. He wrote:

*I think I have found something. I can't tell anybody yet, because I'm not sure, and because the person I'd have to tell first is not someone who would use it kindly. So I'm telling you, because you are a long way away and won't read this for months, if I send it, which I probably won't. The Needle breathes, love. It has two breaths, a short one and a long one, and they beat against each other like strings. Your note is what let me hear it. The note you were born screaming, that I tuned everything to. I don't know if that makes any sense. It is four in the morning.*

*I'm coming home on the Providence. I'll bring the fork. I'll explain.*

He folded the letter and put it in his breast pocket with the others, and put the fork in the pocket of his jacket, and went out into the dome, where the harp was singing its thin high strained chord under the bruise of the mouth and Bettany was asleep at her console with her cheek on her slate. He stood and listened to the harp for a while. Then he woke her, gently, and sent her to bed, and sat down at his own console and opened a new file and named it, after some thought, *SWELL*, and locked it to his own key.

## The Brannock
[
  Place: #Dock Four
  Characters: @@Teo Kesh @@Corrigan Dace @@Lulu Dace @@Ambrose Lyle
  Items: ^Brannock:@Corrigan Dace
  Relationships: ~Teo Kesh/Corrigan Dace:Old lovers, old friends
  When: 26 March 2291

They were cutting a cradle into the Brannock's belly when Teo came onto Dock Four, and she knew before she saw Corrigan's face.

The tug sat in her cradle with her undersides opened up and a team of Concern fitters crawling over her with torches, welding a frame of new steel girders into the space where her sled-coupling had been. A cradle. For a load, carried under, released by a lever. Teo had seen the drawing on the dispatch board that morning, pinned up beside the notice: *TRIAL KEEPING. ONE VESSEL. VOLUNTEER PILOT ACCEPTED.* She had read it and gone cold and told herself it was somebody else.

He was sitting on a crate at the edge of the dock with Lulu beside him, eating fried salt-eel out of a paper cone and watching the fitters work. He saw Teo coming and stood up and wiped his hands on his coveralls and did not try to look as if it was anything but what it was.

"Lulu," he said, "go and see if Fat Diz's boy is on the dock office, would you? Ask him if he's got any bolts."

Lulu looked at her father and then at Teo, with the expression of a nine-year-old who knows exactly why she is being sent away, and went.

"You signed," Teo said.

"I signed."

"The *trial*. Corrigan. The trial. Not even the Keeping. She's sending one ship in, in the middle of the Narrowing, to see what happens. To *see*. You're the experiment."

"I'm the experiment," Corrigan agreed. "Experiment gets the same as everybody else. Debts cancelled in full. Mine, Lulu's, my father's. It's in writing. I've got it in writing, Teo, with the sunburst on." He patted his chest pocket. "And the trial goes on the second of April, so I don't have to sit around for five weeks thinking about it. I just go."

"You might not come back."

"I might not come back," he agreed again, gently. "I know that."

She wanted to hit him. She stood on the dock with the fitters' torches spitting behind him and wanted to hit him so badly that her hands shook. "Then *why*?"

He was quiet for a while. He looked over at the Brannock, at her opened belly and the new steel going in.

"I'm tired, Teo," he said.

"Everybody's tired."

"No. I mean — " He rubbed the back of his neck. "I'm forty. I've been flying glass since I was seventeen. I owe more now than I did when I started. Mira's been dead four years and I'm still paying for her dying. Lulu's going to start paying for it the day I stop. That's — that's it. That's the whole of my life, laid out in a line, and I can see the end of it from here." He looked at her. "And somebody's just come along and said, *fly one run, Corr. One. And none of that's true any more.* One run. And maybe I come back and maybe I don't, but either way, Lulu's free. Either way. Do you know what that's worth? You do. You know exactly what that's worth. You've got a chit on your console too."

She did know. That was the worst of it. She could feel it in her own chest like a hand: *cancelled in full.* Forty thousand six hundred and eighty marks, even after Lyle's six hundred. Struck off. Gone.

"I flew the inner markers with Lyle," she said. "The harp man. In the Mercy. You know what it's like out there? The shear? I nearly lost her. I nearly went *in*. And that was the edge, Corr. That was two weeks ago. It's narrower now."

"Then I'll be careful."

"You'll be — " She stopped. She found that she was crying, which she had not done in front of him in eleven years, and was furious about it. "You'll be *careful*."

He came and put his arms round her, there on the dock, with the fitters looking and pretending not to, and she let him, with her face in his shoulder, which smelled of salt-eel and coolant and him.

"Look after Lulu," he said into her hair. "If."

"Don't."

"If," he said. "Just if."

She went to the Lectern that evening. She had never been up the spindle before; contract pilots did not go to the Needle-ward end. Nobody stopped her. She found the dome by following the sound — a thin high strained singing that got louder as she climbed — and found Ambrose Lyle alone at a console under the bruise of the mouth with his headphones round his neck and a column of figures on his screen.

He looked up and saw her and his face did something complicated.

"Miss Kesh."

"Teo."

"Teo." He stood. "Is something wrong?"

"Corrigan Dace has volunteered for the trial," she said. "The Brannock. Second of April. He's my — he's a friend. I need to know. You're the one who'd know." She heard her voice and steadied it. "How dangerous is it? Really?"

Ambrose looked at her. He looked, she thought, like a man who had been asked a question he had been dreading, and had decided, somewhere in the dread, that he was going to answer it honestly.

"Very," he said. "The waist on the second will be about thirty-five per cent of nominal, if the trend holds. No one has ever flown a throat that narrow. The shear at the waist will be — I don't know. Worse than anything you saw at the inner markers. Much worse." He paused. "And the glass. When he releases it. Nobody knows what brine-glass does when you put it into the throat at the waist. Nobody's ever done it. It might do nothing. It might — " He spread his hands. "It's negative energy, Teo. Raw. In a place where everything is already bent. I don't know what it does."

"So he might die for nothing."

Ambrose was quiet.

"Is there anything," she said, "anything at all, that you know, that would stop it? That would make her call it off?"

Something moved in his face. She saw it. She saw him almost say something, and stop, and look away from her at the screen with its column of figures, and back.

"I don't know yet," he said. "I'm sorry. I don't know yet."

## Who Else
[
  Place: #The Director's Gallery
  Characters: @@Ambrose Lyle @@Hesper Vane
  When: 27 March 2291
]
He told her the next morning, because of Teo Kesh's face.

He had not meant to. He had meant to wait five weeks and watch and know. But he had lain awake all night with the pilot's question in his ears — *is there anything at all that you know* — and the honest answer was that there was, a little; that there was a sum on the back of an envelope and a swell in four old paper traces, and that a man was going to fly into the Needle in six days and it might be for nothing. So at nine in the morning he went up to the Gallery, without an appointment, and Hesper Vane's secretary looked at his face and let him in.

He told her everything. The swell. The old traces, the fork, the drift corrected. The two rhythms. The long tide at its bottom. He laid the envelope on her desk with the sum on it. He told her it was not proven; that it was a hypothesis; that it would take five weeks to know. And he told her that if it was right, the Needle would reach its narrowest on or about the first of May and then begin to recover on its own, whatever anyone did, and that therefore there was no reason to send Corrigan Dace into the throat on the second of April. Or anyone. Ever.

She listened to all of it without interrupting, as she always did. She did not touch the envelope. When he had finished she sat for a while with her hands folded on the desk, looking at it.

"Who else have you told?" she said.

He blinked. "No one."

"Miss Shore?"

"No. She knows I've been looking at the old traces. Not why. Not what I found."

"Your daughter?"

He thought of the letter in his breast pocket, unsent, with the others. "No."

"Good," said Hesper Vane.

She picked up the envelope then, and turned it over, and looked at the sum on the back, and Ambrose watched her read it and understood, slowly and with a sensation like cold water rising in a room, that she was following it. All of it. Every step.

"If this is right," she said at last, "the waist will narrow steadily until the end of April. And then turn."

"If it's right."

"And between now and then? Does your swell do anything else? Are there smaller turns? Places where the waist pauses, or recovers a little, before it goes on down?"

"There — " He stopped. "There's a short ripple on the long one. A few days' period. The waist narrows in steps, not smoothly. It'll pause, sometimes. Recover a point or two. Then go on down."

"When is the next pause?"

He looked at her.

"The trial is on the second of April," Hesper Vane said evenly. "If there is a pause in the narrowing at or about the second of April, then the trial will be followed by a pause in the narrowing. People will see the trial, and then they will see the throat hold. Perhaps even open, a little." She laid the envelope down. "That would be very helpful, Ambrose. You understand that? That would be extremely helpful. To everyone. It would make the Keeping work."

"It wouldn't *work*. It would — it would just be timed to — "

"It would be seen to work," she said. "Which is what matters to the people who will be asked to fly it. And to cut for it. And not to burn Anchorage down in the meantime." She looked at him with her steady grey eyes. "When is the next pause?"

He said nothing.

"Ambrose."

"I'd like to go back to the Lectern now, Director."

She held his eyes for a long moment. Then she nodded, as if he had answered.

"Of course," she said. "Thank you for bringing this to me. It's important work. I'd like you to keep working on it, and I'd like it to stay between the two of us until it's proven. Lock the file. You've locked it already, I expect. Good." She picked up her slate. "I'll ask Miss Shore for the short-term projections. She can read a trace as well as anyone, I'm told. You trained her."

He went out. He walked back down the spindle to the Lectern with his hand in his jacket pocket round the fork, and he could not afterward remember a single step of the way.
