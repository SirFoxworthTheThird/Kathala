The Lamplighter's readers slept from midnight to four, and in those four hours, every night for a week, the harp hall was Lucan's.

They were not bad readers, the two boys the Lantern had sent up. They were earnest and quick and they had learned the Hunger Tone at the Lantern House from a man who had learned it from a Warden who had left the order, and they could read a trace well enough to call a line. But they were frightened of the harp. They sat at the console all day in their black coats with their backs very straight, and called out the waist every hour in voices that cracked, and at midnight they went gratefully to their bunks in the old dormitory on the near wheel and did not come back until morning. And Prior Saar, who had given the night watch to Lucan for twelve years, had said nothing about changing it.

Lucan did not retune the whole harp. He had learned that much. He retuned one course — the ninth, the lowest, the one that had been spliced worst and that nobody would notice was singing differently under the others — to the Fork, every night at midnight, and every night at four he put it back. One course was enough. One course tuned true, if you filtered the others out, would show you the mass at the waist the way a single lamp shows you a cave.

He had spent the first two nights just looking at it.

On the third, Isra came. She came up from the greenhouse on the near wheel, where Lucan had got her taken on as a hand — the Lantern let the Wardens keep their greenhouse, and a bone-picker girl watering beans was beneath anyone's notice — and sat on the floor beside his console in the dark with her knees drawn up and the shard on its cord in her fist, and watched. She did not ask questions. She had stopped asking questions since her grandmother died. She watched the way Pike worked the grapple arm: patiently, completely, as if nothing else existed.

It was she who saw the bands.

"Can you make it bigger," she said, on the fourth night, at about two. "The edge. There. Where it's ridged."

He made it bigger. The single true course sang into the console, and the edge of the mass drew itself across the screen in pale green, and grew, and grew, until the ridges on its surface became lines, and the lines became layers, packed one on another, thin and thick and thin, like the side of a cliff where the sea has cut it. Like the root of her shard.

"There," said Isra. "Those."

"I see them."

"Count them."

He looked at her.

"Count them," she said. "I've been counting the ones on the Returns for ten years. On the roots. You can count the years on a ship sometimes, my gran said, by the bands, like rings in old wood. I never knew if it was true." She did not look away from the screen. "Count them."

It took him the rest of that night and most of the next. The layers were very fine near the bottom of the mass, so fine that the single course could barely separate them, and he had to sit with his ear pressed to the headphones and the filter narrowed to a thread, listening to each band ring its own tiny note as the throat's noise went over it, and mark each one with a pencil on the screen's glass. Isra sat beside him and kept the count. She did it on her fingers, and then on a scrap of paper, and then, when they lost their place, on the floor beside her with a piece of charcoal from one of the nine little lamps that had burned round Hanne.

At ten past three on the fifth of June, with the Lamplighter's boys due back in fifty minutes, Lucan took off the headphones and sat back.

"Fifty-nine," said Isra.

He did not answer.

"Fifty-nine. Lucan. And the Sixtieth's in eighteen days."

"I know."

He was looking at the screen. At the bands, marked in pencil. They were not all the same. That was the thing he could not stop looking at. Some were fine as a hair. Some were thick. And the thick ones were not anywhere. They were at the top, the newest, the outermost — two of them, very thick, laid down on everything else like two coats of paint — and in the middle, a long run of medium bands, steady, one after another, thirty or so of them; and then, below those, nearer the bottom, a stretch where the bands thinned out again and became irregular and faint, some barely there at all.

He got up and went to the shelf at the side of the hall where the order kept its books — the few that had survived the Quiet, the daybooks from 2741 onward — and took down the Book of Keepings, the order's own record, which listed every Keeping it knew of with the number of skiffs that flew it, as far back as the books went. He brought it back and opened it on the floor beside the charcoal marks.

*Fifty-Ninth: seven skiffs.* The outermost band. Thick.

*Fifty-Eighth: nine.* The band beneath. Thick.

*Fifty-Seventh: two. Fifty-Sixth: one. Fifty-Fifth: none flown.* Three bands — and the third, when he looked, so faint he had almost not marked it at all.

He went down the book, band by band, with Isra watching, and it was there all the way down. Where the order's records said many skiffs, the band was thick. Where they said few, it was thin. Where they said none, it was a ghost: barely a line, as if the throat had laid down only what little glass had drifted in by itself. Below the Forty-Second, where the order's books stopped, he could not check it any more; but the long run of medium bands went on down, steady, thirty-odd of them, one after another, exactly as even as you would expect if, for three hundred years before the Quiet, somebody had been flying the same number of skiffs into the throat every four thousand days.

And at the very bottom of the mass, against the throat's own wall, there was one more band. Very thin. Almost nothing.

"The first," said Isra.

"The first," said Lucan.

He sat down on the floor beside the Book of Keepings. He found that his hands were shaking and put them flat on his knees.

"It's not starving," he said. "It never was. It's not even sick. It's — " He stopped. "We did this. Every Keeping. Every skiff. Every gram of glass we've ever flown in there to feed it. It didn't feed anything. It *stayed*. It stuck to the waist and grew, layer on layer, Keeping after Keeping, three hundred years, six hundred years, until there was no room left for the door." He looked at the screen. "We've been choking it. The Keeping is what's killing it."

Isra did not say anything for a long time. She was looking at the two thick bands at the top. The Lamplighter's.

"And she's going to give it a hundred," she said.

"Yes."

"Every gram in the Reach."

"Yes."

"How thick does that make the band, Lucan?" Her voice was very quiet. "A hundred skiffs. How thick."

He did not have to work it out. He had already worked it out, in the back of his mind, while she counted.

"Thick enough," he said.

## The Bell

"It rings," said Isra.

It was the next night. They had not slept. Lucan had spent the day calling numbers beside the Lamplighter's two boys with his face perfectly composed and his mind somewhere else entirely, and Isra had spent it watering beans and thinking, and at midnight she had come up to the hall and sat down beside him on the floor and said it before he had even finished retuning the ninth course.

"What does?"

"The thing. The glass. In the throat." She held up her shard. "It rings. You heard it. The first night you tuned to the Fork. Like a bell. The whole station could hear it." She turned the shard in her fingers. "And this sings at the cittern. At the Fork. It's the same note. It's all the same note. The glass in the salt, the glass in the throat, the Fork, the low string — it's one note, Lucan. Everything's tuned to it."

"Yes."

"So what happens if you ring it?"

He looked at her.

"Really ring it," she said. "Not listen to it. Ring it. Hit it with its own note, as hard as you can, at the right time." She was speaking faster now. "The Returns come out when the throat squeezes. That's what you said. Every Narrowing, it squeezes, and things that are stuck get pushed loose, and come out with the glass grown on them backwards. So it's already trying. Every four thousand days it tries to push the stuff out and it can't, it only gets a few. What if you helped it? What if, at the Narrowing, when it's squeezing hardest, you rang the whole mass at its own note — " She brought her two fists together, hard, in front of her. "Glass breaks, Lucan. You hit glass at its own note, it breaks. Every cutter knows that. That's what a burst is. That's what took Fennick's hand, in the old song. You go too fast at the wrong note and it lets go."

Lucan sat very still on the floor.

"The harp's a listener," he said slowly. "Not a — it picks up. It doesn't put out. And it's two million kilometres from the waist. You couldn't ring a teacup from here."

"Then you need something closer."

They both turned their heads at the same moment and looked at the telescope.

It took him an hour to find the Bell again. It had not moved. It never moved. A small grey cylinder hanging in the glow at the very edge of the mouth, where the pull held it, nose toward the dark, its lattice of wires at the tail bent and furred with frost. He had looked at it a hundred times and thought of it as a ruin, a curiosity, a thing the old Wardens had made a story about. He looked at it now through the brass eyepiece for a long time.

"It's a little harp," he said. "That's what the old books say. A listening buoy. Built to sit right at the mouth and hear the throat close. Built by — " He stopped. "Built by whoever built *this*." He put his hand on the gold frame beside him. "Same maker. It must be. Nobody else could have."

"Can it ring? Instead of listen?"

"I don't know." He was still looking through the eyepiece. "The harp can. Not much. There's a — there's a driver in the frame. Old. I found it years ago when I was restringing and I never knew what it was for. A coil, under the base, that can set the wires going instead of only hearing them. If the buoy was built the same way — " He stopped again, and his breath went out of him slowly, and he stepped back from the telescope.

"Look at the end of it," he said. "The tail. Where the wires come out of the end-plate. In the middle of the plate. Look."

She looked. It took her a while. The frost, the bent lattice, the grey glow. And then she saw it: a small dark hole in the centre of the end-plate, round, neat, deliberate, about the width of a finger. Like a keyhole.

"What's that?"

"I don't know," said Lucan. "But I know how wide it is." He took the Fork out of his sleeve and held it up, stem first, in the lamplight. "I've been staring at that socket for twelve years. I always thought it was a mounting bolt." He turned the Fork's stem slowly. "It's not a bolt. It's a cradle. It's the right size, Isra. It's exactly the right size."

She took her eye from the telescope and looked at him, and at the Fork in his hand, and at the harp behind him, and at the shard in her own fist. All one note.

"He built it to take that," she said. "Whoever it was. He built the buoy with a place for the Fork in it. To tune it."

"Or to wake it." Lucan was very pale. "If the Bell's been sitting out there dead for six hundred years because nobody's put the Fork back in it — "

"Then somebody has to go and put it back."

They looked at each other.

"At the Narrowing," Lucan said. "It has to be at the Narrowing. When the throat's squeezing. When the Returns come. That's the only time the mass is loose enough to — " He was thinking aloud. "The Bell's right at the mouth. Somebody would have to fly to it in the Long Night, in the shear, with the Keeping fleet going past, and put the Fork in it, and give it power — it'll have none, after six hundred years, it'll need a ship's power, a cable — and then somebody here would have to drive the harp at the same time, tuned true, through the coil, at the Bell. So they ring together. The harp to wake it and the Bell to throw it. And keep it up. For as long as it takes."

"How long does it take?"

"I have no idea," said Lucan. "Nobody's ever done it. Nobody's ever *thought* of it." He laughed suddenly, a short cracked laugh. "And I'm going to be on her ship. I said yes. I'm going to be on the Lamplighter's flagship at the head of a hundred skiffs, giving her the line. I can't be here at the harp."

"Then you'll give her a bad line," said Isra. "Slow. You'll hold them at the markers. Tell her the shear's too heavy, tell her to wait for a better wave. You're her reader. She trusts you. She said so. Hold her as long as you can." She was not looking at him. She was looking at the telescope. "And I'll take the Gristle to the Bell."

"Isra — "

"I've been flying the Ossuary for ten years. I know the shear at the mouth better than anyone alive. Better than you. Better than her boys." She held out her hand. "Give me the Fork."

"Not yet," said Lucan. He put it back in his sleeve. "Not yet. I have to get the coil working first. And someone has to be at the harp." He looked at her. "Someone who can tune it true and hold it. For as long as it takes. While I'm on her ship lying to her."

"Who?"

He did not answer. He looked at the iron door, toward the passage, toward the cells, where twelve old men in grey were sleeping in a station built for six thousand.

## Pike

Pike told her the next morning, in the greenhouse, among the beans.

He had been in the Wardens' infirmary for ten days with his ribs strapped, and the Wardens' old doctor had given him something for the cough that worked a little and something for the pain that worked more, and he had been sitting up every day in the greenhouse in a chair by the lamps, because he said the green smelled like nothing he could remember. She came in at seven with her watering can and he was in his chair. He had a black coat over his knees. He had a bead on a cord round his neck.

She stopped in the doorway.

"Sit down," said Pike.

"What's that."

"Sit down, Isra."

She did not sit down. She stood in the doorway with the watering can in her hand and looked at the bead at his throat, a nub of cloudy glass on a black cord, lying very slightly to the left. Toward the mouth.

"The Deacon came up last night," Pike said. "With the list. For the Keeping. They've got eighty-one. They want a hundred. He asked the infirmary if any of us wanted to give ourselves." He looked down at the coat on his knees. "I said yes."

"No."

"I've said it, girl."

"*No.*" She put the watering can down, very carefully, because otherwise she would have thrown it. "You can't. Pike. You can't. You don't know — I didn't tell you, I didn't tell you what we found, Lucan and me, I should have, I didn't want you to — " She was across the greenhouse, she was on her knees beside his chair, she had his big ruined hand in both hers. "It's choking. The Needle. It's not hungry, it's *full*. It's full of glass. Every Keeping makes it worse. Every skiff. We counted, Pike, we counted the layers, there's one for every Keeping, there's fifty-nine of them and they're killing it — " She was crying. She did not care. "If you go in with a cradle of glass you're not saving it, you're making it worse. You're one more layer. You'll be one more layer, Pike, you'll be — "

"I know," said Pike.

She stopped.

"I heard you," he said. "In the hall. At night. You two think you're quiet." He turned his hand over in hers, and held hers back, very gently, the way he held the grapple controls. "I've been lying in that infirmary listening through the vents to the two of you counting. Fifty-nine." He was quiet for a while. "I believe you. For what it's worth. I believe you."

"Then *why*."

He looked out at the beans, at the green, at the lamps.

"Couple of reasons," he said. "My sister's got three kids in the Fourth Terrace. The Lantern's promised them rations till they're grown, if I go. They'll keep that. They keep that sort of thing; it's how they get the next hundred." He coughed, and waited for it to stop. "And I'm dying, Isra. You know I am. The doctor here's an honest old man. He gives me till the autumn, if I'm lucky. I'd rather not do the autumn. I've seen the autumn. My father did the autumn."

"That's not a reason."

"No," he agreed. "It's not." He was quiet for a long time. "The boy," he said.

She went still.

"At the Fifty-Ninth. The one who turned round. *I don't want to. I'm sorry. I don't want to.*" He said it in a flat soft voice, exactly as he had said it in Hanne's room. "I was on the second gun. I've been thinking for eleven years that somebody ought to go in for him. Into the place he didn't want to go. So he's not there on his own." He looked at her. "That's stupid. I know it's stupid. I know it'll make the glass thicker and I know it won't help him and I know he's not anywhere, he's just dead. I know. It's not a reason. It's just what I've got."

Isra knelt on the greenhouse floor with his hand in hers and could not speak.

"Do your bell," said Pike. "You and the Warden boy. Hanne's note. Do it. I heard that too." Something moved in his beard that might have been a smile. "I'll be out there in a skiff with a cradle of glass and a bead on. If you ring the thing, and it works, and I'm not in yet — " He shrugged, carefully, with his strapped ribs. "Then maybe I don't go in. Maybe I turn round. Maybe I'm the one who turns round, this time." He looked down at her. "And there's nobody on the second gun."

"And if it doesn't work?"

"Then I'm one more layer," said Ostap Pike. "I've been one more layer all my life, girl. I'm used to it."

He let go of her hand and put the black coat round his shoulders, slowly, with his ribs, and got up out of the chair, and stood for a moment among the beans in the green light, huge and stooped and grey, with the bead at his throat.

"They want us at the Lantern House tonight," he said. "For the blessing. All the Keepers. On the shuttle. I'll see you on the night, Isra." He touched her hair, once, the way Hanne used to. "Mind the shear at the mouth. It comes from behind."
