## Charter
[
  Place: #Dock Four
  Characters: @@Teo Kesh @@Ambrose Lyle
  Items: ^The sounding buoy:@Ambrose Lyle ^The Fork:@Ambrose Lyle
  Relationships: ~Teo Kesh/Ambrose Lyle:Pilot and passenger
  When: 13 March 2291
]
The charter came through on the dispatch board at five in the morning, which was how Teo knew it was going to be bad.

*PRIORITY CHARTER. TUG AND PILOT, ONE DAY. DOCK FOUR, 0800. PASSENGER AND CARGO TO THROAT MOUTH, INNER MARKERS. RATE: 600 MARKS AGAINST INDENTURE.*

She read it three times on the little screen in her bunk-cube in the pilots' dorm on Anchorage, with the cube's one bulb buzzing over her head and somebody being sick in the cube next door. Six hundred marks. She had never seen a rate like it. Six hundred marks was three months of glass runs. Six hundred marks was a whole column on the chit. And *inner markers*, which meant past the last beacon, past the place where the lanes stopped and the throat began, into the zone where the tidal shear came off the mouth in long invisible waves and tore at your hull like a dog at a rope. Nobody went to the inner markers in a tug. You went to the inner markers in a throat-hauler with a reader aboard and shear-plating three hands thick.

She was at Dock Four at seven-forty.

The passenger was already there. She had expected a Concern technician, some bright young thing from the Lectern with a slate and an attitude. What she got was an old man in a cardigan, sitting on a packing crate on the dock floor with his hands between his knees and a pair of reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, looking up at the Small Mercy in her cradle with an expression of mild, polite concern, as if she were a dog that might or might not bite.

"Miss Kesh?" He stood up when he saw her. He was taller than she had thought. "Ambrose Lyle. From the Lectern. I'm told you're the best tug pilot on the station."

"Who told you that?"

"The dispatch office. I asked them who would actually be able to do it, rather than who was free, and they gave me your name, and then they gave me a long list of reasons I shouldn't hire you, and then they said that was the same thing." He smiled, slightly. "I'm sorry about the hour."

"You're the harp man."

"I'm the harp man."

She looked at him. She had heard of him all her life without ever seeing him, the way you hear of the Director or the Board: the man who read the Needle. The man who told the pilots where the throat would be. Every through-pilot on the station had his charts on their boards and swore by them, and she had sometimes thought of him as a sort of oracle in a dome, remote and terrible. He had a coffee stain on his cardigan.

"What's the cargo?" she said.

He showed her. It was in the crate he had been sitting on: a long cylinder of dull grey alloy two metres from end to end, with a cluster of short fins at one end and a lattice of fine wires at the other, strung on a frame like the ribs of an umbrella. It looked hand-made. It looked very carefully hand-made.

"It's a sounding buoy," he said. "It listens to the throat, the way the harp does. Only from much closer. The harp is a long way back, and the Narrowing — " He stopped. "The Narrowing is doing things I can't hear properly from the Lectern. I want ears at the mouth. Right at the edge, as close as I can get it. If I can put it at the inner markers it'll sit there in the mouth's pull and sing to me for years."

"You want me to fly into the shear and throw that out the airlock."

"I want you to fly into the shear and let me throw it out the airlock," he said. "I'll do the throwing. I would very much like you to do the flying."

She almost laughed. She did not. "Why me? You've got a dozen throat-haulers with shear-plate sitting in the yards with nowhere to go. The lanes are closed half of every shift."

"The throat-haulers belong to the Concern," Ambrose Lyle said, "and the Concern would like to know why I want to go, and I'd rather not tell it until I know what I'm going to find." He looked at her over the buoy. "You're contract. You belong to yourself."

"I belong to the Concern to the tune of forty-one thousand marks."

"Yes," he said gently. "But not to the Director's office. Not this morning."

Teo thought about that for a while.

"Six hundred," she said.

"Six hundred."

"And you sign a waiver that if you die it was your idea."

"I'll write it out by hand," he said, and she believed him.

They loaded the buoy into the Mercy's little cargo bay together, with the dock's chain-hoist and a lot of swearing on her part and a lot of apologising on his. When it was in its straps he did a thing she did not expect. He took out of his jacket pocket a fork — a tuning fork, the brass kind, with a long stem — and struck it against the heel of his hand, and fitted the stem into a socket in the buoy's end-plate that she had taken for a mounting bolt. It slid in like a key. The fine wires of the lattice, which had been still, began very faintly to hum. He bent close to them with his head on one side, listening, for a long minute, and turned a screw somewhere inside the frame with a jeweller's driver, and listened again. Then he drew the fork out of its socket and the humming died, and he wiped the fork on his cardigan and put it back in his pocket.

"What was that?" she said.

"Calibration. Everything has to be tuned to something." He patted his pocket. "That's what everything I've built is tuned to. The harp, the buoy, all of it. So that they all agree with one another about what they're hearing."

"What if you lose it?"

He looked startled, and then amused, and then something else. "Then I suppose nothing would agree with anything," he said. "I try not to lose it."

She had seen the engraving on the stem when he wiped it. *FOR CLARE.*

"Who's Clare?" she said, and immediately wished she had not, because his face changed.

"My daughter," he said. "She's in Ardent. I haven't seen her since she was four." He did not look at her. "I'm going home on the *Providence*. In April. I'm going to give it to her."

"That's a strange present."

"Yes," he said. "She'll think so too."

## The Inner Markers
[
  Place: #The Needle
  Characters: @@Teo Kesh @@Ambrose Lyle
  Items: ^The sounding buoy:#The Needle ^Teo's bead:@Teo Kesh
  When: same day
]
Past the last beacon the lanes ended and the sky went strange.

Teo had been out to the outer markers a hundred times, hauling sleds for the throat-haulers to pick up, and she knew the way the stars began to slide there, the first long smears at the edge of the canopy. Past the inner markers it was another country. The Needle's mouth filled the whole forward canopy and spilled over its edges, a vast curved dark that was not dark, that seemed to lean toward you the longer you looked at it, and around its rim the starlight was not smeared but *wound* — wrapped round and round the mouth in tight bright coils, so that the whole sky seemed to be draining into it like water into a sink. The Eye was not a ring of light any more. It was a wall. It went up and up and curved over the top of the canopy and down behind them, faintly luminous, grey-gold, like the inside of a shell.

The Small Mercy did not like it. Teo could feel her not liking it, through the seat and the yoke and the soles of her feet: a long low shudder that came and went, came and went, as the shear-waves rolled off the mouth and broke over the hull.

"Talk to me," she said.

Ambrose was strapped into the jump-seat behind her with a pair of old cracked headphones on, plugged into a portable reader on his knee. He had his eyes closed. "The next one's about forty seconds out. It's a big one. From your right, up, about two o'clock."

"How do you know?"

"I can hear it coming."

She looked at him in the mirror. He was not joking. His face was very still and intent, the face of a man listening to music in another room.

"Thirty seconds."

She put the Mercy's nose round toward two o'clock and up, the way you would turn into a wave in a boat, and fed the mains, and waited, and at twenty-nine seconds the shear hit them like the side of a building. The tug lurched. Something in the cargo bay banged against its straps. The whole canopy filled with a flash of grey-gold light as the hull caught the edge of the wave, and the yoke bucked in her hands, and then they were through it, wallowing, upright.

"Good," said Ambrose, without opening his eyes. "That was very good. The next is smaller. Fifty seconds. Dead ahead."

They went in like that, wave by wave, for half an hour. He called them and she flew them. She had never flown with a reader before. She had always thought of readers as a kind of priest, men in domes who drew charts and sent them down to the docks on slates, and she had not understood that you could fly with one beside you, that it was like flying with a second pair of eyes that saw in a direction your own did not. By the tenth wave she was turning into them before he finished speaking. By the twentieth she had stopped being frightened, mostly, and started being something else, something that made her grin into her helmet like an idiot.

"You've done this before," she said.

"Not for twenty years. I used to fly with the throat-haulers when the Lectern was new. Before they decided I was too valuable to risk." He opened his eyes. "You're very good, Miss Kesh."

"I know."

"I mean it. They told me you were good. They didn't tell me you were — " He seemed to look for the word. "Attentive."

"I'm a tug pilot," she said. "You fly forty tonnes of sled on a bad tow, you get attentive or you get dead."

The bead was pulling.

She noticed it somewhere in the last few minutes, the way you notice a sound that has been there for a while. The little nub of glass at her throat had swung on its cord and was lying, not against her breastbone, but out from it, a finger's width from her skin, the cord stretched taut, leaning toward the mouth like a dog straining at a leash. She put her hand over it. It pressed into her palm, very gently, insistently.

"Mr Lyle."

"Ambrose."

"Ambrose. Does glass — " She felt stupid. "My brother's a cutter. He says glass knows where the Needle is."

He leaned forward in the jump-seat and looked at the bead in her hand, and then at her, and for a moment he looked so interested that she forgot to feel stupid.

"Your brother is right," he said. "It does. It's the same stuff, you see — the throat and the glass. Negative energy. That's what holds the Needle open: a ring of something like brine-glass, only stranger, at the waist, pushing outward. The glass in the salt is a kind of — cousin. It feels the throat the way a compass feels a pole." He smiled. "Cutters have known that for fifty years. Physicists keep rediscovering it and writing papers."

"Is that why it's narrowing? The ring at the waist?"

The smile went. "I don't know why it's narrowing," he said. "That's why we're here."

They came to the inner markers — two old beacon buoys, dark, dead for decades, hanging in the grey-gold glow like drowned men — and Teo brought the Mercy round between them and held her there, heading into the shear, mains just feathering, while the tug shuddered and wallowed and the wall of light leaned over them.

"Here?"

"Here. Hold her as steady as you can."

He went back to the cargo bay. She heard him in there, the clank of straps, the grunt of a man too old for this hauling something heavy. She heard the inner airlock cycle and the outer one open. Then she felt the buoy go — a small lurch as the mass left the tug — and in her rear camera she saw it tumble away from the Mercy's stern, a little grey cylinder turning end over end, slower and slower, until the mouth's pull caught it and it stopped turning and hung there, quite still, nose to the dark, as if it were listening.

Its lattice of wires opened, like an umbrella. Like a flower.

Ambrose came back up and dropped into the jump-seat and pulled his headphones on, and plugged the reader into something, and closed his eyes, and his face changed.

"Oh," he said.

"What?"

He did not answer. He was listening. She watched him in the mirror. She watched the colour go out of his face and come back, and his lips move as if he were counting, and his hands, which had been loose on his knees, close slowly into fists.

"What is it?" she said. "Ambrose. What is it?"

"It's — " He opened his eyes. They were wet. "It's beautiful. I didn't know. I didn't know it sounded like this up close. I've spent twenty-three years listening to it from the back of the room." He gave a sort of laugh. "And the waist is going down. It's going down faster than the harp says. The harp's too far away. It smooths everything out." He listened again. "It's like a drawstring. Like someone's pulling a drawstring. Very slowly. Very evenly."

"Can it close?"

"I don't know." He took the headphones off. "But there's — there's something else. Under it. I heard it in the Lectern the other night and I thought it was the instrument. It isn't the instrument. It's here too, it's louder here. A kind of — "

The shear hit them from behind.

There had been no warning. He had had his headphones off. It came out of the mouth at their stern like a fist, and the Small Mercy went over on her side and then on her back and the canopy was full of grey-gold light and then of stars and then of light again, and the proximity alarm was shrieking, and somewhere behind her Ambrose had been thrown against his straps and was saying something she could not hear. The yoke was dead in her hands. The tug was tumbling toward the dark — she could see it in the canopy each time they came round, the bruise, nearer, nearer, the coils of starlight going past like the walls of a drain.

She did not think. She cut the mains, and fired the starboard attitude jets in one long burn until the tumble slowed to a spin and the spin to a wallow, and then she put the nose, not away from the mouth, but along it — skimming the edge of the wall of light, letting the pull take her round in a long falling curve the way you let a current take a boat round a rock — and when the curve brought her nose round to face the inner markers again she gave the mains everything they had and a little more, and the Small Mercy screamed and shuddered and clawed her way up out of the pull, metre by metre, until the wall of light fell away behind them and the shear-waves were only waves again, and then only shudders, and then they were past the last beacon and in the lanes and it was over.

She sat with her hands on the yoke. They were shaking. She could not make them stop.

Behind her, Ambrose Lyle was laughing. It was not a happy laugh, exactly. It was the laugh of a man who has been very frightened and has found himself alive.

"Six hundred marks," he said. "You could have died for six hundred marks, Miss Kesh."

"I die for less than that every day," said Teo. "Usually it's slower."

He stopped laughing. In the mirror she saw him looking at her, really looking, as if he had just seen something he had not expected to see.

"Thank you," he said.

"Write the waiver," she said, and her voice came out wrong, and she turned the Mercy for home.

## The Tithe
[
  Place: #The Assembly Hall
  Characters: @@Teo Kesh @@Nico Kesh @@Corrigan Dace @@Salome Okafor @@Hesper Vane @@Okoro Brandt
  When: 15 March 2291
]
The Assembly Hall in Tallow was the oldest room in the Reach: a cavern cut into the salt by the first Concession machines, its walls still scored with their long curved grooves, its ceiling so low at the back that tall men stooped. Nobody had built anything in it. Nobody had put up a gallery or a terrace or dressed the salt with stone. There were benches, and a platform at the front, and a single bank of floodlights that made everyone look ill, and that was all. The Concession held its notices here, and its inquiries, and its debt hearings, and on the fifteenth of March it held the Director's address, and the room was so full that people stood in the doorways and sat on the floor between the benches and climbed on each other's shoulders at the back.

Teo stood against the wall halfway down, with Corrigan beside her and Nico on her other side, and watched Hesper Vane walk out onto the platform without any notes.

She did not hurry. She walked to the front of the platform and stood there with her hands folded and waited for the room to be quiet, and it took a long time, and she waited, until it was.

"You know there is a Narrowing," she said. "I told you so on the second. I told you it would be deep. I'm here to tell you how deep."

She told them. She told them in numbers. She did it the way Teo imagined she did everything, precisely and in order, without softening anything: the waist at sixty-one per cent of nominal on the second, at fifty-two on the ninth, at forty-eight this morning. The rate of narrowing. The projections. She said that the Concession's throat-readers did not know whether the waist would stop narrowing before it reached zero. She said that if it reached zero the Needle would close, and that a closed Needle had never been observed, and that no one could say whether it would ever open again.

The room had been quiet. It became something else, a silence with a held breath in it.

"I am not here to frighten you," Hesper Vane said. "I am here to tell you what we are going to do about it."

She told them that too.

She said that the throat of the Needle was held open by a ring of exotic matter at its waist — that this was known, that it had been known for fifty years. She said that brine-glass was the same kind of matter, in a cruder form. She said that the Concession's analysis indicated that the waist was narrowing because the ring was weakening: because it was, in effect, starving. And she said that if brine-glass were delivered to the waist during the Narrowing, released there at the narrowest point, it would brace the ring as a splint braces a bone, and the throat would hold.

"We will feed it," Hesper Vane said. "That is the long and the short of it. We have the glass. We are sitting on the largest deposit of brine-glass in human space. We will take it out to the Needle in the Narrowing and we will feed it to the throat, and the throat will hold. I am calling it the Keeping." She let the word sit. "It will require pilots. It will require pilots who are willing to take a small ship, loaded with glass, into the throat during the Narrowing, to the waist, and release their load there, and come back. I will not pretend to you that this is safe. It is not safe. It has never been done. It is the most dangerous flight anybody in the Reach has ever been asked to make."

She looked out over the room.

"So I will make this offer, and I will make it once. Any pilot who flies a Keeping run — any pilot who goes to the waist and releases their glass — will have their indenture cancelled. In full. Not reduced. Cancelled. And not only theirs. Their family's. Every debt held by the Concern against every member of their household, struck off the books on the day they fly." A pause. "That is my offer. Thank you."

The noise came up out of the room like water out of a burst pipe.

Teo did not hear most of it. She was looking at Hesper Vane, standing quite still on the platform with her hands folded, waiting for it to subside the way she had waited for the silence; and she was thinking of Ambrose Lyle's face in the mirror with the headphones on, two days ago, at the inner markers. *Like a drawstring. Very slowly. Very evenly.* He had not said anything about starving. He had not said anything about a ring weakening. He had said *I don't know why it's narrowing*, and he had been the man who would know.

"Feed it," said someone at the front, very loud. Mother Salome. She had stood up on her bench, tall and stooped, a head above everyone round her, and the floodlights made her face look like a carving. "You want to feed it. Feed it what, Director? Our glass, you said. Our glass, that our children cut out of the salt and die of. Our pilots, that you'll send into the dark in little ships. You want us to feed it our children."

"I want you to keep it open," said Hesper Vane. "So that your children have a door."

"A door for what? Your ships leave in six weeks. What's on the other side of that door for us, Director? The Board? Ardent? The people who hold our paper?"

"Everything is on the other side of that door," Hesper Vane said evenly. "Medicine. Machines. Seed stock. Every ship that will ever come to the Reach. Every ship that will ever leave it. If the Needle closes, Mother, there is no other side. There is the Reach, alone, forever. Ask yourself whether your children would thank you for that."

Salome stood on her bench and said nothing. Teo had never seen her with nothing to say.

"It's a lie," said Nico.

He said it quite quietly, beside Teo, and then much louder, and then he was on the bench too, and the cutters round him were looking up at him with their faces lit by the floodlights. "It's a lie. She doesn't know it's starving. Nobody knows. She's just — she's *saying* it. She needs the glass shipped and she needs us scared and she needs us to — "

"Nico," Teo said. "Get down."

"— to keep sending it after they've *gone*, that's what this is, it's a way of keeping us — "

Marshal Brandt's men came through the crowd from the doorway, four of them, big, unhurried, in the grey of Concern Security, and the crowd parted for them the way water parts for a hull. Nico saw them coming and kept talking. He was still talking when they reached him. Two of them took his arms and lifted him down off the bench as easily as lifting a child, and he kicked and shouted, and one of the cutters beside him swung a fist, and Brandt himself, who had come up behind his men, put out one large hand and caught the fist in mid-air and held it, without apparent effort, and looked at the cutter until he lowered his eyes.

"Out," said Brandt. "All of you. Now. Quietly."

They took Nico out. Teo went after him. She shoved her way along the wall with Corrigan behind her, and caught them in the passage outside, where the Security men had put Nico against the salt and were going through his pockets. Brandt was watching. When he saw Teo he raised one finger, not to her, but to his men, and they stopped.

"Kesh," said Brandt. "The pilot."

"He's my brother."

"I know who he is." Brandt's voice was mild. "Take him home. He's not charged. The Director doesn't want anybody charged tonight. Take him home and tell him the Director doesn't want anybody charged tonight." He looked at Nico. "Tonight."

They let him go. He came off the wall with blood on his lip and his eyes blazing, and Teo took him by the back of the neck the way their mother used to, and walked him away down the passage, and he let her.

Corrigan walked with them in silence as far as the Lantern Row lift. Then, at the lift door, with Nico gone ahead into the car, he put his hand on Teo's arm.

"Teo."

"Don't."

"I haven't said anything."

"You're going to say *cancelled in full*. You're going to say *Lulu's too*."

He was quiet. His hand stayed on her arm. Under the lift's bad light his face looked older than it was, and she remembered all at once, for no reason, a night eleven years ago on the Brannock with the canopy full of stars and his laugh in the dark.

"Lulu's too," he said softly. "Teo. She's nine. She'll be paying off Mira's medical till she's fifty. Mine. My father's. She'll never — "

"It's never been done, Corr. You heard her. Nobody knows what's at the waist. Nobody's ever *been* to the waist in a Narrowing. I was at the inner markers two days ago in the Mercy and I nearly didn't come back, and that's the *edge*."

He looked at her strangely. "You were at the inner markers?"

"Charter."

"In a *tug*?"

"Don't change the subject."

"I'm not." He let go of her arm. He smiled, a little, the old smile. "I'm just thinking that if you can do it in a tug," he said, "so can I."

The lift door opened. Nico was inside, holding the door, with blood on his lip. Teo got in. Corrigan did not.

"Corr," she said.

"I'll see you on the lanes," he said, and the door closed.

## Something to Do
[
  Place: #The Director's Gallery
  Characters: @@Ambrose Lyle @@Hesper Vane

  When: same day
]
"There is no basis for it," Ambrose said.

The Director's Gallery was at the top of the station's spindle, the Needle-ward end, a long low room with a curved window that took up the whole of one wall, so that the mouth of the Needle hung outside it like a vast dark painting. Hesper Vane had furnished it with almost nothing: a desk, two chairs, a cabinet, a rug of some deep red Inward wool that must have cost more than Ambrose's harp. She sat behind the desk now with her hands folded on it. He had not sat down.

"I never said it was starving," he said. "I never said anything about a ring weakening. I never said that feeding glass to it would do anything at all. Nobody has ever said that. There is no data. There is no theory. You made it up."

"I extrapolated."

"From what?"

"From what you told me." Her voice was level. "You told me the waist is narrowing. You told me you don't know why. You told me the throat is held open by exotic matter. You told me brine-glass is exotic matter. You told me you have no model and no theory and four data points and a lot of hope." She spread her hands slightly on the desk. "I have given them a model. I have given them a theory. I have taken away the hope and given them something better."

"Which is what?"

"Something to do."

He stared at her.

"People can bear almost anything, Ambrose," Hesper Vane said, "except not knowing what to do. You know this. You've watched it, these last two weeks — the bars, the dorms, the shafts. Forty thousand people who have just been told that the company that owns them is leaving and that the door to the rest of the universe may be closing behind it. What do you think they will do with that? If they have nothing to do with it?" She did not wait for an answer. "They will riot. They will stop cutting. They will tear Anchorage apart looking for someone to blame and they will find me, because I am the one who is staying. I have given them something to do. I have given them a way to save themselves. Whether it works — "

"It won't work."

"You don't know that either." She looked at him, and her eyes were very steady. "Do you? Can you tell me — with data, Ambrose, with a theory — that it won't?"

He opened his mouth and shut it.

"No," she said. "You can't. You've said so to me twice." She leaned back in her chair. "So. The Narrowing will do whatever it does. If the Needle opens again, the Keeping will have kept it open. If it doesn't, nothing would have. Either way, for forty-seven days, the Reach will have something to do other than burn itself down. That is the whole of my policy. You may disapprove of it. I would think less of you if you didn't."

"And the pilots?"

Something moved in her face, very briefly, and was gone. "The pilots will be volunteers. Every one. I will not order anyone into the throat. They will be paid more than anyone in the Reach has ever been paid for anything, and the ones who come back will be heroes, and their families will be free." A pause. "And the ones who don't come back will be heroes too."

Ambrose turned away and looked out of the window at the mouth. He thought of the buoy out there at the inner markers, two days in the pull now, singing to him across two million kilometres: the long, slow, even drawing-in of the waist, and under it the other thing, the swell, the tide. Louder there. Real. He had sat in the Lectern last night with the headphones on and listened to it until three in the morning. It had a period, he was almost sure, of a little over four minutes. He did not know what it was. He did not know what it meant.

He had not told her about it. He realised, standing at her window, that he was not going to — not yet, not until he knew. He did not examine why.

"Your berth on the *Providence*," Hesper Vane said behind him, "is confirmed for the twenty-eighth. Senior grade, as I said. Cabin twelve. I checked." He heard her slate click. "Forty-four days, Ambrose. Do your job until then. Read the throat, give me the numbers every six hours, don't speak to the bars. And then go home."

He put his hand in his jacket pocket and closed it round the fork.

"Yes, Director," he said.
