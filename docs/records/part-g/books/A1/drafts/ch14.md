## True Pitch
[
  Place: #The Lectern
  Characters: @@Adaeze Quill

  Facts: ?The Narrowing passes on its own:@Adaeze Quill

She retuned the harp on the night of the seventeenth of November, eighteen days before the Thirtieth Keeping, because she could not think of a reason good enough not to.

She had thought about it for nine days. She had thought about it in the infirmary with her hands in dressings, and in the archive, among the black ruin of the racks, sifting what the fire had left; and in her little office with the singed daybooks stacked on her desk and the Draw ledger open at its last page, at a small neat *s* in a margin. She had thought about it in chapel, singing the Hunger Tone with the others at the morning office, and found that she could no longer sing it without hearing, underneath it, the Fork's note fighting it in the reliquary alcove — *wah, wah, wah*. She had prayed. She had not been answered. She was not sure any longer that she had expected to be.

What decided her, in the end, was a line in a daybook.

It was one of the ones she had carried out of the fire: a slim volume bound in black, its spine scorched, its first pages brown and brittle at the edges. The daybook of the Lectern for May and June of 2291. The first daybook of the order, written in the weeks after the First Keeping by whatever readers had been left to read. She had catalogued it fifteen years ago and never opened it since, because it was all numbers. She opened it now. And on its first page, at the head of the first entry, dated the second of May, in a small upright hand that was not the hand of the rest of the book, she read:

*Henceforth the harp is to be tuned each morning to the H.T. and not otherwise. By order of the F.W.*

She sat with that for an hour.

*Henceforth.* So there had been a before. A way the harp was tuned before the First Keeping, which the First Warden had then forbidden. On the day after. On the same day, very nearly, that forty-four years of records had been taken from the archive and never returned.

At midnight she took the Fork from the reliquary with her archivist's key, and went out into the empty harp hall under the dome, and began.

It took her until four. Nine courses. She did it the way she had been taught to tune a harp as a novice, before she ever learned the Hunger Tone — with a reference and her ears — only the reference was the Fork, struck against her bandaged palm and held to the frame, and her ears were twenty years older and very tired. Her hands hurt. Twice she had to stop and sit on the floor and breathe until the burns stopped throbbing. Somewhere in the fourth course she started to hear the difference. By the seventh she was weeping, steadily, without noticing, the tears running into her collar.

The harp did not drone when she had finished. It sang.

She would think afterward that there had been no other word. The long low ache of the Hunger Tone was gone, and what had come up out of the strings in its place was wider and deeper and more alive: a great slow chord with something moving in it, a rise and fall under the surface, so slow you felt it more than heard it, like the swell under a ship. She stood in front of the harp in the dark with the Fork in her hand and listened to it, and after a long time she understood what she was listening to. It was breathing.

She went to the reader's console and woke it.

She knew how to read a trace; she had been trained to it, before the archive. She watched the waist line draw itself across the old screen in pale green, the throat narrowing toward its point, the Narrowing eighteen days from its trough. And underneath it, where the Hunger-tuned harp had never shown anything but the throat's own steady noise, there was now a second line. Slow. Enormous. A long low wave running under the Narrowing like a tide under a boat. And it was not falling. It was at the bottom of its trough, or within a day or two of it — she could see it flattening — and on the far side of the trough, the line began, very gently, to rise.

The Narrowing was deep because the tide was low. And the tide was turning.

She did not trust it. She went down into the ruin of the archive with a lamp, to the oldest rack, where the crystal spools had come through the fire scorched but whole, and she brought them up one by one — the First, the Fifth, the Twelfth, the Twentieth, the Twenty-Ninth — and fed them through the old reader. They had all been recorded through a harp tuned to the Hunger Tone; but she knew the Hunger Tone's offset from the Fork's note now, to the hair, because she had just spent four hours taking it out. She could take it out of the records too. It was arithmetic. It was the kind of thing an archivist did all day.

She did it for the Twenty-Ninth first, because she had stood in this hall and read its numbers aloud eleven years ago with her voice shaking.

The tide was there. Under the Twenty-Ninth Narrowing, as under this one: the long slow wave. And the waist had begun to recover — the short breath, the Narrowing itself, turning up off its trough — at fourteen minutes past six on the morning of the Long Night. The Keepers had gone in at nine. Nearly three hours later. She remembered it. She had been here. Everyone had stood in this hall and sung while they went in, and the High Warden had struck the Fork, and an hour afterward the readers had called out that the waist was opening, and the whole Anchorhold had wept for joy.

It had been opening for three hours before they flew.

She did the Twentieth. The Twelfth. The Fifth. It was not always three hours; once it was eleven, once forty minutes. But it was always before. Every time. Every Keeping in the archive had been flown into a door that was already opening, timed by readers who could see the waist turn on a harp that could not see why, and called a miracle.

She did the First last. The spool for the second of May, the first spool in the rack, the morning after. It was all that survived; there was nothing from the Long Night itself. But she could see on it, at the very start, the tail of the long tide's turn — a little lower than now, a little earlier in its rise. The First Keeping, too, had been flown at the bottom of a tide that was already turning.

There was one other thing. She almost missed it, at the end, with her eyes burning. At the waist, at the very narrowest point, where the trace should have shown nothing but the passage, the retuned harp was showing something small and hard and bright. A knot. A ringing. As though something were lodged there. It had not been visible on any trace she had ever seen. She looked at it for a while, and did not know what it was, and made a note of it in her private notebook, and let it go, because she had no more room in her for anything else.

At five in the morning, Anchorhold time, Adaeze Quill sat on the floor of the harp hall with her back against the reader's console and the Fork in her lap, under the dome, with the harp singing its true chord above her and the mouth leaning in, and she put her face in her bandaged hands and wept for a hundred and twelve people whose names she had read aloud on the anniversaries for twenty years.

## Corrie Rock
[

Corrie Rock was a lump of dirty ice the size of a city block, with a smugglers' hab dug into it like a tick into a dog, and it had been neutral ground in the Severance for six years because both sides needed somewhere to buy things they were not supposed to have.

Juno came in on a Navy jump-shuttle with its transponder off and Marsh's voice in her ear the whole way, quietly furious. *If Squadron asks, Captain, I'm going to tell them you've got food poisoning. I'm going to tell them it's very bad. I'm going to describe it.* She had told him to describe it as much as he liked. She landed in a hangar that smelled of rust and ammonia, and a Shoals girl with a clan tattoo along her jaw met her at the hatch and looked at her uniform and spat on the deck and led her through a maze of ice tunnels to a door.

He was sitting at a table in a room with no windows, with a pot of something hot and two cups, and his right hand wrapped in a dressing to the wrist.

She had not seen his face in eleven years except in grainy pictures. It was older. Thinner. There was a scar along his jaw she did not know and grey at his temples he was too young for. It was still his face. He looked at her standing in the doorway in her greys and did not get up, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all.

"You came," he said.

"You sent a message on the cadet band."

"Eleven years ago I'd have said that was romantic." He nodded at the other chair. "Sit down, Juno. I'm not going to shoot you. I can't hold a gun." He lifted the bandaged hand. "The *Callum*'s bridge took a hit from your forward battery. Very good shooting. Your gunner should get a medal."

"He did."

"Good."

She sat. He poured. Neither of them drank.

"I didn't mean the wheel," he said. "I need you to know that. Whatever else. I told my cutters the stores, the doors, nothing above them. Two of the boys came round the far side and — they're nineteen, Juno. They were frightened. They fired everything they had at whatever they could see. Nobody meant the wheel." He looked at his tea. "Four Keepers. I heard. And seven of yours."

"Ezra Lind," said Juno. "He was twenty-two. He walked a hundred metres through a vented section to tell my sister he'd found her, and then he sat down and died in her lap."

Rhee closed his eyes.

"Callum was eighteen," he said after a while. "He went in at the Twenty-Ninth and didn't come out. You were there. You were at the Hall."

"I know."

"No. You don't." He opened his eyes. "You don't know, Juno. You've never known. You came to the Hall and stood on your mother's terrace and sang the Hunger Tone and went home. You didn't — " He stopped. "Sorry. That's not fair. You're here."

She took out her slate. Not a Navy slate; a cheap civilian thing she had bought in Tallow with cash. On it was a single photograph: a page of a black ledger with a scorched spine, nine names in a clerk's hand, and beside the third, in the margin, a small neat *s*.

She pushed it across the table.

He looked at it for a long time. His face did not change. Then he put his good hand flat on the table beside the slate, very carefully, as if the table might move.

"I knew," he said. "I didn't know. I knew." He was very quiet. "I filed that complaint in our third year. About the Shoals cadets. You signed it."

"I know."

"My name was on top." He looked up at her. "I used to lie awake. In the League, in the first year. Thinking: it was the complaint. It must have been the complaint. And then thinking, no, that's mad, that's grief, nobody would do that. The Wheel is the Wheel." He laughed, without any sound. "How many?"

"One every Draw. That we know of. A sleeve name. The High Warden carries it in."

"And Pell."

"And Pell."

He pushed the slate back across the table to her. "Then you know why we're going to stop it," he said. "The League voted. Three days ago. At the Thirtieth Narrowing, when the skiffs go out to the mouth, we'll be there. Everything we've got. We're not letting nine more people go into that thing so that a man in a grey robe can tell the Assembly how to vote."

"If you fire on the Keepers you kill my sister."

"We won't fire on the Keepers. We'll fire on their escort." He held her eyes. "Which is you."

"And then the Keepers fly in anyway, because the Wardens will send them in whether the escort's there or not, into a throat full of crossfire. Do you think Mor will call it off because the Shoals are shooting? He'll call it a martyrdom. He'll have nine more names in the liturgy by Christmas." She leaned forward. "Rhee. Listen to me. What if it doesn't need doing?"

He frowned. "What?"

"What if the Keeping does nothing?" She said it very low, though the room had no windows and no one else in it. "There's a Warden. The archivist at the Anchorhold. Adaeze Quill. She found — " She stopped, and began again, and told him: the Blank Year; the Fork and the harp not agreeing; the daybook from the fire. *Henceforth the harp is to be tuned to the H.T. and not otherwise.* She had heard the last of it only that morning, by tight-beam, in the archivist's voice, flat and exhausted: *I tuned it to the Fork. It breathes, Commander. The door breathes. It opens by itself. It always has.*

Rhee listened without moving. When she had finished he sat looking at the wall.

"Can she prove it," he said at last. Not a question.

"She thinks so. She thinks she can show anyone who can read a trace. She thinks — she says she can make it so anybody can *hear* it. On the harp. If it's tuned true."

"And who's going to listen? The Assembly? The Hall? They'll burn her for heresy before she gets the first word out."

"Not if everybody hears it at once." Juno had been thinking about it for two days. "On the Long Night. The harp hall broadcasts on every channel in the Reach while the Keepers fly. It always has. Every house in Tallow Under has the Long Night on. Every ship. Every hab in the Shoals that has a receiver — you know they do, Rhee, you used to listen to it in the dormitory with the sound down. Everyone in the Reach is listening to that harp on the night of the fifth." She held his eyes. "If it's tuned true, on the night, and somebody's in the hall to tell them what they're hearing — "

"Then the Wardens kill whoever it is and switch it off."

"Then somebody has to hold the hall long enough."

He looked at her across the little table, in the windowless room, over the two cold cups of tea.

"You're asking me," he said slowly, "not to shoot at you."

"I'm asking you to hold fire on the night. All of it. And to be ready, if I call, to come in and stand between the Anchorhold and the Home Squadron for as long as it takes to play the Reach a sound." She heard her own voice and did not recognise it. "And if it doesn't work — if she's wrong, if nobody listens — then you can do whatever the League voted, and I won't be in a position to stop you, because I'll be in a cell. Or dead."

"And if she's right?"

"Then nobody goes in," said Juno. "Not Pell. Not anybody. Ever again."

Rhee Calder looked down at his bandaged hand on the table. Then, slowly, he turned it over, palm up, and left it there.

After a moment she put hers in it. His fingers closed, carefully, over the dressing.

"Eleven years," he said.

"I know."

"I'll put it to the League," he said. "I can't promise. Some of them will want to fire anyway. Some of them have lost more than I have." He held her hand. "But I'll be there, Juno. On the night. On the cadet band. Call, and I'll come."

## The Door
[

He came into the harp hall at dawn on the nineteenth, on the second morning, while she was still at the console.

She had not retuned it. She had meant to. She had told herself, on the first morning, that she would sit for one more night and copy everything — every trace, every spool, every correction — onto crystal, three times, and hide the crystals in three places, and then put it back to the Hunger Tone before the novices came in for the morning office. She had sent the novices away, the first morning, at the door. *A fault in the harp. I'm working on it.* They had gone. She had not thought it would hold for two.

She heard the door and did not turn round. She knew his step. She had known it for twenty years.

He did not say anything at first. He came and stood in the middle of the hall, under the dome, a few metres behind her, and stood there, listening. She could see his reflection faintly in the console glass, laid over the green trace: the tall straight figure in grey, the white cropped head lifted. The harp sang its true chord above them both, wide and deep and breathing.

"Well," said Cassius Mor softly. "Well, well."

She turned round.

He was not looking at her. He was looking at the harp, with an expression she had never seen on him, and could not at first name. Then she could. It was the face of a man hearing a piece of music he had loved as a child and had not heard in a very long time.

"It's beautiful, isn't it," he said.

She said nothing.

"I've heard it once before," said the High Warden. "Like this. Many years ago." He came forward, slowly, and laid his palm against the harp's gold frame, the way all the readers did. "What have you found, Sister?"

She had decided, in the two nights, what she would say if he came. She had decided to tell him everything, all of it, plainly, and watch his face. So she did. The daybook. *Henceforth.* The retuning. The tide under the Narrowing. The twenty-nine Keepings, corrected; the waist turning, every time, before the Keepers flew. The First, at the bottom of its tide like this one. She told it in order, carefully, the way she would have catalogued it, and she watched him the whole time.

He did not argue. He did not ask a single question. He stood with his hand on the harp and listened to her as if she were telling him something he had heard before, a long time ago, and was glad to hear again from someone who told it well.

When she had finished there was a long silence.

"You have done remarkable work," Cassius Mor said. "Truly. I have never in my life seen a piece of scholarship like it. The First Warden would be very proud of you."

"Would the First Warden," said Adaeze, very quietly.

He looked at her then. His eyes were grey and warm and steady, and very sad.

"Retune it, Sister," he said. "Before the novices come. They're waiting in the passage. I sent them away a second time, but I can't do it a third." He held out his hand, palm up. "And give me the Fork. It belongs on its cushion."

She looked at his open hand for a long time. Then she put the Fork in it. She did not know why. She would think about that afterward, often, and never decide — whether it was the habit of twenty years, or the gentleness of his voice, or simply that her hands were burned and tired and the Fork was heavy.

He closed his fingers round it. He went to the reliquary alcove and laid it on its grey cushion, carefully, the way she would have, and closed the iron grille, and locked it with the key on the chain round his neck. Then he stood for a moment with his back to her, his hand on the bars.

"You'll have copied it," he said, without turning. "Three times, I'd guess. You're very thorough."

She did not answer.

"Yes," he said. "Of course you have." He turned round. "Retune the harp, Adaeze. Then go to bed. You haven't slept in two nights. We'll talk about all of this after the Keeping, when there's time." He smiled at her, the warm kind tired smile that he had given the Commander across his desk, the smile the whole Reach knew. "There's always more time after the Keeping."

He went out. She heard him in the passage, speaking gently to the novices, sending them to breakfast.

She stood alone in the hall with the harp breathing above her. Then she went to the console and, with her bandaged hands, began to take down the true chord, course by course, peg by peg, back to the Hunger Tone; and as she worked she found that she was not afraid, which surprised her, and that what she felt instead was something very cold and very clear, like the light at the rim of the mouth. *I've heard it once before,* he had said. *Like this. Many years ago.*

He had known. Not suspected; known. He had come into the hall and heard the true chord and recognised it, the way you recognise a voice. And he had not been surprised by a single thing she said.
