## Dock Four

There were a hundred skiffs on Dock Four on the morning of the Sixtieth Narrowing, and Isra counted them, because counting was what she did when she could not do anything else.

They filled the hangar. They had been coming up from Tallow for a week on the Lantern's ore-haulers, two and three at a time, narrow black twelve-metre hulls with a lamp painted on every nose, and the Lantern's fitters had racked them in rows across the great cold floor under the one working light, each with its cradle slung under its belly and its cradle full: glass from the Gutter, glass from the Pans, glass torn out of the walls of Rope Street and the floors of the bone-pickers' skiffs, glass from every Return in the Ossuary that the Lantern had stripped since April. Every gram in the Reach. You could feel it in the hangar. It was like standing in a room full of held breath. The shard on its cord against Isra's chest had not stopped humming, faintly, since she came down the lift.

A hundred Keepers stood in front of them in their black, in their rows, with beads at their throats, and sang.

They were singing the Long Night. All forty verses, Isra thought; somebody had collected them, the way Hanne had, and taught them all. The Hunger Tone under it, from the hangar's speakers, from the harp hall, where the Lantern's two boy-readers sat at the console in their black coats keeping the drone. A hundred voices over it. *The door grew narrow in the dark. The Reach was cold and small.* They were mostly young. A great many of them were smiling. They looked, Isra thought, the way Pike had said they looked at the Fifty-Ninth: happy. So happy it was like a light coming off them.

She found him in the fourth row.

He was taller than everyone round him and greyer and he was not singing. He stood in his black coat with his hands at his sides and his strapped ribs under it, and the bead at his throat, and when he saw her coming through the rows he did not look surprised. He looked, for a moment, as if he were going to say something; and then he only shook his head, and smiled in his beard.

"Number forty," she said.

"Number forty."

"They put you in the fourth row."

"Tall men at the back," said Pike. "So we don't block the view." He coughed, carefully, into the rag he still carried. "You've got your heater fixed?"

"I kicked it."

"Good girl." He looked at her. He did not ask where she was going. He did not look at the bulge under her flight jacket where the Fork lay against her ribs, or at the old cittern on its strap over her shoulder. "Mind the shear," he said. "At the mouth. It comes from behind."

"I know."

"I know you know." He put out one big ruined hand and touched her hair, once, the way Hanne used to. "Go on, then."

She went.

At the far end of the hangar, by the Lamplighter's black ore-hauler, a knot of black coats stood round a small figure in black, and in the middle of them, between two of the Lantern's boys, with his hands bound in front of him with a strip of cord, Lucan. They had brought him up from the penitents' cells at dawn. He saw her across the hangar and his face did not change at all, and he did not move his head; but his eyes went to the bulge under her jacket, and away, and he closed them for a second, and opened them again.

The Lamplighter had seen her too.

Mercy Oduya stood by her ship in her black coat with her hands bare. She had not slept, Isra thought; her face was grey and swollen, and there was something in it that had not been there before the fifteenth — a stillness, a kind of looking-through. Her son lay in the harp hall under the dome, in his skiff, with nine little lamps round it. She had sat with him every night. She had not changed a single order. She had not called off a single skiff. She had only, the day after the Deacon left, struck her own name off the flagship's roster, and put it on the Keepers' list instead, at the top. Number one. She would fly the lead skiff herself.

She looked at Isra across the hangar for a long time. Then she inclined her head, the way she had to the Prior in the harp hall six weeks ago, which was more than he had done for her.

Isra did not incline hers back. She walked out of the hangar to the bone-pickers' berth at the far end of Dock Four, where the Gristle sat at her lines, and climbed in, and closed the hatch, and kicked the heater, and sat with her hands on the yoke and the cittern across her knees and the Fork against her ribs and waited for the Long Night.

## The Bell
[
  Place: #The Needle
  Characters: @@Isra Venn
  Items: ^The Fork:#The Needle ^The sounding buoy:#The Needle ^Gristle:@Isra Venn ^Hanne's cittern:@Isra Venn
  When: same day

The readers called the Long Night at nineteen minutes past four in the afternoon, Anchor time, and the hundred skiffs went out of Dock Four in a line behind the Lamplighter, and Isra went out of the bone-pickers' berth three minutes later, alone, with her running lights dark.

Nobody stopped her. Nobody was looking. Everything in the Reach that could fly was out at the mouth that afternoon, every Lantern cutter and ore-hauler and shuttle, lined up beyond the shear in a great ragged crescent to watch the Great Keeping go in; and every receiver in the Reach was on, in Tallow and the Gutter and the Pans and the rocks, tuned to the harp hall's channel, to the Hunger Tone and the Lamplighter's voice. Isra went out under them all in the Gristle with her transponder off, low, the way she had gone out to the Ossuary every day for ten years, and nobody so much as hailed her.

On the open channel, over the drone, she heard the flagship's reader calling the line. Not the Lantern's boys. Lucan.

"*Keepers, flagship. Hold at the outer markers. Shear is very heavy from two to six. I say again, hold. We'll wait for a gap.*"

His voice was perfectly steady.

She went in past the outer markers on her own line. Not his; hers, the Ossuary line, the one she had flown for ten years, low and to the left, under the worst of it, where the mouth's pull bent the shear-waves up and over and the old Returns drifted in the lee. She did not need a reader. She knew it. The Gristle bucked and shuddered and screamed in every seam the way she always did, and Isra turned into the waves before they came, and did not think about anything at all except Pike's voice in her head saying *it comes from behind*, and every time she turned to look behind, it did.

The Bell was where it had always been.

She saw it come out of the glow at the very edge of the mouth, where the pull held it: a small grey cylinder, nose to the dark, its lattice of wires at the tail bent and furred with frost, hanging quite still in all that violence the way a stone hangs still in a fast stream. She brought the Gristle in beside it on the lee side and put the grapple on it, gently, Pike's way, and it took her two tries, and the second time it held.

She put on her suit and went over with the cable.

Up close it was smaller than she had thought. Two metres, nose to tail. Hand-made. She could see that even under the frost: the welds were neat and slightly uneven, the welds of somebody careful working alone. On its flank, as Lucan had said, there was a little hatch. It had rusted shut. She broke it with the butt of her cutter, and inside there was a port, old and odd, a pattern of pins she did not recognise; and she cursed for a while, in her helmet, and then took the cable's head apart with her cutter's blade and her gloved fingers, and twisted wires to pins by guesswork and by the colour of the insulation, until the Gristle's board in her helmet speaker said, very faintly, *external load*.

Then she went to the tail.

The end-plate was furred with six hundred years of frost, and the socket in the middle of it was packed with it, hard and white. She chipped it out with the point of her blade, very carefully, a flake at a time. It took a long time. The shear went over her like surf. On the open channel, faint in her helmet, she heard Lucan again: "*Hold, Keepers. Hold at the markers. Gap in eight minutes. I'll call it.*" And the Lamplighter's voice, low and warm and patient, *We'll hold, Brother.*

When the socket was clear she took out the Fork.

She held it in her gloved hand at the edge of the mouth, in the grey-gold light, with the Needle filling the whole of the sky in front of her. A little brass thing, fifteen centimetres long, its tines worn bright. *For Cl.* She thought of Lucan on his knees in the harp hall at two in the morning with the hinge-pins on the floor. She thought of Hanne's hand on the low string. She thought, for no reason, of the boy in the first Return she had cut in April, with the grey rag over his face.

She put the Fork into the socket, stem first.

It went in like a key. All the way down. And at the bottom, she felt it through her glove — a small definite *click*, as some catch inside the end-plate that had waited six hundred years took hold of it, and held.

The lattice of wires at the tail shivered.

Frost came off it in a fine bright spray. The bent wires straightened, slowly, as if they were stretching after sleep, and began very faintly to hum; and the shard against Isra's chest, inside her suit, woke and hummed back. She felt it through her ribs. She had been holding her breath. She let it out.

The lattice was not facing the waist. It had drifted; it was facing off to the left, toward the crescent of watching ships. She went back to the Gristle and took the grapple controls and turned the Bell, very gently, a degree at a time, the way you would turn a sleeping child, until its humming wires faced the dark at the centre of the mouth.

Then she sat in the Gristle's pilot seat with the cable running out of her ship into the Bell and the cittern on her knees, and waited for the harp.

On the open channel the Hunger Tone droned on.

The harp did not change.

## The Prior
[
  Place: #The Lectern
  Characters: @@Benedek Saar @@Isra Venn:phone

  When: same day

The two boys had gone to the dock to watch the Keepers out, because the Prior had told them they should — *you'll want to tell your children you saw it, boys; go on; I'll hold the drone* — and so at twenty to five on the afternoon of the Sixtieth Narrowing, Benedek Saar was alone in the harp hall with the harp, and the Lamplighter's dead son in his skiff under the dome with nine little lamps round him, and his own two sticks.

He had been alone with the harp, in that hall, for most of sixty years. He knew it the way you know a room in the dark.

He had not known, until the last few minutes, whether he would do it.

He had sat on the bench at the side of the hall since the Long Night was called, with the boys at the console in front of him singing, and listened to Lucan's voice on the channel holding the Keepers at the markers, steady and level and lying; and he had thought about eleven old men in their cells, and about a station built for six thousand, and about what Mercy Oduya would do afterward to whoever did this, and to everyone near them. He had thought about it with great care, the way he had been taught to think about everything. And then he had looked across the hall at the little black skiff under the dome, at the soft round face behind the thin glass of the canopy, and the yellow name painted above it, and the hands on the lever not pulled, and the three neat holes in the engine housing; and he had found that he was not thinking about any of it any more.

He sent the boys away.

When they had gone he got up off the bench, which took some time, and went to the console. He knew where Lucan had put the switch. Under the third floor-plate from the left, beside the reader's chair; Lucan had told him, on the night of the fourteenth, sitting on the end of his bed. The Prior had remembered every word Lucan said that night. He had gone over it every night since, in his cell, lying awake.

He did not have the Fork.

He had known that, too, for eight days. He had given it to the girl himself. He stood at the console on his sticks and looked at the harp, singing the Hunger Tone into the dome as he had made it sing every morning of his life, and he thought: *I cannot tune it true without the Fork. I am eighty-one. I have sung this note for sixty years. I do not know the other one. I heard it once, the night the boy tuned it, and I made him take it down.*

He leaned on his sticks and closed his eyes.

And over the channel, under the drone, very faint, from somewhere out at the edge of the mouth, there came a single low note, plucked on a string.

Old. Round. True.

It came again. And again. Steady, patient, every few seconds, the way you would pluck a string to give somebody a note to tune to. Not on the harp hall's channel. On the Wardens' own channel, the order's old private band, which nobody but the order had used in a hundred years, which the Lamplighter's boys did not know existed. Somebody out at the mouth had found it. Somebody had asked Lucan for it.

*The low string,* the Prior thought. *Never retuned. The tellers kept it.*

He laid his sticks down on the floor.

He knelt at the base of the frame, which he had not done in twenty years, because his knees would not allow it; and they did not allow it now, and he did it anyway; and with the girl's grandmother's low string sounding over the order's channel from two million kilometres away, every few seconds, steady, patient, he began to tune the First Warden's harp away from the Hunger Tone.

It was very slow. His hands shook. His ears were eighty-one years old. Course by course. Peg by peg. Every time the string sounded he stopped and listened and turned the peg a hair, and listened again, until the wire and the string lay down together into one note; and then the next wire; and the next. Somewhere around the third course the harp began to change. Somewhere around the sixth it began to ring. The great slow bell-sound he had heard once, through the door of this hall, five weeks ago, and had ordered taken down. It came up out of the strings round him and filled the dome, deep and layered and enormous, and went out on the harp hall's channel into every receiver in the Reach, into every house in Tallow and the Gutter and the Pans and the rocks, into every ship in the watching crescent and every skiff at the markers.

The Prior did not stop to hear what anyone said about it. He did not have time. He tuned the ninth course, the last, on his knees, with the tears running down his face into his beard; and then he reached out with one shaking hand and lifted the third floor-plate from the left, and found the switch Lucan had put there, and closed it.

## The Ringing

Lucan heard it on the flagship's bridge, through the speakers, with his hands bound in front of him and the Lamplighter's second at his elbow: the harp changing.

He had been calling a false line for twenty-six minutes. *Hold at the markers. Shear is very heavy. Wait for a gap.* The shear was heavy; that was true. It was always heavy. He had watched the hundred skiffs hang at the outer markers in their long black line with their engines warm, waiting for his word, and the Lamplighter's skiff at the head of them, small and black with the yellow lamp on its nose; and he had listened to her voice on the channel saying *We'll hold, Brother,* patient, trusting, as the minutes went by; and he had wanted, more than he had ever wanted anything, to be sick.

And then, under the Hunger Tone, over the order's old band, which he had told Isra about at the grille of the fourth cell — very faint — a low string, plucked. Again. Again.

And then the harp began to change.

He heard it go. Course by course, slowly — whoever was doing it was doing it slowly, with old hands — the false drone sliding and fighting and lying down, wire by wire, into the bell. He knew who it was before the third course. Nobody else could have. He stood on the flagship's bridge with his bound hands and his eyes shut and listened to Benedek Saar tune the First Warden's harp on his knees, over every channel in the Reach, and he began, without any sound, to weep.

"*Brother,*" said the Lamplighter's voice on the channel. "*What is that?*"

He opened his eyes.

"That's the harp," said Lucan. "Tuned to the First Warden's Fork. That's what it sounds like when it's true."

Silence on the channel. On the bridge round him the Lamplighter's people had gone still. On the display the hundred skiffs hung at the markers. The bell rang on, louder, deeper, as the Prior reached the sixth course, the seventh.

"*It sounds like a bell,*" said Mercy Oduya.

"It is a bell. That's what's in the throat. That's the glass. Every Keeping that ever was, Lamplighter. Fifty-nine layers, at the waist, as big as the Anchor. Ringing." He made himself go on. "That's what the girl told you. On Dock Four. On your son's skiff. It's true. It's choking. It's not hungry. It never was. Every skiff we've ever sent in has made it worse. If you take a hundred in now, with every gram in the Reach — "

"*Be quiet, Brother.*"

He was quiet.

The Prior reached the ninth course. The bell filled every speaker on the bridge. Lucan heard, under it, very faint, the sound of an old man breathing hard, close to a microphone; and then a click.

And out at the mouth, at the very edge, where the grey-gold fire met the dark, something answered.

He saw it on the display. Everyone did. A point of light at the rim of the mouth — the Bell, the dead buoy, that had hung there for six hundred years — lit, suddenly, bright as a star, as the harp's ringing reached it and it took the note and threw it, all of it, every course, a single huge true note, straight down into the dark at the centre of the mouth like a stone into a well.

The Needle rang.

There was no other word for it. The mouth *rang*, from rim to rim. The bruise at its centre went white and blue and white again, and the grey-gold fire round it shivered and blurred, and the sound came back up out of the throat over every channel — not the harp now, not the Bell, but the thing itself, the mass at the waist, answering its own note: a deep, terrible, layered, rising bell-sound, louder and louder, as if something enormous were straining against itself in the dark.

On the channel the Lamplighter said, very quietly, "*Tobin.*"

Then her skiff moved.

It went forward out of the line at the markers, alone. Lucan saw it. Small and black, the yellow lamp on its nose, its engine lit, going toward the mouth, toward the ringing, toward the white-blue light at the centre.

"Lamplighter," said Lucan. "Mercy. *Don't.*"

"*I'm not taking them, Brother.*" Her voice was calm. "*Hold them. Hold the rest. You're right. I heard it. I can hear it.*" The skiff went on. "*I'm only going to look. I have to see it. I put him in there. I have to see.*"

"*Mercy — *"

"*Hold them, Brother.*"

And then the throat let go.

Afterward, nobody in the Reach could agree how it had looked. Everybody had seen it. Every ship in the crescent, every skiff at the markers, every screen in Tallow with a feed from the Anchor's telescopes; and no two people ever told it the same, and Hanne Venn would have collected all of them and sung them all and said they were all wrong. But they all began the same way. The mouth went white — pure white, the whole of it, rim to rim, for the space of a breath. And then it opened.

Not wider. *Out.* The throat turned itself inside out, the way a cough turns you inside out, and everything that had been stuck in it for six hundred years came out at once.

Ships.

Hundreds of them. More than anyone had ever imagined there could be. They came pouring out of the white in a long tumbling river, end over end, in a storm of shattering glass — skiffs, little narrow Keepers' skiffs, the black ones of the Lantern and the grey ones of the Compact and older ones, shapes nobody alive had ever seen, all of them crusted and furred and wrapped in glass that was breaking off them as they came, so that the whole river glittered, like a waterfall in sunlight, like a galaxy turning over. They came and came. They spilled out of the mouth past the markers and through the line of black skiffs and on into the Ossuary and beyond it, scattering, slowing, turning in the grey-gold light, and still more came behind them, and the glass came off them in great bright clouds and drifted, and sang — you could hear it, on every channel, the shattered glass ringing as it tumbled, a sound like ten thousand wineglasses touched with a fingertip at once.

The hundred skiffs at the markers broke and scattered before it like birds.

Lucan saw the Lamplighter's skiff for a moment, small and black against the white, right at the mouth, in the middle of the river. Its nose was up. It was not turning away. It was holding there, in the outrush, with the dead pouring past it on every side.

"*I see them,*" said Mercy Oduya on the channel, very softly. Her voice was full of wonder. "*Oh, Brother. I see them. They're all — they're all coming — *"

The river took her.

He did not see her skiff again. Nobody did.

It went on for eleven minutes. Then the white at the mouth began to fade, and the river thinned, and the last few ships came tumbling out of the light in ones and twos, slowly, almost gently, like the last leaves off a tree; and the glass-clouds drifted and spread and settled; and the ringing died away, on every channel, into silence.

Into a silence. And then, faint, under it, from the open channel where the harp still sang its true chord from the Anchor: a breath.

Lucan looked at the display.

The mouth of the Needle hung where it had always hung. But it was not a bruise any more. At its centre, where for all his life there had been a dull dark wound the colour of old blood, there was a ring. A ring of soft grey-gold light, faint and steady and very slightly irregular, like a coin worn by thumbs. The waist trace on the flagship's console, which Lucan had been watching without knowing he was watching it, said a number he had never seen it say. It was still narrowing. It was the Long Night; it would narrow for hours yet, the way it always had. But there was nothing in the waist. Nothing at all. Only the passage, wide and clear, breathing.

The Eye. His grandmother had told him once that old Wardens called it that. He had never believed there was such a thing.

"*Flagship,*" said a voice on the Keepers' channel. Thick, wet, coughing. "*Flagship, this is number forty.*" A long pause, while the voice coughed. "*I've turned round. I'm coming in. I don't — I don't want to.*" Another pause. "*Is there anybody on the second gun?*"

Lucan looked at the Lamplighter's second, beside him on the bridge, a heavy grey-haired woman with the lamp on her shoulder and her face wet, who had been staring at the empty place on the display where her prophet's skiff had been.

"No," the woman said into the channel. Her voice broke. "No, Pike. There's nobody. Come home."

They found her on the third day.

There were six hundred and forty-one of them by the Wardens' count, which the Wardens did not finish making for a month: six hundred and forty-one Returns, drifting in the Ossuary and beyond it, in a long loose cloud that stretched halfway from the mouth to Hollin, with the glass that had come off them hanging among them in slow glittering drifts. Every bone-picker in the Reach was out among them. Nobody was stripping them. Nobody had said not to; nobody needed to. They went from hull to hull in their skiffs with their lamps, slowly, looking in at the canopies, at the faces; and when they found one they knew — a family name, a Shoals clan mark painted on a nose, a Lantern lamp — they put a grapple on it, gently, and brought it home.

The Lantern's boys had found the Lamplighter's skiff on the first day, at the very front of the river, three hundred kilometres out. It was empty. The canopy was open. Nobody understood it, and everybody had a story.

Isra went out on the third day with Pike in the jump-seat, coughing, with his ribs strapped, and Lucan in the cargo bay with a lamp and a box of the Wardens' grey cloth, and the cittern wedged behind the pilot's seat where she could reach it. She did not go into the near cloud, where everyone was. She went out to the edge of the river, the far edge, where the oldest hulls had come to rest — the strange ones, the shapes nobody had a name for, the ones so furred with glass that you could not see their outlines at all.

"There," said Pike.

It was an ore-tug.

She knew that much from the shape under the glass: a stubby blunt hull built for pulling, with a tow-hook at the stern and a short wide cockpit at the bow. A design nobody had built in the Reach in four hundred years; she had seen one like it once, in a picture in Hanne's room, pinned to the wall, a drawing some teller had made from a description by some other teller. The glass on it was thicker than on anything she had ever cut. It had grown back along the hull in long sweeping plates, like the feathers of a bird folded in flight, so that the whole tug looked as if it had been flying for a very long time, very fast, into a wind.

They put the grapple on it. They went over, the three of them, Pike moving slowly, and stood on the hull.

The glass was thinnest on the canopy. Isra put her helmet lamp to it, and through it — through the milk of six hundred years — she could see the canopy beneath. It had a crack in it. An old one, forward, low, shaped like the delta of a river.

Lucan cleared the glass from the side of the nose with the flat of his cutter, very carefully, the way he had chipped the frost from the Bell's socket — she had told him about that, in the infirmary, and he had made her tell him twice. Under the glass there was paint. Old, flaking, a faded white on a hull that had been some dark colour once. Letters. He cleared them one at a time.

SMALL MERCY.

Nobody said anything.

They cut the canopy open. It took an hour. Inside, the cockpit was very cold in the way only things from the mouth were cold, and very small, and very still. It smelled, Isra thought, of nothing; and then, faintly, as the cold air moved, of something she could not place. Something sweet. Like the peel of a fruit she had once seen in a picture.

She was in the pilot's seat.

Isra had seen a great many of them. They were always in their seats. This one was a woman. Thirty-something; it was hard to tell. Dark hair, cut short. Her helmet was not on her head; it sat on the console in front of her, where she had put it, the way a pilot does on a long run. Her hands were on the yoke. Her face was turned a little upward, toward the canopy, toward the delta crack, as if she had been looking at something through it; and her eyes were open, and her mouth was open very slightly, and she was not afraid. You could see that. Whatever she had been looking at, at the end, she had not been afraid of it.

At her throat, on a cord gone black, there was a small cloudy bead of glass.

It was not lying against her skin. It was standing out from it, straight out on its cord, taut, toward the delta crack in the canopy, the way a compass needle stands toward a pole. Six hundred years in the throat, and it was still straining toward the mouth.

In the breast pocket of her coveralls, buttoned shut, there was a small hard shape. Lucan undid the button, very gently, with his gloved fingers. It was a bolt. A plain steel bolt, the kind that held a cradle-frame together. He held it in his palm and looked at it and did not know what it was, and put it back, and buttoned the pocket.

Isra did not put a rag over her face.

"The recorder," said Pike, behind them. "Under the console. They always had them, the old ones. Built to keep."

It was where he said. A box of dull grey alloy under the console, its own hull, its own seals, unbroken. Lucan put a cable from the Gristle's board to it, as Isra had to the Bell, by guesswork, by the colour of the wires. It took him a long time. Then the board in Isra's helmet said *external load*, and the little grey box under the console gave a soft click, and a light on its face that had been dark for six hundred and forty-six years came on, amber, and blinked.

And a voice came into their helmets.

"*This is Teodora Kesh.*"

A woman's voice. Low, roughened, a little breathless, very close, as if she were speaking with her mouth almost against the microphone. An accent none of them had ever heard. Isra's hand went out, without her deciding it, and closed on the back of the pilot's seat.

"*Pilot. Small Mercy. Out of Dock Four, Anchorage. First of May, twenty-two ninety-one. I'm in the throat. I'm not a Keeper. I want that on the record before anything else. My name's on a list of Keepers and I'm not one. I've got two tonnes of glass in my cradle and I'm not going to release it.*"

They listened.

They stood in the tiny frozen cockpit of the Small Mercy, at the edge of the river of the dead, the three of them, and listened to Teodora Kesh tell them who she was. A tug pilot. Forty thousand marks of debt she had inherited from her mother and had paid off by agreeing to fly into the Needle. A brother called Nico, a glass-cutter, in a cell under a station called Anchorage, for a murder nobody did. A friend called Corrigan Dace, who had flown in first, alone, a month before, and not come back — *a pilot,* she said, *not a Keeper; say that; Lulu scratched it on the Tally.* And a man called Ambrose Lyle.

"*He was the Senior Throat-Reader at the Lectern for twenty-three years,*" said the voice, "*and on the twenty-fourth of April he was murdered for what's on the spool in this recorder. He heard the Needle breathe. He's got it on here. You'll hear it. It doesn't need us. It's never needed us. The Narrowing's natural, it comes and goes on its own, it's been doing it since before there were people. The Director knew. Hesper Vane. Director of the Concession. She knew, and she made the Keeping up anyway, to keep us cutting after the Concern was gone. Her own memo's on here. Nine pages. It says* proceed *at the bottom.*"

Lucan made a small sound in his helmet.

"*And there's something else on here,*" said Teo Kesh. "*Ambrose worked it out. The glass we put in doesn't go anywhere. It sticks. At the waist. It builds up. He says sixty Keepings and it'll choke. Six hundred and fifty years. Hesper Vane said nobody'll be alive to care.*" A pause. The breath came close to the microphone. "*Well. If you're hearing this, I suppose somebody is.*"

Isra did not realise she was crying until she could not see the amber light.

The voice went on. The waist. The others releasing their glass, one after another, and the flare, and the ringing. *Something stayed,* she said. *I can hear it.* A woman called Marguerite going past on the way home, crying and laughing. *Tell them it doesn't need us. Tell them his name. Ambrose Lyle. Tell them Nico Kesh didn't kill anybody.* And then: *Turning into the wave.* And then a long time with nothing in it but engines, and breathing, and under both, faint, very faint, a sound Isra knew. A low, slow, vast rising and falling, like someone sleeping in the next room.

"*I'm past the waist,*" said Teo Kesh. Her voice had changed. It was quiet now, and slow, and full of something that was not fear. "*I think I'm past it. There's no shear. There's nothing. It's light. All of it. It's on my hands. The engines are running and I don't think I'm moving. Or everything's moving with me. I can't tell.*" A breath. "*I can hear it breathing. Ambrose, you should hear this. You should be here. You'd know what it was.*"

A long silence.

"*Clock says four minutes. Clock says — no. Clock's wrong. Clock's stopped.*" Another breath. "*I don't think I'm going through. I don't think there's a through. Not tonight. I think it's holding me. Like it's holding its breath and I'm inside the breath.*" She laughed, very softly, a sound so close and so alive that Pike, behind Isra, made a noise like something breaking. "*It's all right. It's — it's all right. The recorder'll keep. It's built to keep. Ambrose said the Needle keeps what you give it. I don't think he meant this. But it will.*"

Silence. The breath, rising and falling.

"*Nico. I kept my promise. I didn't feed it. Remember.*"

Silence.

"*Whoever you are,*" said Teodora Kesh. "*Whenever you are. It doesn't need you. It never did. Don't give it anything. Don't let anyone tell you it's hungry. It's just breathing.*" A long pause. "*Let it breathe.*"

And then, very softly, so softly the recorder barely caught it, in a voice full of wonder:

"*Oh. It's beautiful. I didn't know.*"

The amber light went on blinking. There was nothing else on the channel but the breath — the recorder's own, six hundred and forty-six years old, the long slow sound of the Needle sleeping in the next room — and then a soft click, and the spool began, and a man's voice, old and gentle and precise, said, "*This is the long breath. Forty days of it, in forty minutes. You don't have to understand anything. Just listen.*"

Lucan switched it off.

None of them said anything for a long time.

"For Clare," said Lucan at last. He said it to the frozen cockpit. He was holding the side of the canopy with one gloved hand, as if he needed to. "The Fork. In the Bell. *For Cl.* She said his name. Ambrose Lyle. His daughter's name was — " He stopped. "It doesn't say *For Clarity*. It never did. It says *For Clare*."

Isra did not know what he meant. She did not ask. She would ask later, and he would tell her, and she would tell it to somebody else, and they would tell it to somebody else, and it would be wrong by the time it reached the end, as everything was. Wrong is better than nothing. You can fix wrong.

She reached out and took the bead at Teo Kesh's throat in her gloved fingers — very gently, the way you would take a child's hand — and lifted the cord over the frozen head, and held it. It lay in her palm, a nub of cloudy glass the size of a pea, and leaned, insistently, toward the mouth. Toward the Eye.

Then she reached behind the pilot's seat for the cittern, which she had brought over without knowing why, the way you bring a thing you cannot put down, and set it on her knee in the tiny cockpit, the way Hanne used to, and plucked the low string. Once.

The old note went out into the cold. Round. True. In her palm, the bead hummed back. Against her chest, the shard. Somewhere behind them, two million kilometres away, under a dome, the First Warden's harp, still tuned to it. And under all of them, faint, steady, patient, like someone sleeping in the next room, the Needle, breathing.

"I hear you," said Isra Venn to the woman in the pilot's seat. "Teodora Kesh. Pilot. I hear you."

She plucked the string again, and began, for the first time in her life, to sing.
