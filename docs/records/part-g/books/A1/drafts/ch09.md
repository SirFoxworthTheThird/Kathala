## True Wire

The new wire would not lie down.

Lucan had strung four courses of it now, the whole of the girl's spool, and every morning he tuned them to the Hunger Tone the way he had been taught, standing with his eyes shut and the note in his throat, turning the pegs a hair at a time until the wire stopped fighting and lay down inside the sound. And every evening, when he came back to the hall, the new courses had risen up again. Not drifted. That was what he kept coming back to, lying awake in his cell. Old wire drifted: it went slack, it went flat, it crept, and you heard it creep. This was not that. The new wire stayed exactly as tight as he had left it. It was the *note* that would not hold. As if the wire knew some other note, and kept leaning toward it, the way a compass needle leans north however you turn the box.

And the girl's glass had hummed at an old woman's cittern, and lain dead in his palm at the harp.

He thought about that for ten nights. On the eleventh, a little after two in the morning, he got up and put on his robe and went down through the dark spindle to the harp hall, and stood in front of the reliquary grille, looking at the Fork.

It lay on its grey cushion in the lamplight the way it had lain all his life: fifteen centimetres of brass, worn bright at the tips. *The First Warden's Fork.* He had been taught that it was the holiest thing in the Reach. He had been taught that it was struck once at every Keeping and never otherwise touched except by the Prior's own hand, to be cleaned. He had been taught, by the Prior, at seventeen, kneeling on this floor, that the Hunger Tone was the note the First Warden had heard in the Needle, and that the Fork was the instrument with which the First Warden had heard it.

He had never once been taught that they were the same note. He had assumed it. Everybody had.

The grille was locked. It had been locked for as long as he had been at the Anchor, and the Prior had the key on a chain round his neck. But the Anchor had been built for six thousand people and was lived in by twelve, and Lucan had spent twelve years repairing it with whatever came to hand, and he knew that the grille's hinge-pins on the left side had rusted half through in the damp of the hall and could be lifted out with a pair of pliers and a prayer. He had brought the pliers. He said the prayer anyway.

The Fork was heavier than he had expected. Cold. He held it in his palm in the lamplight and his hand was shaking.

He went out into the middle of the hall, in front of the harp, under the huge dark leaning dome of the mouth, and struck it against the heel of his hand.

The note came up out of it, thin and clean. It went out into the hall and met the harp's long drone, and the two of them fought. *Wah. Wah. Wah.* The slow throb of two notes not quite the same. It went on until the Fork died in his hand.

And the four new courses — the girl's wire, the Compact wire — *rang*. Not much. A faint bright shimmer in the chord, as if they had heard something they had been waiting for.

Lucan stood in the middle of the hall with the Fork in his hand.

Then he took off his robe, because it was in the way, and knelt down at the base of the frame in his shirt, and began.

It took him the rest of the night. Nine courses, two hundred and sixteen wires, each one tuned not to his own throat but to the Fork: strike it, hold the stem to the frame, listen to the beat, turn the peg a hair, strike it again, until the beat slowed and slowed and stopped and the wire and the Fork were one note. It was slow and his hands were cold and twice he had to stop and sit back on his heels and breathe, because what he was doing was, by every rule he had ever been taught, a sacrilege — retuning the First Warden's harp, with the First Warden's relic, against the Hunger Tone. He did it anyway. Somewhere around the fifth course he stopped being frightened. Somewhere around the seventh the harp began to sound different, and he stopped thinking about anything at all.

At a little before six, with the grey light of the mouth beginning to brighten in the dome the way it did at what the Anchor called morning, he turned the last peg on the last wire of the ninth course, and stood up, and listened.

It was not the Hunger Tone.

He had known that it would not be. He had not known what it would be instead. The harp did not drone now; it *rang*. The long wide many-stranded chord he had listened to every day of his life, the sound he had always heard as a held breath, an ache, a hunger, was gone, and in its place there was something he had never heard: a deep, hard, layered resonance, like a great bell struck very far away, and struck again, and again, each time a little differently, so that it seemed to have no end. Under it the old throat-noise still ran, faint now, small, like wind in the cracks of a door. The bell was over it. The bell was enormous.

He went to the old reader's console at the side of the hall and woke it, and its screen came up, and the waist trace drew itself across the glass in pale green as the harp sang into it.

For twelve years he had looked at that trace. Every reader had. A long line — the throat — narrowing toward a point, the waist, and at the waist a narrow gap, the passage, which had been getting narrower Narrowing by Narrowing for as long as anybody could remember. Nineteen per cent of nominal. A starving throat. An empty, closing door.

The trace on the screen now was not that.

The throat narrowed toward the waist as it always had. But at the waist there was no gap. There was a shape. A mass — he had no other word — filling the waist almost wall to wall, so dense that the trace went bright and solid across it, and so large that the throat bulged around it like a gullet around something swallowed whole. It was not smooth. Its outline was ridged, banded, layered, like the side of a cut cliff, like the root of the girl's shard. And through the middle of it, not where the passage should have been but squeezed off to one side, crooked, there ran a single narrow crack: all that was left of the way through.

He sat and looked at it while the harp rang its great slow bell above him.

The throat was not empty. It was full. All the readings of all the readers for all the years he knew of — *the waist at nineteen*, *the waist at twenty-four*, *it is starving, it is closing* — had been readings taken with the harp out of tune; and out of tune by exactly enough, he understood now, slowly, his skin going cold, that the ringing of the thing at the waist fell between the strings and was not heard. Tuned to the Hunger Tone, the harp heard a gap closing. Tuned to the Fork, it heard what was closing it.

"It's not starving," Lucan said aloud, to nobody. His voice was very small in the hall. "It's choking."

## The Shard Sings
[

The message came to the bone-pickers' board at the Pans the next morning, scrawled on a scrap of order vellum and sent down on the Anchor's supply shuttle in a sealed tube addressed, in a hasty hand, to *Isra Venn, Gristle, bone-picker — URGENT — please*. It said: *Bring the glass. Come today. Don't tell anyone. — L.*

She went. She went without Pike, because Pike had a fever, and because something in the hand made her want to go alone. It took her six hours. The Gristle's heater died twice on the way and she kicked it back to life twice, and she came in to Dock Four with her teeth chattering, and he was waiting on the dock, and he looked as though he had not slept in two days.

"You came," he said.

"You said *please*."

"I did. Sorry. I'm — come and see. Come and see, and then tell me I'm mad. I'd quite like someone to tell me I'm mad."

He took her up to the harp hall at something close to a run, and at the iron door he stopped, and put his hand on her arm to stop her too, and said, "Listen."

She listened.

It came through the door before she was even in the hall: a sound she had never heard. Low, huge, layered. Like a bell, but no bell she knew — like a bell the size of a town struck once at the bottom of a sea, and the sea carrying the sound up to you slowly, in waves, each wave a little different from the last. She felt it in the bones of her face. The shard against her chest, under her shirt, on its cord, gave a single sharp *tick* against her breastbone, like a fingernail on glass, and then began to hum.

She took it out. It was humming in her palm like a live thing, so hard that it buzzed against her skin. Stronger than at Hanne's cittern. Much stronger.

Lucan looked at it and let out a long breath.

"I tuned the harp to the Fork," he said.

She stared at him. "You said it was a relic."

"It is a relic. I tuned the harp to it anyway. All night." He pushed the door open. "Come and look."

He showed her the screen. He explained it twice, the second time more slowly, with his hands, drawing the throat in the air between them: the long line, the narrowing, the waist. The gap that every reader since the Quiet had been watching close. And then the thing that was not a gap — the great ridged mass on the screen filling the waist almost from wall to wall, the crooked thread of passage squeezed round its edge.

"That's glass," Isra said.

He looked at her.

"That's glass." She was sure. She did not know how she was sure, except that she had spent ten years cutting it off the hulls of dead ships and knew its shapes the way Hanne knew songs. She held up the humming shard and turned it so the light went into its root, into the fine pale bands packed thin as pages. "Look at the edge of your thing. On the screen. Look at the lines. It's the same as this. It's layered like this." She heard her own voice rising. "It's glass, Lucan. There's a — a lump of glass in the throat the size of — "

"The size of the Anchor," he said quietly. "Rather larger, I think."

She lowered the shard. It hummed on in her fist.

"The Returns," she said slowly.

"Yes."

"They're all coated in it. They all come out with it grown on them backwards. Swept back. Like hair in a wind." She was working it out as she said it. "Like they were *pushed*. Like something pushed them out through all that, through the glass, and the glass grew on them while they were — "

"The throat's trying to clear itself," said Lucan. "I think. I think that's what the Returns are. The throat flexes, every Narrowing, and things that are stuck in it get squeezed loose, a few at a time, and come out through the mouth. Ships, mostly. Whatever's been put in it. And the glass — " He rubbed his eyes under his spectacles. "I don't know about the glass. I don't know why there's so much. I don't know where it came from. I don't know anything except that for at least as long as the order's books go back, every reader at the Anchor has been looking at that thing through a harp tuned so it couldn't be heard, and calling it hunger."

They stood side by side in front of the screen with the great slow bell ringing over them.

"The Lamplighter's going to put a hundred skiffs into that," Isra said. "A hundred. With every bit of glass in the Reach."

Lucan said nothing.

"Into *that*." She pointed at the screen with the humming shard. "Into a throat that's choking on glass, she's going to — "

"Yes," Lucan said. "I know."

## For Clarity
[

The Prior came into the hall at noon, on his two sticks, alone, and stood in the doorway, and listened.

Lucan had known he would come. The whole station could hear the harp now; it rang through the spindle like a bell through a church, and the other Wardens had been gathering in the passages all morning, whispering, and not coming in. The Prior did not whisper. He stood in the doorway in his grey with the frayed cuffs and his white head lifted, listening, for a long time. Then his eyes went to the reliquary grille, with its hinge-pins lying on the floor beneath it, and the empty grey cushion.

"Where is it?" he said.

Lucan took the Fork out of his sleeve and held it up.

Isra, beside him, felt the old man's look go over her and dismiss her, the way you would dismiss a chair. It rested on Lucan. It rested on the Fork. Then, slowly, on the screen.

"Explain," said Prior Saar.

Lucan explained. He did it better the third time; Isra listened to him do it and thought that he had found the words now, standing in front of the man who had taught him to sing the Hunger Tone at seventeen. The new wire that would not lie down. The shard. The Fork, and the beat. The night's work. The bell. The thing in the throat. He took the Prior to the screen and showed him, and the old man stood leaning on his sticks and looked at it, at the great banded mass in the waist and the crooked thread of the passage, for a very long time without speaking.

"Twelve years," the Prior said finally, to the screen. "I've looked at that trace for fifty-eight years, Brother. Twelve with you." He did not say anything else for a while.

"Prior," said Lucan. "The Lamplighter — "

"I know."

"If she takes a hundred skiffs into that and releases — "

"I *know*, Lucan." The old man turned from the screen. His face, Isra saw, was grey, and very calm, the calm of somebody who has been hit and has not yet fallen down. "Put it back."

Lucan stared at him.

"The Fork. Put it back on its cushion, and put the pins back in the grille. Then retune the harp. To the Hunger Tone. All of it. Tonight."

"Prior — "

"Her reader is coming tomorrow." Saar's voice was perfectly level. "The Lamplighter's. For the readings. You know she is; she said so. He will come into this hall and he will hear that." He moved his head slightly toward the ringing harp. "And then he will look at that screen. And then he will go back to Tallow and tell Mercy Oduya that the Wardens of the Throat have desecrated the First Warden's Fork, and tuned the harp to a heresy, and are saying that the Needle is not hungry but sick — sick with *glass*, Brother. Sick with what she has spent twenty-five years telling the Reach to give it. Sick with what her son flew into it." He did not raise his voice. "What do you think she will do?"

Lucan was silent.

"I'll tell you what I think," said the Prior. "I think she will come here with her black coats, and her Deacon, and she will burn this hall. And the harp. And the Fork. And us. And then she will fly her hundred skiffs into the throat anyway, with nobody left alive who can read it, and that will be the end of it." He shifted on his sticks. "There are twelve of us. I keep telling you. *Twelve.*"

"So we say nothing."

"So we wait. So we think. So we do not hand her the match." Saar held out his hand, old and shaking, palm up. "The Fork, Brother."

Lucan looked at the Fork in his own hand. Isra watched him look at it. Then he stepped forward and laid it in the Prior's palm, and the old man's fingers closed over it.

"Retune the harp," Saar said. "Tonight." He turned toward the door on his sticks, and stopped, and without looking round said, in a different voice, an old man's voice, almost gentle: "It says *For Clarity*, you know. On the stem. I used to think that was the finest thing ever written on anything." He was quiet for a moment. "Retune the harp."

He went out. They heard his sticks go away down the passage, tap and tap, very slowly.

Lucan stood in the middle of the hall with his empty hands at his sides. Isra waited. After a long time he went to the console and, without a word, plugged a little data-stick into its side, and copied the trace onto it, and pulled it out, and put it in her hand, and closed her fingers round it.

"Not here," he said. "Not in the Anchor. Somewhere stupid. Somewhere nobody would look."

"I live in the Gutter," said Isra. "Everywhere's somewhere stupid."

## Rope Street
[
  Place: #The Gutter
  Characters: @@Isra Venn @@Ostap Pike @@Hanne Venn
  When: 18 May 2937
]
She got back to the Pans after midnight and found the bone-pickers' dock full of black coats.

They had come at the shift-change again. They had come down on the dock with lamps and crowbars and gone through every skiff tied up there — the Coopers', the Mallon girls', old Teodric's, the Gristle's empty berth — and taken up the deck plates and opened the tanks and cut into the insulation, looking for glass. They had found some. Bone-pickers always kept some back. They had found enough to be angry about the rest, and they had dragged three people out onto the dock and beaten them in front of the others, slowly, under the lamps, so that everybody would remember.

One of the three had been Pike.

He had not even been on the dock. He had come down from the Gutter with his fever on him, the Mallon girls said, because someone had run up Rope Street shouting that the Lantern was on the boats, and he had thought of the Gristle; and he had walked out onto the dock in his shirt, coughing, to stand in front of her empty berth, as though there were something there to protect. And the boys with the lamps had asked him where the Gristle was and where her glass was, and he had told them he didn't know, and they had asked him again.

Isra found him on Hanne's bed in the room at the end of Rope Street, with Hanne sitting beside him holding a wet rag to his face. His ribs were strapped with strips torn off one of Hanne's old shawls. One eye was shut. When he breathed it whistled, and when he coughed it was so bad that Hanne had to hold him up.

"Didn't tell them," he said, when he saw Isra. It came out in a wet whisper. "Didn't know. You didn't tell me where you were going. Good girl."

She sat down on the floor by the bed with the data-stick in her fist and her other hand on his big broken hand, and for a long time she did not say anything at all.

Hanne watched her over the rag with her bird's eyes.

"Well?" said the old woman at last, low. "Where *were* you going?"

Isra opened her fist and looked at the little stick lying in her palm.

"To see what's in the throat," she said. "Gran. I saw it. It's not hungry." She looked up. "It's full of glass. It's choking on it. And she's going to give it more."

Hanne did not say anything. She put the rag down on the edge of the bowl and reached across Pike and took her cittern from where it leaned against the wall, and set it on her knee, and plucked the low string, once, very softly, so as not to wake anybody in the tunnel.

Inside Isra's shirt, against her skin, the shard hummed back.
