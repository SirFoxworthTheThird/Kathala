## Raid
[
  Place: #Anchorage Station
  Characters: @@Juno Asante @@Danny Marsh @@Nan Hollis @@Rhee Calder:remote
  Items: ^Vigil:@Juno Asante
  When: 7 November 2608
]
The Shoals came for the glass at four in the morning, Anchorhold time, out of the shadow of the mouth itself, which nobody had thought possible.

"Contacts," said Marsh, and then, in a different voice, "Contacts, Captain, *contacts* — eleven, twelve, fourteen, bearing zero-zero-zero, they're coming out of the *shear*, how are they flying in the shear — "

Juno was on the bridge in her shirt, with her boots unlaced. She did not remember getting there. On the main display the mouth filled the forward quarter, and against its grey-gold rim fourteen small dark shapes were sliding out of the fire, one after another, in a long loose line, riding the shear-waves down toward the Anchorhold the way surfers ride a swell.

"They've got readers," said Juno. "They've got a reader aboard calling the waves. Somebody's taught them." She heard her own voice, flat and calm, the command voice, and was grateful for it. "Battle stations. Wake up the forward battery. Hollis, I want everything. Signal the Anchorhold: enemy strike inbound from the mouth, fourteen vessels, they're going for the stores. Signal Squadron. Where's the *Constant*?"

"Refitting at Tallow, Captain. It's us and the *Ardour* and the station guns."

"Then it's us and the *Ardour* and the station guns."

The *Vigil* had been on station at the Anchorhold for a week. Squadron had brought her forward from the twentieth after the Shoals hit the glass convoys off Dorcas twice in a fortnight; Juno had not complained. She had spent the week walking the Anchorhold's empty passages at night, and sitting in the harp hall, once, at the back, watching her sister sit in front of the harp with her eyes closed, and leaving before Pell saw her. The glass for the Thirtieth Keeping — eighteen tonnes of it, the best that Hollin had cut in a decade — was stowed in the holds of the Anchorhold's dead wheel, the one that had not turned since the Concession, behind armoured doors. That was what the Shoals had come for. Everybody knew that was what they had come for. You could not fly a Keeping without glass.

The fourteen came on. They were cutters, mostly, Shoals-built, fast and lightly armed — and two heavier ships behind them, frigates, old Compact hulls with new paint.

"The big one," said Marsh. "Captain. Look at her flag."

Juno looked. On the display the lead frigate's identification beacon had lit, which no Shoals ship ever did in action; and the name on it was *CALLUM*.

"Open channel from the *Callum*, Captain. All frequencies."

"Put it on."

"*Anchorhold, this is Rhee Calder of the Free League.*" His voice. Older, rougher, but his. "*We are here for the glass. We are not here for you. We are not here for the Keepers. Get your people out of the dead wheel and off the second wheel. Get the Keepers out. You have six minutes. After that we fire on the stores.*" A pause. "*Juno, if you're out there — I'm sorry. Get your sister off the second wheel.*"

The channel closed.

The *Ardour*'s captain was on the command link before Juno could speak, a fat, frightened, decent man called Pryce whom she had known for years. "Asante. Asante, did you — "

"I heard."

"The Keepers' dormitory's on the second wheel. Asante, if they fire on the dead wheel the second's right beside it — "

"I know where it is." She was looking at the display. Fourteen ships. Six minutes. "The station guns will engage the cutters. You take the second frigate. The *Callum* is mine." She switched channels. "Anchorhold, *Vigil*. Evacuate the second wheel. Evacuate the archive section and the Keepers. Get them into the spindle. Do it now."

The Anchorhold's answer came back a long ten seconds later, in the voice of a Warden guard-captain who sounded as though he had been woken by it. "*Vigil, the High Warden orders that the Keepers remain at their stations. The Keepers are under the protection of the order. The Shoals will not fire on — *"

"The Shoals have just told you they're going to fire on the dead wheel," Juno said. "The dead wheel is two hundred metres from your Keepers. Get them out."

"*The High Warden's orders, Commander — *"

She cut him off. "Helm. Take us in. Between the *Callum* and the dead wheel."

"Captain?"

"You heard me, Linnet. Put us in the way."

They went in. The *Vigil* came round the Anchorhold's spindle on full burn and put herself across the *Callum*'s approach, between the frigate and the dead wheel, close enough to see the new paint on the frigate's flank and the scorched place on her bow where some Compact gunner had once nearly done for her. The forward battery fired. The *Callum* fired back. The *Vigil* shook like a struck bell, and somewhere aft something tore, and the bridge lights went red, and Hollis in engineering began to swear in a long steady monotone that was, Juno knew from six years of it, the sound of the Chief having fun.

It went on for nine minutes. Afterward, nobody on the bridge could agree about the order things happened in.

The cutters went for the dead wheel. The station guns got four of them. The *Ardour* took the second frigate in the engines and it went spinning off into the shear and broke up there, silently, in long bright pieces. The *Vigil* and the *Callum* traded fire at a range where you could not miss, and the *Vigil* lost her number two battery and a third of her starboard plating and seven crew, and the *Callum* lost her forward guns. And at the fifth or sixth minute — before the six were up, before anyone had been evacuated from anywhere — two of the Shoals cutters came round the dead wheel's far side, where nobody was watching, and fired everything they had at the armoured doors of the glass stores.

The doors held. The wheel did not.

The shots went high, into the wheel's spokes, into the old Concession structure that had not turned in three hundred years; and the spokes, which were very old, let go. The dead wheel tore free of the spindle on one side and swung — slowly, horribly, like a door on a broken hinge — and hit the second wheel.

Juno saw it on the display. She saw the second wheel's rim crumple where the dead wheel struck it. She saw the second wheel's windows go dark in a long running ripple, section by section. She saw, from one of the dark sections, a sudden white plume of air and debris and small tumbling shapes venting into the grey-gold light of the mouth.

The Keepers' dormitory was on the second wheel.

"Danny," she said. "You have the ship."

"Captain — "

"You have the ship. Pull back. Cover the station. Let the *Callum* go if she runs. Hollis, I need a damage-control party at the forward lock in two minutes, suited, with cutting gear. Get me as close to the spindle as you can and let me off."

"Juno," said Marsh, very quietly, not using her rank, for the first time in two years. "Juno, the *Callum*'s still — "

"*Callum*'s breaking off," said the helm. "Captain, she's breaking off. They're all breaking off. They're running for the mouth."

On the display the Shoals ships were turning away, all of them at once, the surviving cutters falling in behind the crippled *Callum* in a long loose line, back toward the shear and the fire at the rim of the mouth, the way they had come. Nobody pursued them. Nobody had anything left to pursue them with.

The channel from the *Callum* opened once more, on all frequencies, for a few seconds.

"*That wasn't* — " Rhee's voice, raw. "*That wasn't us. That wasn't the plan. The wheel — Juno, I didn't — *"

It closed.

## The Archive Burning
[
  Place: #The archive
  Characters: @@Adaeze Quill @@Juno Asante @@Pell Asante @@Ezra Lind:dead
  Items: ^The Fork:@Adaeze Quill
  Relationships: ~Pell Asante/Ezra Lind:ended
  When: same day
]
The archive was on fire.

Adaeze did not know how. The archive was in the spindle, not the wheels; it should have been the safest place in the Anchorhold. But the dead wheel's spokes ran into the spindle's heart, and when the wheel tore loose it had wrenched something deep in the structure, and somewhere behind the racks an old Concession power conduit had split and was spitting fire into three hundred years of paper. She had been at her desk, as she was most nights, with her private notebook open. She had smelled the smoke before the alarms. She had gone out into the archive and seen the far end of it lit up orange.

She did not leave. She did not consider leaving. She ran the other way, toward the oldest racks, toward the fire, with her sleeve over her face.

The oldest spools were crystal and would survive heat, a while. The paper would not. She pulled the paper first — the tuning logs, the daybooks, the bound volumes of the first readers after the Blank Year — and threw them into an armful and ran them out into the passage and dumped them on the floor and went back. The smoke got thicker. On the third trip she could not see the racks at all, and found them by touch, and her eyes were streaming and her lungs had begun to feel like something torn. On the fourth trip she remembered the Fork.

The reliquary was off the harp hall, not the archive. It was not burning. But the harp hall was on the far side of the archive from the passage, and the archive was a corridor of fire between them, and she stood in the smoke with her arms full of daybooks and thought, quite clearly, *If the hall goes, the Fork goes,* and dropped the daybooks, and went through.

She would not remember most of it. Heat on the side of her face. Her robe catching at the hem and her beating it out with her bare hands. The iron door of the harp hall, hot to the touch, and then the hall itself, clear, cool, enormous, the harp singing under the dome as if nothing in the world were wrong, and the mouth hanging over it, and — sitting on the floor in front of the harp in her nightshirt, with a body across her knees — Pell Asante.

Adaeze stopped.

It was Ezra Lind. His face was blue. There was frost on his lashes and in his hair. Pell had his head in her lap and was holding it, with both hands, very carefully, the way you would hold a bowl of water, and looking down into his face, and not crying.

"He came to find me," said Pell. She said it to Adaeze as if continuing a conversation. "When the wheel went. I was here. I'm always here at night. He knew. He came through the dormitory to find me and the section went while he was in it, and he got the door shut behind him, and he got as far as the spindle lock, and somebody pulled him in." She looked up. "He walked here. On his own. He walked all the way here from the lock. He wanted to tell me he'd found me." She looked down again. "And then he sat down."

"Pell," said Adaeze.

"He failed his sled licence twice," said Pell. "He told me. He said the Needle had made a clerical error." And then she did cry, all at once, bending over the dead boy's face with her whole body shaking and no sound coming out of her at all.

Adaeze went to the reliquary. She did that first. She would think about that, afterward, for a long time — that she had gone to the grille before she went to the girl — and she would never be sure whether it had been faith or something less. She unlocked the grille with her archivist's key, and took the Fork from its grey cushion, and put it inside her robe, against her skin, where it lay cold and heavy. Then she went and knelt beside Pell on the floor in front of the harp and put her arms round her, and the dead boy too, and held them both.

That was how Juno Asante found them, eleven minutes later, coming through the iron door from the burning archive in a damage-control suit with a cutting torch in one hand and three of her crew behind her, with her faceplate up and her face black with smoke.

She stopped in the doorway, as Adaeze had.

Then she came across the hall and knelt down in front of her sister.

"Pell."

Pell lifted her head. She looked at Juno for a long moment as if she did not know her. Then she said, quite clearly, "Juno, he's dead," and Juno said, "I know, I know he is, I'm here," and took her sister's face in her two gloved hands.

They got everyone out through the harp hall's far door, the one that led up the spindle away from the fire: Adaeze, and Pell, and two of Juno's crew carrying Ezra Lind between them on a stretcher made of a Warden's cloak. On the way past the passage door Adaeze stopped, and Juno, behind her, saw what she was looking at: the heap of daybooks and bound volumes and tuning logs on the passage floor, where she had dumped them, singed, smoke-blackened, lying in a spreading pool of the foam the station's suppressors had finally begun to spray.

"Those," said Adaeze. "Please. Those."

Juno looked at her, and at the books, and did not ask. "Marsh. Get those. All of them."

The *Vigil*'s wardroom was the size of a large cupboard and smelled of burned insulation, and at two in the morning, twenty-two hours after the raid, it held the captain, the archivist, a heap of singed books, and a brass tuning fork lying on the table between them on a folded napkin.

Pell was asleep in Juno's cabin, sedated by the ship's medic. The other surviving Keepers were in the Anchorhold's spindle, in the High Warden's own guest rooms, under guard. Four of the nine had died when the second wheel was struck: Ezra Lind, and Tavish Orme, and the baker, and the woman who had once been a novice. The Wardens had issued a statement within the hour. *The Shoals have murdered the Keepers of the Thirtieth. The Draw will be held again, for four, at the Compact Hall, in five days.* The Keeping would go on. The glass had survived, behind its armoured doors. The glass always survived.

Adaeze had not let go of the Fork until Juno's medic tried to take her robe off to dress the burns on her hands, and then she had taken it out and laid it on the table and sat looking at it, with her bandaged hands in her lap, for a long time.

"I struck it," she said. "Ten days ago. Did I tell you?"

"No."

"I'm the archivist. I clean it once a month. I've cleaned it two hundred times." She looked at it on its napkin. "Ten days ago I struck it. Against my hand. It's a tuning fork. You're supposed to strike them." She laughed, a little, without much breath. "Your sister asked me once what it was for, a tuning fork you don't tune with. I said it was the sort of question she would ask."

Juno waited.

Adaeze told her. The First Keeping's records. The spool for the second of May that was the first spool in the rack, and the nothing before it. The catalogue: *transferred to the keeping of the First Warden. Not returned.* The forced rack, the sheared clamps. The Blank Year. And the Fork, struck in the alcove, its note going out into the hall and meeting the harp's drone and fighting it. *Wah. Wah. Wah.* The Hunger Tone and the Fork, not the same note.

"I don't know what it means," she said. "I've been trying to think what it means for ten days. Somebody took forty-four years of records out of the archive on the day after the First Keeping and never brought them back. Somebody tuned the harp to a note that isn't the note of the instrument it's supposed to have been tuned by. And the order has been calling the gap a joke for three hundred years." She looked up. "I keep thinking that if it were a mistake, someone would have noticed. Someone would have struck the Fork before me. In three hundred years. Someone." Her voice went very quiet. "Unless they did. And then they did what I've done. Which is nothing."

Juno looked at her for a while. Then she said, "Danny. Would you get it, please."

Marsh, who had been standing at the door, went out. He came back four minutes later with flour on his sleeves and a blank Navy slate in his hand, and put it on the table beside the Fork.

"The galley flour bin," he said, to Adaeze's look. "The Captain said somewhere stupid."

Juno turned the slate round and pushed it across the table, and told her about Mara Teague.

The archivist read the list. She read it slowly, the way she read everything, with her bandaged finger moving down the lines. The half-thickness foils. Nineteen of thirty-six. The sleeve names: four Draws, four names, four careful lines in a dead clerk's hand. She stopped at the last one and read it twice. Then she sat back.

"Teague," she said. "Mara Teague. I knew her. I didn't know she — " She stopped. "She died in custody. I saw the notice. A heart condition."

"Yes."

"She didn't have a heart condition. She used to run the stairs at the Hall at lunchtime. All eight hundred metres. Every day for nineteen years." Adaeze closed her eyes. "Oh, Mara."

"Is it true?" said Juno. "You'd know. You keep the Draw records. Is it true?"

Adaeze did not answer at once. She opened her eyes and looked, not at the slate, but at the singed heap of books on the floor in the corner of the wardroom. Then she got up, stiffly, and knelt down by the heap and went through it with her bandaged hands, slowly, until she found what she was looking for: a thin black ledger with a scorched spine. She brought it back to the table and opened it.

"The Draw ledger," she said. "The archive's copy. The clerks send one up after every Draw; I file it. Every foil, every name, in the order they come out of the Wheel." She turned the pages. "I've always noticed — I thought it was a clerk's mark. A tick. One name in every Draw has a little mark beside it. A small *s*, in the margin. I asked about it once, when I was new. They said it meant the foil had been *sealed*. Checked. I never — " Her finger stopped. "Here. The Twenty-Ninth."

Juno leaned over. The ledger's page, in a clerk's neat hand: nine names. And beside the third, in the margin, very small, an *s*.

*Callum Calder.*

Adaeze turned to the end, to the newest pages, to the Thirtieth, in the same hand, dated the eighth of October. Nine names. She did not have to look for it. Juno could see it from across the table.

*7. Pell Asante, of the Speaker's house.* And in the margin, small and neat and ordinary: *s*.

Neither of them said anything for a long time.

"He chose her," said Adaeze at last. Her voice was perfectly level and her face was grey. "The High Warden. He put her name in his sleeve and drew it out and read it to the Hall. To make your mother — " She stopped. "And he declined your petition. To your face. He told you the Draw was the only fair thing in the Reach."

"Yes," said Juno.

The archivist looked down at the Fork on its napkin. She reached out one bandaged hand and touched it, very lightly, the way you would touch the face of someone sleeping.

"I took the grey," she said, "because somebody has to keep the records honestly." She was quiet. "I've been keeping them honestly for twenty years. All of them. Every name." She laid her hand flat over the Fork. "I just never read the margins."

## The Vote
[
  Place: #The Asante apartment
  Characters: @@Juno Asante @@Efua Asante
  Facts: ?Mor chose Pell:@Efua Asante
  When: 10 November 2608
]
Her mother was at the window when she came in, as if she had not moved from it in a month.

The *Vigil* was in the yards at Tallow for nine days of patching, and Juno had come straight up from the yards in her stained uniform without calling ahead. Efua Asante did not turn round. In the glass Juno could see her reflection laid over the well of lights below — the black and silver, the composed civic face. On the table behind her a slate glowed with the evening's Assembly business. Juno did not have to read it. Everyone in Tallow Under knew. The appropriation for the Shoals campaign had passed two weeks ago, by four votes, with the Speaker's own vote among them. After the raid on the Anchorhold it would have passed by forty. Nobody remembered any more that it had been close.

"You voted for it," Juno said.

"Yes."

"Two weeks after he drew her name."

"Eighteen days," said Efua. "Yes."

"Did he come to you?"

Her mother was silent for a moment. Then she said, to the glass, "Sit down, Juno."

"I don't want to sit down."

"Then stand." Efua turned round. Her face was perfectly composed, and her eyes were not. "He came to me on the ninth of October. The day after the Draw. In this room. He sat where you're standing and drank a cup of tea and told me how sorry he was, and how extraordinary Pell was, and how he had prayed for her. And then he said that the Thirtieth was the deepest Keeping in two hundred years, and that the Shoals had sworn to stop it, and that without the appropriation the Navy could not protect the Keeping, and that Pell's chances of coming back out of the throat would be very much better with a strong escort than a weak one." She paused. "And that he would see to it personally that the best ship in the Home Squadron was given the escort. Commanded by the best captain." Another pause. "He did, didn't he."

Juno felt the floor of the room go out from under her, very slowly, like a lift.

"The *Vigil*," she said. "Our posting. He — "

"Tobiah Greaves didn't choose you for the Keeping, Juno. Cassius Mor did. He asked for you by name. As a favour to me." Her mother's mouth moved in something that was not a smile. "He was very kind about it."

"Did you know?" Juno said. "About the sleeve. Did you know?"

And her mother, the Speaker of the Assembly of the Compact of the Reach, looked at her across the room for a long time, and then nodded once.

"Every Speaker knows," she said. "It's not written anywhere. It's told to you, by the Speaker before you, the night you're elected. That the Wheel is fair except for one foil. That the High Warden is given one name in every Draw, for the good of the Reach. That it's the price of the Keeping, and the Keeping is the price of the door, and the door is the price of everything." Her voice did not change. "I was told on the night of my election, eight years ago. By old Hennessy, at his own dining table, over the cheese. I went home and sat on the edge of the bath and was sick. And then I went to work the next morning, Juno, because what else does one do?" She drew a breath. "I have known for eight years that the High Warden chooses one Keeper in every Draw. I didn't know he'd choose mine. I suppose I thought — " She stopped. "I suppose I thought I'd been careful."

"You voted for a war," said Juno, "to buy my sister an escort."

"I voted for a war that was going to pass with or without me," said her mother, "to keep the one person who might bring my daughter home standing between her and the guns." For the first time, her voice shook. "And it was you. And you did. You brought her out of the harp hall with a dead boy in her lap. I watched the footage. Do not stand in my house and tell me what I bought."

They stood on either side of the room. Below the window the light-well was full of the small lamps of the houses, a well of stars, and from somewhere far down it a voice was singing the Hunger Tone, held and held.

"He's going to draw four more names on Thursday," said Juno. "To replace the dead. Four more sleeves."

"Probably."

"And she's still going in. Pell. She's still going in on the fifth of December, because he chose her, with a foil up his sleeve, to buy your vote. And you've known since the night it happened."

"Yes," said Efua Asante. "What are you going to do about it?"

Juno did not answer. She did not have an answer. She stood in her mother's room in her stained uniform with the list of the dead in her head — Ezra Lind, Tavish Orme, the baker, the novice, Callum Calder, Mara Teague — and found that her hand had gone, by itself, to the breast pocket of her uniform, where a small folded square of paper lay over her heart with a frequency written on it in pencil.

"I don't know yet," she said.

Her mother looked at her hand, and at the pocket, for a long moment. Then she turned back to the window.

"When you do," said the Speaker of the Assembly, to the glass, very quietly, "don't tell me."
