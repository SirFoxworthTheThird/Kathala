## The Prior's Choice
[
  Place: #The Lectern
  Characters: @@Lucan Aske @@Benedek Saar @@Deacon Rusk

Lucan asked the Prior on the fourteenth, because there was nobody else to ask.

He had been building the coil for a week. It was under the harp's base, where it had always been — a ring of old dark copper wound round a core of something he did not recognise, wired into the frame by whoever had built the frame, six hundred years ago, for reasons nobody had written down. He had cleaned it and traced its wires and found the place where they had been cut, long ago, neatly, with a tool, and he had spliced them again with the last of Isra's resonator wire and run a lead from it to the reader's console, hidden under the floor-plates. It would take very little now. Retune the harp to the Fork. Close one switch. And the harp, which had only ever listened, would sing *out* — at the Bell, at the waist, at the thing in the throat — with every course it had.

But someone had to be standing at it when it did. And Lucan would be on the Lamplighter's ship.

So on the night of the fourteenth he went to the Prior's cell and knocked, and the old man let him in, and Lucan sat on the end of his narrow bed under the one lamp and told him everything. The strata. The fifty-nine bands. The Book of Keepings, laid alongside them. The Bell, and the socket, and the Fork. The coil. He told it plainly and in order, and the Prior sat in his chair with his two sticks across his knees and listened without moving, the way he had listened four weeks ago in the harp hall, his face like old paper.

"I need someone at the harp," said Lucan at the end. "On the night. When the Long Night begins and the skiffs go out. I need someone to tune it true and close the switch and hold it. For as long as it takes. I can't. She'll have me on her ship." He looked at the old man. "You taught me to tune it. You're the only other one who can."

Prior Saar was silent for a long time.

"Twelve," he said at last.

"I know."

"Twelve of us, Lucan. Eleven, if they take you." He looked at the lamp. "Do you know what she'll do, if you ring her throat, in front of her hundred Keepers, on the night of her Great Keeping? Whether it works or not? Do you know what she'll do to the station that did it?"

"Yes."

"And you still ask."

"I still ask," said Lucan. "Prior. Her son's in there. A hundred more are going. Ostap Pike is going, with a bead on, because he shot a boy eleven years ago and thinks he owes it. And it's choking. We're choking it. Every one of them who goes in makes it worse. You've seen the bands. You know."

"I know," said the old man. He closed his eyes. "Go to bed, Brother. I'll think on it."

Lucan went to bed.

They came for him at three in the morning, in the harp hall, where he had gone, as he always went, to retune the ninth course and read the night. There were six of them, black coats, with lamps; and the Deacon behind them, his hands clasped behind his back; and behind the Deacon, on his two sticks, at the iron door, not coming in, the Prior.

Lucan stood up from the console. He did not try to run. There was nowhere to run to.

"Brother Lucan," said the Deacon pleasantly. "Good morning. I'm told you've been tuning the First Warden's harp to the First Warden's Fork, at night, in secret, against your Prior's express orders. And that you mean to do something worse on the Long Night. Something to do with — " he glanced at the Prior — "a bell."

Lucan looked past him at the old man in the doorway.

Saar did not look away. His face was grey and perfectly still.

"Twelve," said the Prior. "I'm sorry, Lucan. Twelve."

The black coats took Lucan's arms. One of them went to the reliquary grille and lifted out its hinge-pins — they knew about the hinge-pins; the Prior had told them that too — and found the grey cushion empty, and turned, and the Deacon held out his hand, and Lucan, after a long moment, took the Fork out of his sleeve and gave it to him.

Rusk turned it over in his thin fingers, curiously, and held it up to the lamp.

"*For Cl,*" he read. "Is that all it says?"

"It's worn," said Lucan.

"Hm." The Deacon slipped it into his own coat. "The Lamplighter would still like you as her reader, Brother. She was very clear. She says you're the only one who's ever told her the truth. You'll give her the line from her ship on the night of the twenty-third, as you promised. In the meantime you'll stay in the penitents' cells, under the docks." He smiled. "I'm told they're very old. The Concession built them. For strikers."

They took him down. As he went past the Prior at the door the old man put out one hand, as if to touch his arm; and then did not; and let it fall.

## Tobin
[
  Place: #The Ossuary
  Characters: @@Isra Venn
  Items: ^Gristle:@Isra Venn
  When: same day
]
The Return that came out of the mouth on the fifteenth of June was the first one Isra had ever seen come out.

She was alone in the Ossuary in the Gristle. She had been going out every day since Pike left, because the Lantern let bone-pickers work again now — every Return was tithe, and somebody had to cut it, and the Lantern's own boys were afraid of the mouth — and because if she sat in the greenhouse among the beans she would go mad. She cut what she was told to cut and gave it to the Lantern cutter at the edge of the drift, and was given rations for it, and went back to the Anchor at night and lay awake. She had not seen Lucan in two days. She did not know why. Nobody would tell her.

She was a kilometre from the mouth, in the lee of an old ore-hauler, when the mouth flickered.

She had seen it flicker all her life. Not like this. The bruised dark at the centre swelled, and went pale, and something came out of it the way a fish comes up out of dark water: slowly, and then all at once, tumbling, end over end, with a long trail of glittering dust behind it that was glass, shattering off its hull as it came, a wake of glass, sparkling in the grey light like spray.

A skiff. Small. Twelve metres. A Keeper's skiff.

She went after it before she had decided to.

It was not like the others. That was the first thing. The others came out crusted, furred, their glass grown back along their hulls like hair in a wind, centuries of it. This one had hardly any. A skin of it, thin, milky, a few years' worth — no more. Under it she could see the hull's paint. Black. And on the nose, done in yellow, with a little red flame in it, a lamp.

A Lantern skiff.

She matched spin. She put the grapple on it herself, without Pike, badly, and it took her four tries. Then she went over in her suit and stood on its hull in the grey light at the edge of the mouth and looked at it.

The engine housing at the stern was gone. Not torn; holed. Three neat round holes punched through the plating, one after another, in a line, with the metal curled inward at their edges like the petals of a flower. She knew what made holes like that. She had seen them on the hulls of Shoals wrecks from the old wars, in the deep Ossuary. Forward guns. A cutter's forward guns, at close range.

She went to the canopy. The glass on it was thin enough to see through without cutting. She put her helmet lamp to it and looked in.

He was in his seat. They always were. A boy. Seventeen at the most; a soft round face, a lot of dark curly hair, frozen. His hands were not on the yoke. They were on the cradle release, the white lever, both of them, and the lever had not been pulled. At his throat, a bead. And on the inside of the canopy, above his head, where he would have seen it every time he looked up, someone had painted, carefully, in the same yellow as the lamp on the nose, a name.

TOBIN.

Isra stood on the hull of the skiff for a long time. She did not put a rag over his face. She found she could not.

*I don't want to. I'm sorry. I don't want to.*

*And Rusk said, Fire.*

*I was on the second gun.*

She went back to the Gristle and sat in the pilot's seat with her helmet on her lap, and then she did a thing she had never done in ten years of picking bones. She did not cut. She took the skiff in tow, whole, glass and all, the boy in his seat with his hands on the lever he had not pulled, and turned the Gristle not toward the Lantern cutter at the edge of the drift, but toward the Anchor.

## The Mother
[
  Place: #Dock Four
  Characters: @@Isra Venn @@Mercy Oduya @@Deacon Rusk
  Relationships: ~Mercy Oduya/Deacon Rusk:ended
  When: same day
]
She brought him in to Dock Four and tied him up next to the Lamplighter's black ore-hauler, and climbed out, and stood on the dock beside him, and waited.

The black coats on the dock looked at the skiff, and at the lamp on its nose, and at her, and nobody stopped her. Somebody ran. Ten minutes later the Lamplighter came.

She came alone, at first. Across the huge dark hangar from the lift, in her black coat, fast, faster than Isra had ever seen her move, and then slower, and slower, as she came close enough to see the skiff, and the lamp, and the name through the thin milky glass of the canopy; and then she stopped.

Isra did not say anything.

Mercy Oduya stood on the dock and looked at her son.

After a while she went to him. She climbed the skiff's side the way you would climb a wall in a dream, slowly, not quite there, and knelt on its hull beside the canopy and put her two bare cracked hands flat on the glass over his face. She did not make a sound. She knelt there with her hands on the glass for a long time, while the black coats gathered at the edges of the hangar and did not come closer, and Isra stood on the dock below with her helmet under her arm.

Then the Lamplighter's hands moved on the glass, and stopped, and she leaned forward and looked down along the hull toward the stern. At the engine housing. At three neat round holes in a row, with the metal curled inward like petals.

She looked at them.

"He turned round," said Isra.

The Lamplighter did not move.

"At the Fifty-Ninth. At the mouth. At the start of the Long Night. He turned round. He pulled up and turned and came back out toward the escort." Isra's voice was steady; she did not know how. "And the Deacon told him to go back. And he didn't. He said something, on the open channel. Everyone on the escort heard it. He said, *I don't want to. I'm sorry. I don't want to.*" She swallowed. "And the Deacon said *fire*. And they fired. The *Patience*'s forward guns. And his engine went, and he drifted into the mouth with the pull. And the Deacon said, *Well, he's a Keeper now.*"

The Lamplighter turned her head and looked down at Isra from the skiff's hull.

"Who told you that?"

"Somebody who was on the second gun."

Deacon Rusk had come across the hangar while Isra was speaking. He stood at the foot of the skiff now, a few metres from her, with his hands clasped behind his back, and his pleasant face had gone very still.

"Mercy," he said.

She looked at him.

"He'd been blessed," said the Deacon. "He'd been sung off from the steps. He was carrying a cradle of glass the Needle needed and he was turning *back* with it, in front of six other Keepers, in front of the whole escort, on the open channel. If he'd come home — if they'd seen him come home — " He spread his hands. "You'd have had nobody at the Sixtieth. Nobody. You know you would. You'd have wanted me to. You *would* have, Mercy. You'd have given the order yourself."

The Lamplighter looked at him for a long, long time.

"Yes," she said at last. Very quietly. "I expect I would."

The Deacon let out a breath.

"Get out," said Mercy Oduya.

He stared at her.

"Get off this station. Get on the *Patience* and go back to Tallow and do not come back to me. Do not come to the steps on the night. Do not come to the Lantern House. Go anywhere you like. Go." She did not raise her voice. It did not need raising. "If I see you again, Ezekiel Rusk, I will do what you did. And I will be right to. And I will carry it." She looked at him. "Go."

He went. He walked away across the hangar toward the *Patience* with his hands clasped behind his back, at his ordinary pace, and the black coats at the edges of the hangar moved aside for him, and nobody spoke; and only Isra, watching, saw his right hand come out from behind his back as he went and touch, very briefly, the breast of his coat, where something small and heavy was.

The Lamplighter knelt on her son's hull with her hands on the glass.

"Go away, girl," she said, not looking down.

Isra did not go away. "It's choking," she said.

The Lamplighter's hands went still.

"The Needle. It's not starving. It never was. Lucan tuned the harp to the Fork and saw it. There's a thing in the throat — a mass, glass, as big as a station — at the waist. We counted it. It's in layers. One for every Keeping. Fifty-nine. The two thick ones on top are yours. The Fifty-Eighth and the Fifty-Ninth." Isra's voice cracked at last. "He's in it. Tobin. His glass is in it. That's where it went. It didn't feed anything. It's still there. It's what's killing the door." She took a breath. "And you're going to give it a hundred more."

The Lamplighter turned her head and looked down at her. Her face was wet and still.

"Get out of my sight," she said.

Isra went. At the lift she looked back. The Lamplighter was still kneeling on the hull of the little black skiff in the grey light of the hangar, with her forehead against the canopy now, against the glass, over her son's face, and her shoulders were not moving at all.

## The Grille
[
  Place: #The cells
  Characters: @@Isra Venn @@Benedek Saar @@Lucan Aske

  Relationships: ~Isra Venn/Benedek Saar:Uneasy allies

The Prior found her in the greenhouse at two in the morning, among the beans, sitting on the floor with Hanne's cittern across her knees, not playing it.

She had heard about Lucan by then. Everyone had. The Lamplighter's boys had stopped being afraid of saying things, the day the Deacon left; they had begun to be afraid of other things instead. She had heard that the Warden reader was in the penitents' cells under the docks, and that the Prior had given him up, and that the Deacon had taken the First Warden's Fork from him in the harp hall and put it in his own coat. She had sat on the greenhouse floor since midnight working out how to get onto the *Patience*, which had left for Tallow at six, and how to find the Deacon in Tallow, and what she would do when she did, and she had not got very far.

The old man stood in the doorway on his two sticks, breathing hard from the walk.

"He didn't take it," said Prior Saar.

She looked up.

"Rusk." The old man came in, slowly, and lowered himself onto an upturned seed-tray, which took some time. "He didn't take it to Tallow. He meant to. He took it from Lucan and put it in his coat. And this afternoon, when she sent him off the station, he went to his cabin to pack, and he took it out of his coat, and he stood there looking at it, and then he went down to the harp hall and put it back on its cushion and went." Saar's voice was dry. "One of the novices saw him. He didn't say anything. He just put it back."

"Why?"

"I have no idea," said the Prior. "His mother sang flat." He reached into the breast of his robe and took out something small and heavy and held it out to her across the beans, on his old shaking palm. "I went and got it. An hour ago. Before anyone thought to."

It was the Fork.

Isra did not take it. She looked at it lying in his palm, and then at his face.

"You gave him to them," she said.

"Yes."

"Lucan. You gave him to the Lantern. For twelve."

"For eleven," said the Prior. "Yes. I did." He did not look away. "I've given a great many things to a great many people, girl, to keep eleven old men breathing in a station built for six thousand. I've been doing it for thirty years. I gave her my grey on her steps. I gave her the readings. I gave her Lucan." He held out the Fork. "I find I can't give her this as well. I've been sitting in my cell since the boy came in on your tow-line trying to, and I can't."

She took it. It was cold. It was heavier than it looked, as Lucan had said.

"He's in the fourth cell," said the Prior. "The lay-brothers who guard it are mine. They're asleep. They're old men; they sleep very deeply." He leaned on his sticks and got up off the seed-tray, slowly. "Go and see him. He'll want to tell you about the Bell. He's been telling the wall about it all day."

"And you?" said Isra. "On the night? Will you be at the harp?"

The old man stood among the beans in the green light, eighty-one, on two sticks, in a frayed robe.

"I don't know," said Prior Saar. "I've never in my life known what I would do until I'd done it. That's the trouble with being old. You think you'll have found out." He turned toward the door. "Go and see him, girl."

The penitents' cells were at the bottom of the spindle under the docks, six of them and a guardroom, very old, built of a grey steel that had been painted many times. The two lay-brothers in the guardroom were asleep in their chairs with their mouths open. The fourth cell had a small barred grille in its door at the height of a face.

Lucan's face was in it before she had finished saying his name.

"You've got it," he said. He was looking at her hand. "He gave it to you. The Prior. He — "

"Rusk put it back. The Prior took it. I've got it." She held it up to the grille. "Tell me what to do."

He told her. He talked very fast, with his face pressed to the bars, his spectacles askew, and she listened and did not interrupt, the way she had listened to Hanne. The Bell's end-plate: seat the Fork stem-first in the socket, all the way down, until it locked — he thought it would lock, there would be a catch. The power: the Gristle's main bus, a cable from her external port to the Bell's frame; the Bell would have a port somewhere, it had been built to be serviced, he had seen something like a hatch on its flank through the telescope. The lattice at the tail had to face the waist. If it had drifted, she would have to turn it — use the grapple, gently, Pike's way. And then wait. For the harp. For the Long Night. For someone to close the switch.

"If nobody closes it," said Isra.

"Then the Bell's just a buoy with a fork in it," said Lucan. "And you'll be sitting at the mouth in the shear in the middle of the Great Keeping with a hundred skiffs going past you into the throat." He pressed his forehead against the bars. "Isra. I don't know if anyone will close it."

"Then I'll close it," she said.

"You can't. You'll be at the Bell. You can't be in two places — "

"I'll ring it," said Isra Venn. "Somehow. From the Bell. From the Gristle. I don't know how. I've got the Fork. I've got the shard. I've got Gran's low string." She held up the cittern, which she had brought down with her without thinking, the way you bring a thing you cannot put down. "You said it yourself. It's all one note. The salt and the throat and the Fork and the string. It's all one note, Lucan. Somebody's going to play it on the night. If it's not the harp, it'll be me."

He looked at her through the bars.

"I'll be on her ship," he said. "At the head of the line. Holding them at the markers as long as I can. Telling her the shear's too heavy. Lying to her." He swallowed. "She trusts me."

"I know."

"She'll know. When it starts. She'll know it was me."

"I know."

"Pike's on the list," he said. "Number forty. I heard the boys reading it out."

"I know that too."

They looked at each other through the little barred grille in the old grey door, with the lay-brothers snoring in the guardroom behind her, eight days before the Sixtieth Narrowing. Then Lucan put his hand through the bars, as far as it would go, and she took it, and held it, with the Fork in her other hand and the cittern against her hip, until the old men in the guardroom began to stir.
