# Judgement: the first book (Q) against the second book (T)

I read both books in full, every chapter, the first book (Q) first. Q runs to about 75,000 words and T to about 99,000. Neither is padded in any way that hurts it. T's extra length goes mostly into a third narrative voice and a longer middle-era mystery, not into repeated material.

**The first book (Q).** The eras are *The Closing* (2387 CE: Dagny Ostrova, Teodor Basu, Hale Varga, Kas), *The Owing* (311 AC, about 2698 CE: Tamsin Hask, Orrin Sallow, Wenna Blackwood, Imre Vosk) and *The Healing* (3089 CE: Corvin Saal, Fen Adaire, Quennell Haste). The anchors are the bell of the *Ardent Mercy* and the empty Hook it hung from, the Tally and its root console, the Director's seal (which becomes the Count Key), the buried *Covenant* (which becomes "the Hulk"), the Wick, and the Twilight Song.

**The second book (T).** The eras are *The Crossing* (CR 611: Annick Vell, Dace, Tobiah Ruck, Nadia Szell, Edwin Quarry), *The Account* (CR 929: Hollis Fen, told in the first person, with Lucan Marsh, Isaak Thorne and Calla Dray) and *The Salvage* (CR 1317: Yara Tull, told in the present tense, with Mitya, Pim, Aurel Kade and Ansel Rhee). The anchors are the Book, key 0447, the ship (later the station, later the wreck), the Ash Case (really Voyage III), Ballast Tank 4 (later "the Fourth Cistern", later "the Widow"), the coil song "Heave the iron", and the brass seal-reader working with the Ash bell.

## The first book (Q)

### 1. Brief compliance: interlacing and three distinct eras and casts: **9/10**
The chapters rotate strictly, Closing, Owing, Healing, seven times over, and each era has its own cast, society and problem: a company town abandoned, a theocracy of debt, and a lien-house recovery voyage. The anchors are excellent and actually carry plot:
- Lemuel Adeyemi's freshly cut name (ch1) turns up on the bell at Port Ambry (ch3).
- The cut shackle-stub Dagny leaves on the Hook (ch16) is found by Tamsin (ch20).
- The *Covenant* becomes "the Hulk" (ch11, ch20).
- The Director's seal becomes the Count Key (ch17, ch20).
- Odile's words on the crown are found by Corvin (ch16, ch18).

One small blur is that the Healing era's Senna Hask is plainly Tamsin's descendant. That is legitimate, but it is the only cast overlap in either book.

### 2. Brief compliance: synchronized pacing: **9/10**
The three arcs rise in step.
- **Inciting blows (ch1–3):** the closing announced, the Account "called", the instrument and the bell.
- **Mid-book discoveries (ch10–12):** the Caul Deep ice and the shear, Hull 7731 and the hidden reserve, then the throat-watch data and the cold-sleep hold.
- **Defeats (ch16–18):** the root ambush and Kas's betrayal, Orrin's arrest and the Calling into the Cold, Ode's death and Corvin signing.
- **Climaxes (ch19, ch20, ch21):** these fall back to back, in chronological order, and each one hands the next its premise: the quit-claim, then the lamp lit and the debt found to be owed to the dead, then the names spoken and the debt discharged.

It is about as simultaneous as a strict rotation allows.

### 3. Brief compliance: no spoilers: **6/10**
The later eras mostly *discover* things the earlier era has already shown, which complies with the brief, but several plants reveal earlier-era outcomes before that era reaches them:
- **Ch5 (Owing), the strongest case.** The root's first entry is quoted word for word, 14 chapters ahead of its source: "dated the forty-ninth day of the Closing … ACCOUNT ESTABLISHED. CREDITOR: CAUL … ACCOUNT HOLDER: NONE … AUTHORISED BY A KEY NOT KNOWN TO THE TALLY." That is the exact result of Dagny's quit-claim in ch19. Once ch10 reveals the Caul Deep dead, an attentive reader can predict the Closing's climactic act.
- **Ch3 (Healing), with ch2 (Owing) in support.** The bell left Sabra through the Throat "before the end" and surfaced at Tannet thirty years later. This settles in advance that the bell escapes, before the Closing even conceives the plan (ch13) or carries it out (ch19). Ch2's empty Hook and "they took … the bell" point the same way, though they wrongly blame the Company.
- **Ch12 (Healing).** Fen identifies the hurried band as all 904 Caul miners, alphabetical and complete, before the Closing era starts cutting them (ch13) or finishes (ch16). The same chapter shows that "the Tally changed its keys" on day 49, again pre-announcing a successful act at the root.
- **Ch11 (Owing), mild.** The Hulk is stencilled "HA 7731", a Company hull with a reactor, although ch1 says the *Mercy* was cut up. This hints that a Company ship never left. It does not name the *Covenant*, so the return in ch19 survives.

The genuine twists stay hidden: Kas's betrayal and his redemption, the shear, Basu's death, the *Covenant* coming home and Dagny's death. The reverse problem (an earlier era pre-empting a later one) costs suspense rather than breaking the brief; see section 4.

### 4. Coherence and continuity: **8/10**
The plot logic is tight and the dates check out: 3089 − 2387 = 702 years, and 311 AC = 2698 CE. The Tally's rule that every account must have a creditor, together with the quit-claim, is a clever and consistent piece of engineering that pays off across all three eras.

Weaknesses:
- **Repeated reveals.** Because the Closing reveals each secret first, several later scenes re-reveal what the reader already knows. In ch12, Fen's deductions (no closing phase, 904 missing miners) come two chapters after ch10 showed both. In ch20, the *Covenant*'s nameplate and the Founders' log arrive one chapter after ch19 dramatised the return. In ch21, Senna Hask explains it all a third time.
- **Fuel.** "Fourteen cassettes for fourteen years" becomes seven hundred years of light through a one-paragraph retrofit in ch21 (Lantern learned to skim fuel from Nhai's clouds).
- **Minor points.** It is lucky that Kas's tug crossed a collapsing throat and came out at Tannet. Kell's last-minute change of heart in ch21 is convenient.

There are no outright contradictions.

### 5. Craft: **9/10**
The prose is lucid, concrete and controlled throughout, and the set pieces are the best in either book:
- the man suffocating in a lock four credits over the line (ch1);
- the Wick dimmed to twilight and Joss falling off the axis (ch7);
- the Caul miners standing in the ice, with fingernail gouges on the hatch (ch10);
- the child's tally marks in Aft Store 3 (ch8);
- Marisol Tey waking and dying (ch15);
- the lamp poured down the dark axis (ch20).

Character work is strong: Basu's confession, Varga's guilt, Kas's ambiguous betrayal (and his "RING IT" scratched in the bell), Orrin's lost faith, and Abney's "Yes. You've done it right." The Healing protagonist is passive for long stretches, and Haste and Vosk are rather standard smooth villains. Dialogue is crisp. A few tics recur, such as "four hundred and twelve settlements", "like tired men" and "the way a man walks toward a door he means to close". The ending, nine hundred and four names spoken over the bell ending in "THE TALLY IS QUIT. VALVES RELEASED TO OPEN", is moving and earned, though the last chapter leans on recap.

### 6. Overall: **8/10**
A beautifully built, emotionally powerful novel. It meets the brief well, but some of its later-era scenes feel like re-enactments of reveals the reader has already had.

## The second book (T)

### 1. Brief compliance: interlacing and three distinct eras and casts: **9/10**
The chapters rotate strictly, Crossing, Account, Salvage, seven times over, with three wholly different casts. The eras are more sharply told apart than in the first book because each has its own narrative mode: close third person in the past tense for Annick, a first-person confession for Hollis, and the present tense for Yara.

The anchors are ingenious and do mechanical work in the plot:
- key 0447 (ch1, ch2, ch17, ch20, ch21);
- the shelf with VOYAGE I, II and IV and a gap where III should be (ch2, ch11, ch18), against the Ash Case stamped "VOY. III" (ch21);
- Ballast 4, known in turn as the Fourth Cistern and the Widow (ch3, ch11, ch19, ch20, ch21);
- the coil song, "when the Book goes down the sea";
- the seal-reader and the bell.

The same physical ship serves as transport, then station, then wreck, so the setting itself ties the eras together.

### 2. Brief compliance: synchronized pacing: **9/10**
Each era runs on an explicit countdown: days to the Fork, days to the Burning (and to Wren's birthday), and passes until the Hulk breaks up. That makes the parallel build easy to feel. The beats line up closely:
- **Pressure (ch1–3):** a courier, a murder and a writ.
- **First betrayals and arrests (ch7–9):** the weapons cache found, the sale discovered, the first satisfaction.
- **Mid-book (ch10–12):** Marit spaced, the forged founding page and Hollis's flight, and Kade and Sabeth overheard.
- **Peak violence (ch15–17):** Mitya's death, the Fork rising, the Burning Day riot.
- **Climaxes (ch19, ch20, ch21):** they fall back to back, and each hands the next its means: the Book dropped and the key passed on, then the Book recovered and the entry sealed, then the Book read aloud over the bell.

### 3. Brief compliance: no spoilers: **5/10**
T's later eras run largely on legends about the earlier ones, and those legends give away outcomes early, more broadly than in the first book:
- **Ch2 (Account), the strongest case.** The Catechism, "The Purser would have sold us … Her name was Annick Vell … They burned the Book … We owe our Liberty", reveals all of the following before ch4 even makes Annick purser:
  - that Annick will succeed Benn;
  - that the ship goes to Absalom and not to Shale, which is the Crossing's central question;
  - that "Founders" emerge from the rising;
  - that the Book will be publicly "burned";
  - that a Liberty debt follows (Quarry's own coinage, not proposed until ch16);
  - that Annick will be cast as the traitor.
- **Ch2, ch5 and ch11 (Account).** The shelf holds Voyages I, II and IV, with III "lost before Landfall". This plainly signals the substitution Annick carries out in ch19.
- **Ch11 (Account).** Thorne proves the founding page was composed nine years after Landfall, in Tamar water, with no purser's voice. This shows before ch16 that the Founders' Account is not a purser's copy.
- **Ch3 and ch6 (Salvage).** The Houses are gone, there was a "Night of the Hulk", and "Fen the Turncoat … stole something … never found … Fen's in there somewhere, counting". This gives away the fall of the Houses, Hollis's later reputation and his disappearance inside the Hulk well before the Account era resolves.
- **Ch18 (Salvage), two chapters before the Account's climax.** Yara finds a white-haired man in the Chief Assessor's brass chain tethered by his belt to the cradle pedestal, the purser's safe open, and the Account torn away. Since ch17 has just sent Marsh to the vault with the key, this gives away where and how Marsh ends up. Yara's mistaking him for Fen saves only the surprise of Fen in the well.

The deepest secrets do stay hidden: Szell murdering Benn, Annick giving up Marit, the Book in the ballast, and Hollis in the well.

### 4. Coherence and continuity: **8/10**
The rules of the world are laid down early and kept: the Ordinances' articles 1, 4, 9, 11, 14 and 20, lattice growth layers, the purser's voice, the key and Book together, and the old band. The payoffs are exact. Ch18's misidentified body is later corrected in ch21. The date arithmetic holds: 929 − 611 = 318 Burnings, and 1317 − 929 = 388 years of interest.

Loose ends and slips:
- **The missing Account.** Who took the Account cube out of the vault between ch20 and ch18's "torn loose" bands is never explained.
- **The recovery port.** Described as "a metre across" in ch19 and ch20, it becomes "a metre of Charter iron" when it jams in ch20.
- **Hollis's narration.** His earlier hindsight phrasing ("I've had a long time since") is a stretch once ch20 reveals he is speaking from the well within the hour. The book acknowledges this, but some readers will feel it as a cheat.
- **Plot conveniences.** The lights going out, the frigate firing on the station, and the drone exchange in ch21 are all convenient.

### 5. Craft: **9/10**
The three voices are a real achievement, and Hollis's confessional first person, ending on "Four in. / Hold.", is the boldest single stroke in either book. The moral complexity is exceptional:
- Annick trading Marit Osei for her sister, and her voice shaking only on "granddaughter" (ch10);
- Tobiah's silence outside the day room (ch16);
- Hollis's 309 strikes "written against me" (ch20);
- Yara leaving Mitya with six minutes of air (ch15).

The antagonists vary in kind. Marsh's speech "every ledger is a forgery … who holds the pen" (ch17) is the thematic spine. The ending lands three times: "Who were asleep" answered by "Who were awake", the Book left to burn up with the Hulk so that nobody holds it, and the Ash Case's "VOY. III" coda ("It's still ash." "It is now."). The weaknesses are stylistic: the counting motif is shared by every protagonist, and a few refrains are overworked ("pricing eyes", "dark considering eyes", "planed-smooth timetable voice", "it's a very hard thing to be asked").

### 6. Overall: **8/10**
A more ambitious and more suspenseful book whose ingenious machinery pays off precisely. Its framing legends give away earlier-era outcomes much too early for this brief.

## Verdict

**The second book (T) is better, by a slight margin.**

The three most important reasons:

1. **Every era keeps its own suspense.** Each of T's eras has an independent mystery: what Annick will seal and who killed Benn; who killed Ottilie and whether the Account is forged; what Fen's cache is. In each case the reader learns the answer with the protagonist. In the first book the Closing era solves nearly every mystery first, so the Owing and Healing eras spend important scenes rediscovering what the reader already knows (ch12, ch20, ch21). Those scenes have dramatic irony, but they lack tension.

2. **T has the greater range of craft.** Its three distinct narrative modes, its morally compromised protagonists (Annick and Marit, Hollis's strikes, Yara and Mitya), and its triple ending ("asleep" and "awake", the Book given to no one, "VOY. III") give it the more memorable and more daring execution. The first book matches it in prose quality and outdoes it in individual set pieces, notably the Caul ice and the Wick lit end to end.

3. **The first book is the more faithful to the brief, but not by enough to reverse the result.** Both books plant earlier-era secrets inside later-era legends. T's plants are broader and earlier: ch2's Catechism gives away the Crossing's destination and Annick's fate, and ch18's vault comes two chapters before the Account's climax. The first book's worst case (the root entry quoted in ch5) is more cryptic until ch10. That is a real point in the first book's favour, and it is why the preference is only slight.
