# Judgement: the first book (P) and the second book (T)

I read both books in full, every chapter, the first book first. They are almost the same length (P about 97,500 words, T about 99,100), so length decides nothing here.

The two books are built on a strikingly similar frame:
- Three eras in a strict 1-2-3 rotation, seven chapters each.
- A founding myth in the middle era that the first era turns out to contradict.
- A legend in the last era about the middle era's protagonist, which proves false.
- An object hidden by the first era, found again by the second and sent back.
- A last era that recovers the object, has it read out or broadcast, and disposes of it.
- Climaxes in chapters 16, 17 and 18, and resolutions in chapters 19, 20 and 21.

Because the frames match, the comparison comes down to how well each book carries the frame out.

---

## The first book (P): *The Bonded 2391 / The Siege 2654 / The Last Return 2917*

### 1. Brief compliance: interlacing, three distinct eras and casts — **10/10**
The rotation is strict and the three casts never overlap:
- **Era 1:** Maren, Abel, Hale, Quist and Ekene aboard the skimmer *Patience*.
- **Era 2:** Isaura, Malachy, Mattias, Lucan and Corin in the besieged city of Patience under Kettle's ice.
- **Era 3:** Til, Juno, Oz, Hollis Vey and Marchetti aboard the salvage tug *Dray*.

Each era has its own voice. Era 1 is close third person in the past tense, era 2 is first person in the present tense, and era 3 is close third person in the present tense.

The anchors are excellent and used sparingly:
- The *Patience* becomes the city's Spine, and its drum hall becomes the nave (ch 2).
- Names chalked on the drum, with Teska's comet drawing (ch 1, ch 2).
- The Share Plate (ch 13, ch 2, ch 19, ch 20, ch 21).
- The line knife and the tether-chief's coat stencilled TSOLO.
- The scoop-tug *Gull* (ch 16, ch 17, ch 18).
- The saying "the Lamp takes the dead out and brings them back", found on the wall of a fuel depot (ch 9) and in the Roundsmen's chapels.

### 2. Brief compliance: synchronized pacing — **9/10**
The Lamp comet is a single physical clock for all three eras. In every era the comet's approach drives the plot, and every climax happens at the comet's closest pass:
- Ch 16: the throw.
- Ch 17: Isaura on the comet's black ridge.
- Ch 18: the comet breaking up at Gall's Roche limit.

The structural beats line up across eras:
- **The weapon discovered:** ch 7, ch 11 and ch 12.
- **A betrayal from within:** Hale, Corin and Marchetti.
- **Someone left on a line:** Abel, Corin and Oz.

The resolutions follow in ch 19, 20 and 21. One small slip: era 1's climax and resolution land two chapters before era 3's. That is inherent to the rotation and is handled well.

### 3. Brief compliance: no spoilers — **7/10**
Most secrets are withheld well. The book holds back what Asset Seven is, who cut Lusine's clip, who actually rides the Gull, and what the Plate says until each era reaches them. A few premature reveals remain:
- **Ch 2 (era 2):** The Spine is the old *Patience*, and the "founders bought their air" in 2391 with the "Company's share struck off". This tells the reader early that era 1's bonded end up holding the ship and founding a free city. It is told as a myth that is later subverted, but the general outcome is given away. The scraped "Blank Share" at the top of the Plate also lets an attentive reader guess, from ch 13 on, that Maren's name will be struck off.
- **Ch 14 (era 2):** Dasha's legend of the "Tether-Woman", who went out on the Lamp with the debt, comes before ch 16. It leans toward misdirection, since Abel goes in Maren's place, but it signals that someone in the tether-chief's coat rides the comet.
- **Ch 17 (era 2):** This is the clearest premature reveal. Corin says the Strands descend from "Corwin Hale, First Shareholder, a Purchaser of the *Patience*". That tells the reader Hale wins and founds the ruling dynasty two chapters before era 1 shows it in ch 19.
- **Ch 6, 9 and 12 (era 3):** A radar sweep and a lamp lit on Kettle's night side hint that people survive on Kettle long before era 2 resolves the siege. The debris where the Throat's anchor station should be, and the lane "dark for two hundred years", hint at Grieve's destruction of the anchor (ch 20). These hints are ambiguous and easy to miss.
- **Ch 18 (era 3):** The *Gull* is found knocked onto its side with a second anchor beside the shaft. This is the aftermath of the bombardment in ch 20, shown two chapters early, but few readers would notice.

### 4. Coherence and continuity — **9/10**
The plot's logic is tight and the dates are tracked carefully:
- The register entry "Seized by bonded mutineers 17.xi.2391" matches the seizure date implied in ch 13.
- The 263-year intervals hold.
- Maren's slipped awl on the year (ch 19) matches Malachy's "somebody's awl slipped" (ch 8).
- The 40 K wake threshold (ch 10) carries through the probe readings in ch 21.
- The knife and the two recorders pass correctly from ch 20 to ch 21.

The chain of mechanisms holds:
- The egg cannot be destroyed except in the deep of a giant (ch 10).
- So the bonded send it out on the comet (ch 13 and ch 16).
- The city sends it round again (ch 20).
- The comet finally breaks up at Roche, and its north lobe drops the egg into the giant (ch 21).

Minor issues:
- Tamar is ambiguous: a child in ch 4, apparently a wife in ch 10.
- A few small calendar slips in ch 8 ("eight days into July", "twenty-eight days into the siege").
- In ch 20 Isaura says Corin shot at the cryostat "yesterday" and also "an hour" before.
- The ch 21 radio call from Ama Ocampo, in which the city has watched the *Dray* all along, is convenient, but it was seeded earlier.

### 5. Craft — **9/10**
The prose is concrete, physical and assured. The best set pieces are among the strongest in either book:
- The line walking and Lusine falling into Gall (ch 1).
- The cold-hold standoff and Quist's "Take it. Quickly. Before I find out." (ch 10).
- The sling and Abel's last broadcast (ch 16).
- Corin drifting off the comet saying "Dad" (ch 17).
- Isaura turning every valve on the tier to the stop (ch 20).

The characters are vivid and morally textured: Quist the reluctant factor, Hale the patient informer, Abel's guilt turning into sacrifice, and Isaura's "Nobody dies on the floor" turning to "Breathe".

There are faults:
- Repeated tics: "like a dropped tray", and saying a thing "the way you'd say a thing about the weather".
- The last era recaps too much. Abel's recording is summarised in ch 17 and again in ch 21. Til's Harrow confession is given almost word for word in ch 15 and again in ch 21. Isaura's story is recapped in ch 21.
- Era 3 is the weakest strand. Szabo's harpoon gambit and Marchetti as a secret Directorate major are more pulp than the rest.

The ending is moving and earned: the egg and the dead go down into Gall, and then the lights come on along Kettle with "There's air."

### 6. Overall — **9/10**
A strong, emotionally powerful novel, and the more careful of the two about the brief's hard constraint. The comet is an organic, physical way to synchronize the three arcs.

## The second book (T): *The Crossing CR 611 / The Account CR 929 / The Salvage CR 1317*

### 1. Brief compliance: interlacing, three distinct eras and casts — **10/10**
The rotation is strict and the three casts are distinct:
- **Era 1:** Annick, Dace, Benn, Tobiah, Szell and Quarry aboard the colony transport *Merciful Increase*.
- **Era 2:** Hollis Fen, Marsh, Ottilie, Thorne, Calla and Wren on Increase Station.
- **Era 3:** Yara, Mitya, Pim, Kade, Rhee, Quell and Dunn in the Gutter and the drowned Hulk.

The voices are again differentiated: era 1 is close third person in the past tense, era 2 is first person in the past tense, and era 3 is close third person in the present tense.

The anchors are superb, arguably richer than P's:
- Key 0447.
- The VOYAGE III box missing from the shelf (ch 5, ch 11, ch 18).
- The lettering "WHAT IS WRITTEN IS OWED", which decays to "WHAT IS WRIT — — — OWED".
- Ballast Tank 4, which becomes the Fourth Cistern and then the Widow, with its knocking (ch 3, ch 11, ch 13).
- The coil-gang song, whose words mutate across the centuries.
- The Old Hold's cradles.
- Haskell's index card turning up in era 3 (ch 6).

### 2. Brief compliance: synchronized pacing — **9/10**
Each era has its own countdown:
- Era 1: the days to the Fork.
- Era 2: the days to Burning Day.
- Era 3: the passes left before the Hulk breaks up.

The three are paced in lockstep. Each era also has the same set of beats:
- A sale of debts: Shale, Ostrava and Paschal.
- A reasonable villain: Quarry, Marsh and Kade.
- A betrayal by an intimate: Szell or Tobiah, Bastian, and Mitya.
- A clerk whose rule is "paper is paper": Thorne and Rhee.

The climaxes come in ch 16, 17 and 18. The resolutions form a superb chain of three readings: Annick's to people who "were asleep" (ch 19), Fen's to no one (ch 20), and Yara's to people "who were awake" (ch 21). The parallelism sometimes feels schematic, which is the only reason this is not a 10.

### 3. Brief compliance: no spoilers — **5/10**
T uses the later eras' myths as deliberate dramatic irony. In doing so it states several endings very early:
- **Ch 2 (era 2):** The Catechism says "The Purser would have sold us… Her name was Annick Vell, and it is written in ash… They burned the Book… We owe our Liberty." In the second chapter this gives away the outcome of era 1:
  - the ship reaches Absalom;
  - the mutineers ("Founders") take power;
  - a book is burned;
  - Annick is condemned as a traitor;
  - the Liberty debt follows.

  The truth behind it is withheld until ch 19, but the ending is not.
- **Ch 3 (era 3):** "Fen's dead… Fen's in there somewhere, counting", along with "Fen the Turncoat on the Night of the Hulk" and the Houses' fall. One chapter after Fen is introduced, this reveals that the era 2 narrator dies inside the Hulk and that his era ends in a cataclysm. **Ch 6** repeats it: "Then came the Night of the Hulk, and then the Dimming, and then they were gone", plus the "Fen was an Assessor" skipping song. **Ch 9:** Dunn says nobody has paid on the Account "since the Night of the Hulk", which confirms era 2's outcome long before ch 17 and ch 20.
- **Ch 5 (era 2):** The empty slot where VOYAGE III should be telegraphs Annick's swap of the books well before ch 19. The same goes for the Widow's knocking in ch 3, ch 11 and ch 13. This is clever foreshadowing, but it gives the secret away.
- **Ch 18 (era 3):** Yara finds a white-haired man in a grey coat wearing the Office chain, tied by his belt to the cradle's pedestal. The safe is open and the Account has been torn off its bands. Ch 17 ended with Marsh walking to the vault with the key, so a reader recognises Marsh's chain and learns he dies there. This comes before era 2 stages it in ch 20.

### 4. Coherence and continuity — **8/10**
The legal and plot mechanics are intricate and mostly hold:
- The dates add up: 318 Burnings, 388 years, "seven hundred and six years ago".
- The key's journey works: Annick to Dace, to the Gallows Reach line, to Fen, to Marsh, back to Fen, to Yara, to Pim.
- The privy box holds the brandy, a third of it drunk, and the "ART. 9 — A.V." lead plate (ch 19 and ch 20).
- The recovery-port lid is jammed, consistently, in ch 20 and ch 21.
- Kade's "certified contrary record" is answered by Article 11 and Article 20.

Problems:
- **Fen's retrospective narration.** Era 2 is told as a long retrospective ("I've had a long time to think…"), and ch 20 reveals he is narrating from the well over roughly an hour as he bleeds to death. The twist is effective, but it strains plausibility across seven chapters, and the narration has no medium by which it could be heard.
- **Convenient devices.** The Charter-band seal-reader that talks to the church's bell array, Quell's lead, and the Clerk's bead that "hears the old band" (ch 18 and ch 21) all arrive exactly when needed.
- **Loose threads.** Who took the Account itself from the vault after ch 20 is never said. Why the Fourth Cistern was condemned in CR 718 is left vague.
- **Quarry.** He visibly notices the drop hatch and does nothing (ch 19). This is plausible but thin.

### 5. Craft — **9/10**
The prose is excellent and the moral texture may be the deepest in either book:
- Annick weighing Marit against her sister and giving the name. The spacing of Marit, "Write it truly, Purser", is the strongest scene in T (ch 10).
- Tobiah's silent complicity at the Fork (ch 16).
- Fen's 309 strikes "written against me".
- Thorne's bloodless integrity.
- Mitya's death under the beam (ch 15).

The ending triad is brilliantly built, and the coda pays it off: the VOY. III plate on the Ash Case, "It's still ash… It is now", and "Don't sell it."

There are faults:
- Recurring tics: "dark considering eyes", "pricing eyes", "as if a reading had come back as she expected" used three times, "I want you to know that", "the sound the Hulk makes".
- The three eras' parallel beats can feel mechanical.
- Era 3 relies on devices rather than character for its key turns.

### 6. Overall — **8/10**
A beautifully constructed and thematically rich novel with the more ingenious ending. It weakens its own suspense by stating the earlier eras' endings, as myth, from chapters 2 and 3 onward.

## Verdict

**The first book (P) is better overall. The preference is slight.**

Three most important reasons:
1. **P keeps the brief's hardest constraint better.** Both books open era 2 with a founding myth, but T's version names the era 1 protagonist as the condemned traitor and announces that the Book is burned (ch 2). T's era 3 then repeatedly announces Fen's death and the fall of the Houses (ch 3, 6 and 9), and shows Marsh's death in the vault before era 2 reaches it (ch 18). P's premature reveals are fewer and vaguer: the Purchase myth, Hale's lineage in ch 17, and ambiguous lights on Kettle.
2. **P's synchronizing device is more organic.** In P a single physical comet sets every era's clock, and every climax happens at its closest pass, so the shared pacing feels inevitable. T gets its synchronization from three separate countdowns and deliberately parallel plot beats, which sometimes feel schematic.
3. **P's logic is cleaner. T has the better ending.** T has the more ingenious ending and arguably the deeper moral drama. But it pays for them with strained devices: Fen narrating from the well, the bell-array seal-reader, the Clerk's listening bead. P's plot rests on fewer contrivances, and its emotional peaks are as strong (Abel's throw, Isaura's valves, "There's air"). Its main weakness is repetitive recap in the final era.
