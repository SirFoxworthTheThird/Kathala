# Judgement: the first book (R) vs the second book (T)

Both books were read in full, all 21 chapters each, the first book (R) first. They are close in length (about 98k and 99k words), so length plays no part in the verdict.

**Short version:** both are accomplished, fully realised novels with excellent prose. They differ mainly on the brief's "hard part". The first book keeps each era's secrets until that era reaches them. The second book deliberately lets its later eras carry legends about the earlier ones. Many of those legends turn out false, but several true outcomes leak chapters early.

---

## The first book (R)

Eras: **The Bond · 2431** (Maren Tull, close third, past tense), **The Ledger · 2794** (Ysolde Marr, close third), **The Door · 3190** (Absalom Rook, first-person confession). Strict rotation Bond / Ledger / Door, seven times.

### 1. Interlacing, three distinct eras and casts: **10/10**
The rotation never breaks (Ch1 Bond, Ch2 Ledger, Ch3 Door … Ch19 Bond, Ch20 Ledger, Ch21 Door). The eras are about four centuries apart, and their casts never overlap. The anchors are subtle and work hard:
- the broken surety disc, which becomes the divers' half-moon tags (Ch1, Ch2, Ch3, Ch11) and is joined at last in Ch20;
- the motto "Count what's owed / and not a mark more";
- the breathers' hymn, sung with its third line wrong;
- Hesper High, which becomes the Nave and then the Hulk;
- the Count Board, intact in Ch2 and fallen in Ch9;
- Gallows and its "Hanged Men";
- the three knocks.

All of it builds one overarching story: a debt is invented, kept, and finally receipted.

### 2. Synchronized pacing: **9/10**
The three arcs move in lockstep.
- **Inciting incidents (Ch1–3):** the Scholarship note "Ask the price of 0212"; Anselm's hidden leaf reading "Zero. Year 280"; Wren and the herald.
- **First escalation (Ch4–6):** Schedule C; the forged iron-gall pages; the Notice of Recovery.
- **Commitments (Ch7–9):** the union vote; Quist's dinner and Ysolde's first falsified hours; the raid on Kell Deep.
- **Midpoint revelations (Ch10–12):** the inversion plan; the First Book and the Compact; the receivers come through.
- **Darkest turns (Ch13–15):** the failed farewell; Piet's audit; the Kask/Jory revelation.
- **Runs to Gallows (Ch16–18):** a run to Gallows in every era.

The climaxes then fall in three consecutive chapters (19, 20, 21), in the same room at Gallows. Each climax is the precondition of the next: Maren's recording leads to Ysolde's audit and seal, and Ysolde's letter becomes the receipt Rook transmits. That is an exceptionally clean solution to "climax together". One small cost: the Door era's arc (Ch18 → Ch21) has one chapter of relative calm (the run out) where the other two are at full tension.

### 3. No spoilers: **8/10**
For the most part this works as dramatic irony that does not give the game away. The Ledger era's shut Door does not spoil the Bond era, because closing the Throat behind them is the Combine's own plan (Ch10). Knowing the Door is shut tells the reader nothing about who shut it, or which side the children ended up on. The decisive facts are withheld until their own chapters: Maren closes the Throat *in front of* the Grace (Ch19), the Grace becomes the Nave and its officers the Stewards (Ch20), the First Auditor is Pell (Ch20). Cases where a later era leaks ahead:
- **Ch8 (Ledger), before the Bond farewell (Ch13) and Gallows (Ch19):** Quist's story of a child "young when the Door was shut," raised in the Stewards' care, who writes the first Count. An alert reader can guess the wards never left Vigil. This is a soft leak.
- **Ch11 (Ledger), before Ch13/Ch19:** the Reliquary is plainly a ship's hold, with strapped seats and ceiling hand-grips. The First Book has a page headed WARDS, and the Compact says the Door is "closed … against *them*" and "What was done at Gallows is accounted and closed." Together these point toward the Bond climax. The cues are subtle but substantial.
- **Ch17 (Ledger), before Ch19:** Ysolde finds Gallows dark, frosted, with "an old wound" of torn docking ring. This foreshadows the breach at ring two. It is minor.
- **Ch9/Ch12/Ch15 (Door):** the dead Hulk with its fallen Count Board, and a Steward's bones in a niche. This shows the Ledger civilisation eventually collapses, but it says nothing about Ysolde's own arc or its outcome. Minor.

### 4. Coherence and continuity: **8/10**
The plot logic is unusually well planted. The Door era's resolution depends on a clause in the Ch6 Notice ("statements of payment, under the seal of an authorised officer … for reconciliation"). It also depends on the Prelate's HV ring, mentioned in Ch8 and revealed as Castellane's Factor seal in Ch20. The 380–420-year restoration fits the dates, and the Door begins "breathing" about 30 years before 3190. Contradictions noticed:
- **Ch1:** "eleven pictures … one a year since she was taken" and "a hundred and thirty-two" visits, i.e. 11 years. But Pell was taken at six and is now twelve, Maren was 32 then and is 38 now (Ch4), and Ch13 says "six years." This is a clear contradiction.
- **Ch12:** "thirty-four people breathing" does not match the head-count Ch9 establishes.
- **Ch20:** Quist reacts to a line "near the end": "I hope it's you, Pell". The Ch19 recording has "I hope it's you" near the start and never with the name.
- The Grace carried the whole staff plus some 340 marines, yet "twelve Stewards" is all that survives in the Ledger era. What happened to everyone else is never addressed.
- The receiver handing over title to itself (Ch21) is planted, but it is still a little convenient.

### 5. Craft: **9/10**
The prose is distinguished throughout. The diving set-pieces are memorable: the downdraft and half-dump in Ch1, the slingshot down the limb in Ch19. So are the Count Board's "rain on a roof" and the Old Chimney climb (Ch9). The three voices are distinct. Rook's confessional first person ("I'll tell you the truth … even where it costs me") is outstanding, and his withheld sin (swapping Jory's name for Wren's) lands hard in Ch15 and Ch18. The secondary characters are rich and morally textured: Dov's betrayal and redemption (Ch16, Ch19), Quist the kindly tyrant, Strand, Corin, Haddie, Lucan, Castellane. The dialogue is spare and pointed. The ending is moving and earned: Maren's three knocks, the two halves joined on 0212, and "Paid." Rook's closing knock "Still here" ties all three eras together. Weaknesses: verbal tics repeat ("I'll not tell you", "flat and loud and terrible", "white, not pale" used of both Lucan and Quist), the motto is sometimes overworked, and Kask's last scene tips toward stock villainy.

### 6. Overall: **9/10**
This is a superbly engineered three-era novel whose structure is its theme: one debt, kept and then receipted, across three eras. It is nearly flawless on the brief, with only soft leaks.

## The second book (T)

Eras: **The Crossing · CR 611** (Annick Vell, close third, past tense), **The Account · CR 929** (Hollis Fen, first person, revealed in Ch20 to be told from the well), **The Salvage · CR 1317** (Yara Tull, present tense). Strict rotation Crossing / Account / Salvage, seven times.

### 1. Interlacing, three distinct eras and casts: **10/10**
The rotation is perfect, the eras are about 300–390 years apart, and the casts are wholly separate. The anchors are excellent:
- key 0447 (Ch1, Ch2, Ch17, Ch19–21);
- Ballast Tank Four, which becomes the "Fourth Cistern" and then "the Widow";
- "WHAT IS WRITTEN IS OWED", shown flaking away in Ch9 and Ch15;
- the coil song, the Ash Case, and the gap at VOYAGE III;
- the brass plaque and the knocking in the tank;
- the Hold of cradles, later the memorial and then the drowned ruin.

The three distinct narrative modes (past tense, retrospective first person, present tense) add real texture.

### 2. Synchronized pacing: **8/10**
This is also very good.
- **Inciting incidents (Ch1–3):** the courier; Ottilie's murder; the writ.
- **Rising pressure (Ch4–9):** Benn's death and the Shale order; the dead accounts and the Sale; the bailiffs and first satisfaction.
- **Reversals (Ch10–12):** Marit's betrayal; the founding-page reading and Hollis's flight; Mitya's sale.
- **Climaxes:** the climaxes are again staged in sequence (Ch19, Ch20, Ch21), each enabling the next: Annick's sealed discharge, then Hollis's recovery of the Book and his own entry, then Yara's broadcast under Article Eleven.

It is slightly less lockstep than the first book. The Crossing's action peak, the Fork rising (Ch16), comes three chapters before its true climax. The Account's action climax (the Hall riot, Ch17) is split from its resolution (Ch20). The Salvage reaches its vault in Ch18, two chapters before its climax. The effect is three peaks spaced across Ch16–21 rather than a single synchronised crest.

### 3. No spoilers: **6/10**
The book is built on purpose so that later eras carry myths about earlier ones (Annick as the traitor, Fen the Turncoat, the burned Book), and the past then overturns them. Several of the deepest secrets are well kept: Annick's discharge and the Book sent "down the sea" (Ch19), and Fen's lodging and death with the Book (Ch20). But a number of *true* outcomes are stated chapters before their eras reach them.
- **Ch2 (Account), the second chapter of the book:** the Catechism says "The Increase carried us … The Purser would have sold us … Her name was Annick Vell … The Founders saved us … They burned the Book." Before Shale is even introduced (Ch4), the reader knows the ship reaches Absalom/Tamar, a "Founders" takeover succeeds, a Book is burned, and Annick is the one blamed. That answers the Crossing's central question, Shale or Absalom, twelve chapters early. This is the most serious case.
- **Ch3 and Ch6 (Salvage), at the very start of the Account arc:** the "Night of the Hulk", the Houses "gone" afterwards, the Concordance's arrival, and "Fen the Turncoat" who "went out on the tide when the Concordance came." The Account's last entry is dated 388 years before 1317, i.e. the Burning of 929, so the Account outlives its own era as a copy. Together these sketch the Account era's ending, much of it accurately, before Hollis's story has got going.
- **Ch5 (Account):** VOYAGE III "lost … Before Landfall." This hints at the swap Annick makes in Ch19. It is mild, a clue rather than an answer.
- **Ch11 (Account), before Ch16/Ch19:** Thorne establishes that the founding page was composed nine years after Landfall and that no purser ever sealed it. This gives away part of what happens at Landfall in the Crossing before the Crossing gets there.
- **Ch12 (Salvage), before Ch17/Ch20:** Kade's statement that on the Night of the Hulk Fen "stole something … that would have destroyed the Account" and that it never came out. This tells the reader that Hollis gets hold of the proof and that it does not surface in his time.
- **Ch18 (Salvage), before Ch20:** Yara finds the vault: the Account torn away, the purser's safe open and empty, and a white-haired man in grey tethered by his belt to the cradle, wearing the Chief's brass chain with its scales medallion (established for Marsh in Ch2). Ch17 has just shown Marsh leaving the dais for the vault with the key in his pocket. The reader therefore learns two chapters early that Marsh opens the safe and dies in the vault. Yara's mistaking him for Fen softens this, but it is still a substantive leak of the Account climax.

### 4. Coherence and continuity: **8/10**
The plotting is intricate and pays off strongly.
- Article Eleven is set up in Ch1 and pays off in Ch19 and Ch21.
- The seal-reader's clicks at the bell (Ch3, Ch6) set up the Ch21 broadcast.
- Benn's brandy is planted in the privy box in Ch1 and turns up in Ch19 and Ch20.
- "Witnesses who were asleep" (Ch19) is answered by "Who were awake" (Ch21).

The legal logic is consistent and fair throughout. Problems noticed:
- **Ch20:** the Account-era narration turns out to be told from the well in real time. Earlier lines such as "I've had a long time since to think" (Ch8) and "Calla told me afterward" (Ch20) are explained away, but the conceit is strained.
- The Account cube's later removal from the vault and the re-closing of a vault door last seen wedged open (Ch20 → Ch9/Ch15) are never accounted for.
- Some plot points are convenient: the Clerk accepting a radio reading as "before witnesses", Kade releasing Pim on trust before receiving the box (Ch21), and Yara cutting a buckled frame alone in forty minutes.
- There are small numerical slips: the Voyage III book is "twenty years" old in one place and "twenty-three years" in another (Ch19).
- Sabeth Orrin, built up as the client, drops out of the final act.

### 5. Craft: **9/10**
The prose is excellent and textured: the drowned Index "snowfall" of cards (Ch6), the burning banners (Ch9), the Crown breaking up at perigee (Ch12), the Book's trembling page-glass. The characters are morally complex. Annick's giving up Marit to save Dace (Ch10) is devastating. Tobiah's silent complicity, Szell, Quarry ("Call it something they can love … Liberty"), Marsh's "every ledger is a forgery", Thorne, Calla, Mitya and Pim are all memorable. The dialogue is precise and often witty. The ending is quietly magnificent: "Who were awake", the VOY. III plate under the Ash Case ("It's true now. It just took longer"), and "Don't sell it". Thematically it is perhaps even richer than the first book's: each era ends with someone refusing to hold the pen. Weaknesses: deliberate refrains are sometimes overused ("as if a reading had come back as expected", "It's a very hard thing to be asked", "Fen's in there somewhere, counting"), and the first-person framing device is a little contrived.

### 6. Overall: **8/10**
This is a beautifully written, morally serious novel with a superb ending and a clever myth-against-truth design. That same design, however, repeatedly gives away its earlier eras' outcomes, against the letter of the brief.

## Verdict

**The first book (R) is better overall. Strength of preference: clear** (though not strong, because the two are close in craft).

The three most important reasons:

1. **Spoiler discipline, which the brief names as the hard part.** The first book keeps each era's decisive secret until that era delivers it. Who shut the Door, and on which side of it the children ended up, stays hidden until Ch19–20, and the later eras offer only dramatic irony and soft hints (Ch8, Ch11). The second book tells the reader in its second chapter that the Crossing ends at Tamar under a Founders' council, with a Book burned and Annick blamed. It sketches the Account era's aftermath in Ch3 and Ch6, and it shows Marsh dead in the vault (Ch18) before the Account era reaches that scene (Ch20).
2. **Tighter synchronisation of the climaxes.** All three of the first book's arcs crest in consecutive chapters in a single room, each climax causally feeding the next (recording, then audit and seal, then receipt). The second book's three peaks are staggered: the Fork in Ch16, the Hall in Ch17 and the vault in Ch18 come before the Ch19–21 resolutions.
3. **Equal craft, so structure decides.** Both books are exceptional: fine prose, morally complex casts, and moving endings that are fully earned. The second book is arguably the richer of the two thematically. With craft and coherence roughly level, the first book's cleaner fulfilment of the brief's structural demands decides it.
