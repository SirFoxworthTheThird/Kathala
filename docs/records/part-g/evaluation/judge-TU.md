# Judgement: the first book (T) vs the second book (U)

Both books were read in full, every chapter, the first book first.

- **The first book (T)** is about 99,000 words. A colony ship's purser in CR 611 ("The Crossing", Annick Vell), an Assessor of a debt office in CR 929 ("The Account", Hollis Fen) and a salvage diver in CR 1317 ("The Salvage", Yara Tull) are tied together by a debt-ledger, the Book, its key 0447, and the ship that later becomes the Hulk.
- **The second book (U)** is about 102,000 words. A bonded rigger building a transit ring in FY 2291 ("The Combine", Sol Dace), a Keeper of the lit Ring in 336 AL ("The Concord", Idris Vey) and a Tally of a dying hold in Year 419 of the Dark ("The Dark", Mira) are tied together by a work song, the Count, a steel flask (TTH-9 · 0417), bunkroom C-14, the tug *Patience* and a locking pin, the Knot.

Both are serious, accomplished novels written to the brief. The gap between them is narrow.

---

## The first book (T)

### 1. Brief compliance: interlacing, three distinct eras and casts — **10**
The chapters rotate strictly, Crossing, Account, Salvage, from Ch1 to Ch21. The eras are 318 and 388 years apart. Their casts are wholly separate, and each era also has its own narrative mode: close third person past for Annick, retrospective first person for Hollis, present tense for Yara. The anchors are handled with real subtlety:
- the key 0447 (Ch1, then Hollis's "family junk" in Ch2, then the Ch17 reveal, then Yara's "Don't sell it" in Ch21);
- the strongroom with its red letters, the Ordinances plaque and the privy box;
- the dead shelf with Voyage III missing (Ch5, Ch11, Ch18);
- Ballast Tank Four, which becomes the Fourth Cistern and then the Widow (Ch1, Ch11, Ch3, Ch21);
- the coil-gang song, which mutates from era to era;
- the Ash Case, and the seal-reader.

### 2. Brief compliance: synchronized pacing — **9**
The three arcs escalate in step, with rhyming beats:
- **Discovery** (Ch1–3): the courier, the D-0447 card, Sabeth's commission.
- **First betrayal under pressure** (Ch10–12): Annick gives up Marit; Hollis is framed by Bastian; Mitya is revealed as Sabeth's informant.
- **Crisis** (Ch16–18): the Fork rising; Burning Day; the empty vault.
- **Climaxes** (Ch19, Ch20, Ch21), one per era: the Book goes down the sea; Hollis in the well; Yara's broadcast.

The last chapter resolves all three at once, because Yara reads Annick's and Hollis's entries aloud. The Fork in Ch16 is a little early as a peak for era 1, but Ch19 is clearly its true climax.

### 3. Brief compliance: no spoilers — **7**
Most of the later eras' "knowledge" of earlier ones arrives as false legend. That protects the real secrets: Annick's discharge of the debts and the swapped book, and Hollis's lodging and his recovery of the Book. Even so, several outcomes are given away early:
- **Ch2 (era 2) → era 1.** The Catechism establishes that the *Increase* reached Absalom and Tamar rather than Shale, that "the Founders" burned the Book, and that "Annick Vell" is remembered as the Purser who would have sold them. Era 1's central question, Shale or Absalom, is effectively answered 14 chapters before the Fork, and so is the fact that Annick survives to Landfall and is scapegoated. The legend inverts the truth, but the outcome itself is revealed.
- **Ch5 and Ch11 (era 2) → era 1.** The founding page was composed nine years after Landfall, and Voyage III is missing from the shelf. This previews the Founders' fraud before Quarry proposes "Liberty" in Ch16, and plants the book swap. These are mostly clues rather than reveals.
- **Ch3, Ch6 and Ch9 (era 3) → era 2.** The "Night of the Hulk", "Fen the Turncoat", "Fen's cache" (he took something from the Office and it was never found), the Houses later "gone", and "nobody on Tamar has paid a mark on the Account since the Night of the Hulk". Together these reveal that era 2 ends in a rising that ends the Houses' rule, and that Fen vanishes inside the Hulk, all before Ch17 and Ch20.
- **Ch12 (era 3) → era 2.** Kade says the instrument passed by sale to a Vey trading house. This hints that a copy of the Account escapes, which is Severine's flight in Ch20.
- **Ch18 (era 3) → era 2.** This is the most concrete case. The body Yara takes for Fen, tethered in the vault beside the empty cradle and the open privy box, has white hair and the Chief Assessor's brass-link chain with the scales medallion. That is plainly Lucan Marsh. So the reader learns Marsh's death and its place, and that the privy box has been opened, two chapters before Ch20 dramatises them. It is cleverly misdirected for Yara, but not for an attentive reader.

### 4. Coherence and continuity as experienced — **8**
The plot is impressively engineered. The Ordinances recited in Ch1 (Articles 1, 9, 11, 14 and 20) each pay off in turn. The dates are consistent: 706 years from Annick's entry to Yara's, 388 from Fen's, and the Account's interest compounding "for three hundred and eighty-eight years" is correct. Hollis's retrospective narration turns out, in Ch20, to be told from the well, which retroactively explains all his "I've had a long time to think".

Small slips and strains:
- The Widow's weld is "three centuries old" in Ch3, but the cistern was closed in CR 718, about six hundred years before.
- Voyage III is "twenty-three years gone" in one line and "paid twenty years before" in another.
- Quarry spots the drop hatch in Ch19, yet nobody ever uses the recovery port in three centuries. This is explicable, since the Founders would want the Book buried, but it is unstated.
- Hollis's face is "whole" after 388 years in the ice.
- The Clerk accepts a radio broadcast as an Article Eleven reading. This is set up carefully (the bead on his chest, the seal-reader, the bell), but it still asks something of the reader.

None of these breaks the experience.

### 5. Craft: prose, character, dialogue, structure, ending — **9**
The prose is exact, weighty and controlled, and its moral complexity is the book's great strength:
- Annick trading Marit for Dace, entered in a voice that shakes only on "granddaughter" (Ch10);
- Tobiah's acquiescence: "Somebody's got to pay for the air" (Ch16);
- Marsh's "every ledger is a forgery" (Ch17);
- Thorne's "Paper is paper".

The supporting cast is distinct: Quarry, Szell, Haddad, Calla Dray, Mags Tiller, Kade, Rhee and Quell. The structural coup of Ch20 is outstanding, and so is the reversal in the closing lines: "Who were asleep" in Ch19 becomes "Who were awake" in Ch21. The ending's "VOY. III" laugh in the Ash Church, followed by "Leave it. It's true now. It just took longer", is earned.

Faults: the counting motif and certain tics ("a sound like…", "the way you'd…", "level voice") are leaned on hard. Quarry's and Kade's identical "It's a very hard thing to be asked" is deliberate but heavy.

### 6. Overall — **9**

## The second book (U)

### 1. Brief compliance: interlacing, three distinct eras and casts — **9**
The chapters rotate strictly, Combine, Concord, Dark, across all 21. The eras are roughly 336 and 419 years apart, and the casts are entirely separate. The anchors are superb and organic:
- the flask TTH-9 · 0417, passed "where it's wanted" and finally carried home;
- bunkroom C-14 with "abandon hope", and Sol's marks, which become the Tallies' "First Count";
- the *Patience* and its COMBINE plate;
- Sol's driver, which becomes the "Founders' Wrench";
- the Count itself, which drifts from rigging song to Keepers' invocation to skipping rhyme to funeral rite.

It scores slightly below the first book only because eras 1 and 2 share the same narrative mode and much the same cadence (close third person past), so the eras are told apart by setting more than by voice.

### 2. Brief compliance: synchronized pacing — **9**
The arcs are tightly parallel:
- **Opening death or loss** (Ch1–3): Kostya; the Levy; Pim.
- **Hidden truth found on paper** (Ch7–8, Ch10–11): the insurance schedule; the *Clemency*'s mass and the Instrument.
- **Betrayal and assault** (Ch13–15): Juno and the command deck; the Knot uncovered; the zealots and the empty lung.
- **Crisis** (Ch16–18): the dog; Firstlight Eve raid; the Halo walk.

The three climaxes fall in the last three chapters, one per era, and each causes the next: Sol sets the lock (Ch19), Idris turns it (Ch20), Mira counts it back down (Ch21). That chain-reaction design is the most elegant fulfilment of "climax together" in either book.

### 3. Brief compliance: no spoilers — **6**
The book relies on dramatic irony that frequently runs ahead of the earlier eras:
- **Ch2 (era 2) → era 1.** The Ring is lit and the Reach "born into the Light". Castellane, Haddad ("the Lightbringer's Hand") and Lind are gilded on the Founders' Roll. So the Lighting goes ahead, and the brass who planned the massacre survive to be venerated, before era 1 has raised the question.
- **Ch3 (era 3) → era 2.** The very first Dark chapter establishes that the Eye has gone out and Tithe has lived in darkness for 419 years. Era 2's eventual outcome, the Light ending, is known before era 2's threat (the Instrument and the Squadron) has even appeared. The Tallies' rule "Never sing the Count on the Halo", and a First Tally who "came up out of the Dark when the Dark was new, and would not give his name", foreshadow Idris's fate.
- **Ch3 and Ch8 (eras 3 and 2) → era 1.** The C-14 marks: "some of them have a line through, and some do not"; "You can see they didn't finish" (Ch8). Both telegraph that Sol never completes her bond.
- **Ch11 (era 2) → era 1.** Immediately after Sol cuts her line under Day 288 in Ch10, era 2 shows that same cut: "Teodor says it's where they stopped." In effect this announces Sol's end on Lighting day eight chapters early.
- **Ch6 and Ch9 (era 3) → eras 1 and 2.** The Reeve and Cinder link the Knot ("shall not be turned") to the Count: "your song is how it's untied". The mechanism of both earlier climaxes is visible well before Idris works it out (Ch14) and before Sol writes the locking verse (Ch19).
- **Ch12 (era 3) → era 2.** The knocking "from the other side" of the shut Eye hints that people went through, which is Lark's passage in Ch20. Mild, since it cannot be decoded until Ch21.

The real surprises, the plate's inscription and the knock as the Count sung upward, are kept. But the outcomes of eras 1 and 2 are more legible in advance than in the first book, because here the later eras inherit literal facts (darkness, the marks) rather than misleading legends.

### 4. Coherence and continuity as experienced — **8**
The continuity is often dazzling:
- Sallow's body in Ch21 wears two cords, his own and Idris's line;
- the Door verse reverses correctly between Sol's locking version, the Sump skipping drift ("old men", "kiss it goodnight") and the Tallies' unlocking version;
- Quill's "false" Door is in fact the locking order;
- the nine sheared hulls are still tumbling 419 years later;
- the plate gains three hands of writing across the three eras.

Strains:
- The robot "dogs" guarding the Spindle are a convenient obstacle with no clear origin.
- The *Patience* survives for some 750 years.
- Cinder's all-opening "Keepers' key" is a convenience.
- Lind's demand that one person stay at the Spindle "as surety" is contrived to put Sol where the plot needs her.
- Dov turns out to be Sol's brother only in Ch19. This is not a contradiction, but it is withheld oddly long.
- Bracken lifting a powered pressure hatch (Ch13) strains belief.

The plot logic holds overall.

### 5. Craft: prose, character, dialogue, structure, ending — **9**
The book is deeply moving and humane:
- Kostya's death on the open channel (Ch1);
- Dov's letter, "I would like her to get to the end of them" (Ch19);
- Lark's "Let us through. And *then* lock it" (Ch20);
- Mira's final call-and-response with a voice from the other side of the door.

The characters are vivid: Sol, Teague, Juno (an unrepentant informer), Haddad, Castellane, Idris, Lark, Sallow, Mira, Ness, Cinder and the Reeve. The engraved plate, read and added to in three eras, is the single best structural device in either book. The closing insurance claim at the end of Ch19 ("INSURED BONDED LABOUR LOST: 1") is devastating.

Weaknesses:
- Some action set pieces are more conventional: the gas assault, the zealot mutiny, the robot dogs.
- Refrains are over-used: "as though speaking to someone just beside", "a hair one way, a hair back", and "like being remembered", which is used for two different characters.
- Eras 1 and 2 sound alike.
- Sentiment occasionally tips toward the expected.

### 6. Overall — **9**

## Verdict

**The first book (T) is better overall. The preference is slight.**

The three most important reasons:

1. **It protects earlier eras' secrets better.** Later eras in the first book know earlier ones through propaganda and myth, which point the wrong way: Annick "the Purser who would have sold us", "Fen the Turncoat", "they burned the Book". The real secrets therefore land as reversals (Ch19, Ch20, Ch21). In the second book the later eras inherit literal facts: the Dark itself (from Ch3), and the marks "where they stopped" (Ch11). So Sol's and Idris's outcomes are known well in advance.
2. **Its eras are more distinct, and its form does more.** Each era has its own narrative mode, and the revelation in Ch20 that Hollis's retrospective narration comes from the dying man in the well is a formal coup that the second book's two similar third-person eras do not match.
3. **Its moral complexity runs deeper.** Annick trading Marit for her sister, Tobiah consenting to "Liberty", and Marsh's "every ledger is a forgery" make the first book's argument about debt and record-keeping richer and harder than the second book's clearer opposition of workers and Company.

In fairness to the second book: its chain of climaxes, in which each era's climax enables the next, and its three-hand inscribed plate are the most elegant answers to the brief's "climax together" in either book, and it is at least as emotionally powerful. That is why the preference is only slight.
