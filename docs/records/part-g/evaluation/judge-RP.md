# Judgement: the first book (R) vs the second book (P)

I read every chapter of both books: R first, then P. They are about the same length (roughly 98,000 words each), so length is not a factor.

**Summary of the two books**

- **R** has three eras: *The Bond* (2431), *The Ledger* (2794) and *The Door* (3190).
  - In 2431, Maren Tull, a gas-giant diver, fights a "Scholarship" that is really the sale of 412 bonded children. The Combine plans to leave and close the Throat behind it.
  - In 2794, Ysolde Marr, an auditor in a debt-religion, discovers that the sacred Count was paid off decades ago.
  - In 3190, Absalom Rook, a former child-taker, and Wren, a child he once tithed, face a "receiver" coming through the reopening Door to collect the debt.
  - All three arcs converge on the Gallows station that controls the Door.
- **P** has three eras: *The Bonded* (2391), *The Siege* (2654) and *The Last Return* (2917).
  - In 2391, Maren Tsolo, a tether-chief, leads bonded workers who seize their ship and a Combine nerve agent (the "Hush"), then put the agent on a periodic comet, the Lamp.
  - In 2654, Isaura Venn, an air-warden in the city built on that founding, is sent to recover the agent during a siege.
  - In 2917, Til Brask, a salvage captain, is hired to retrieve the agent before the comet falls into the gas giant.
  - Each era's climax happens at a pass of the comet, 263 years apart.

## The first book (R)

### 1. Interlacing; three distinct eras and casts: **10/10**
- The rotation is strict (Bond, Ledger, Door, repeated across 21 chapters). The three casts never overlap.
- Each era has its own register: close third-person past for Maren and for Ysolde, and a first-person confessional for Rook ("I'll tell you the truth, because I've decided that in this account I am going to tell the truth even where it costs me", ch. 3).
- The anchors are excellent and genuinely subtle:
  - The broken steel surety discs become the divers' heirloom "tags" (ch. 2, 5) and then the First Auditor's relic (ch. 11).
  - Hesper High becomes the Nave and then the dead "Hulk", where the fallen Count Board plates become children's counting toys (ch. 9, 12).
  - The phrase "Count what's owed and not a mark more" recurs in every era.
  - The three-knock "still here" signal is first used at the Nursery glass (ch. 1) and closes the book (ch. 21).

### 2. Synchronized pacing: **9/10**
The three arcs rise in lockstep:
- Inciting discoveries come in ch. 1–3: the note "The Scholarship is a sale", Anselm's hidden zero, and the herald's word "Account".
- The midpoint turns come in ch. 10–12: the inversion plan, the First Book and Compact, and the receivers coming through.
- The dark moments come in ch. 13–15: Pell is dragged onto the *Grace*; Piet is audited; Kask exposes Rook's substitution of Wren for his son.
- A mentor dies on the road to Gallows in each of ch. 16–18: Lucan, Corin and Haddie.
- The climaxes fall in the last three chapters, one per era (ch. 19, 20, 21).

The climaxes are chained causally:
- Maren's log and key at Gallows (ch. 19) are what Ysolde finds and acts on (ch. 20).
- Ysolde's sealed reconciliation is the "receipt" that Wren transmits to the receiver (ch. 21).

The only reservation is that the parallelism is sometimes almost schematic: three mentor sacrifices in three consecutive chapters.

### 3. No spoilers: **8/10**
Nothing is stated outright before its era reaches it. The big reveals are held with real discipline:
- Ysolde reaches Gallows at the end of ch. 17, but the body in the chair is withheld until ch. 20, after Maren's climax in ch. 19.
- Ysolde's letter and the closed account are held for ch. 21.

The later eras do leak some inferential hints:
- **Ch. 2 and ch. 6 (Ledger, Door):** the Door/passage is "shut" and the account is "placed in Recovery upon the closure of the passage". This comes before the Bond era shows how. It is mitigated: the Bond era itself says in ch. 4 that the Throat will close within about 18 months, and the real question (who closed it, and on which side the *Grace* ended up) is protected. Quist's version in ch. 8 ("the Combine… shut the Door behind it") actively misdirects.
- **Ch. 2 and ch. 5 (Ledger):** every diving family wears a broken half-disc tag as an heirloom. A sharp reader can infer that both halves of the surety discs stayed on Hesper, so the wards were never sold. This comes before ch. 13 and ch. 19.
- **Ch. 8 (Ledger):** "a number of children who came into the Stewards' care", and a First Auditor who "learned her numbers at the Stewards' knees". This hints that wards stayed behind. It is dressed as myth.
- **Ch. 11 (Ledger):** the First Book has a "WARDS" page, and the First Auditor's chair carries a broken diver's tag. This hints that Pell stayed, before ch. 13 and ch. 19.
- **Ch. 3, 6, 9, 12 (Door):** Count-books are relics, the "old believers" are dwindling, and the Nave is a ruin dead for 200 years. This reveals that the Ledger era's institution eventually falls, before that arc ends. It does not reveal Ysolde's own fate.

### 4. Coherence and continuity: **9/10**
- The internal logic is unusually tight and the numbers hold. The Count's year 341 corresponds to 2794 only if the first Count began about 20 years after the closure, which matches Pell writing it as an adult. The console's "380–420 years" restoration estimate matches the Door's reopening around 3159–3190.
- Planted details pay off cleanly:
  - The herald's notice that "Debtors may present statements of payment, under the seal of an authorised officer" (ch. 6) is exactly how the plot resolves in ch. 20–21.
  - "He kept the account" on the last niche (ch. 11) is paid off by the seal ring reading CASTELLANE (ch. 20).
  - The Mercy's pup boat (ch. 17) is found at Gallows in ch. 21, labelled "*Mercy* 2".
  - The torn docking ring from ch. 19 is still there in ch. 20 and ch. 21.
- Weak points:
  - **Why only twelve Stewards?** The *Grace* turned back carrying the Factor, all his officers, crew and 340 marines, yet the Nave's founding is "twelve Stewards". This is never explained.
  - **Convenient devices.** A live Combine mine is conveniently placed for Corin's sacrifice (ch. 17). The Prelate's secret oath happens to contain Pell's name (ch. 20).
  - **The ending is a little mechanical.** The receiver's capitulation to a sealed statement is planted, but it still resolves by machine.

### 5. Craft: **9/10**
- **Prose and voice.** The prose is consistently excellent: concrete, sensory and rhythmic. Rook's voice is the standout ("I've had a lot of thoughts I'm ashamed of. That's the one", ch. 9).
- **Characters.** They are morally dense:
  - Dov's betrayal for a seat on the *Grace*, and his redemption singing the hymn wrong as the frigate fires (ch. 16, 19).
  - Quist's argument that the lie holds 190,000 people together is a real argument, not a straw man (ch. 8, 14).
  - Rook's substitution of Wren for his son, and Wren's "So pay what you've got, and don't stop" (ch. 18).
- **Structure.** The debt-and-ledger theme is the plot's actual mechanism, not decoration.
- **The ending** ("Receipt", the knocks, "*Still here.*") is earned and resonant.
- **Faults.**
  - The refrains ("count what's owed", "I'll not tell you about this") are heavy by the end.
  - The Door era's drones and receiver are more generic SF than the human material around them.

### 6. Overall: **9/10**

## The second book (P)

### 1. Interlacing; three distinct eras and casts: **10/10**
- The rotation is strict and the casts are fully distinct.
- The narrative modes are well differentiated:
  - close third-person past (Maren);
  - first-person present (Isaura: "The first air I cut that year belongs to a family named Ocampo", ch. 2);
  - third-person present (Til).
- The anchors are superb:
  - The Lamp itself, a periodic comet that "takes the dead out and brings them back".
  - The Share Plate and its Blank Share.
  - Teska's chalked comet on the drum (ch. 1), which reappears in the Spine's nave (ch. 2) and on Platform Four (ch. 9).
  - The tether-chief's coat and line knife, which pass across all three eras.
  - The Roundsmen's salt-and-loop rites (ch. 3), revealed as dipper customs.

### 2. Synchronized pacing: **9/10**
- The comet's 263-year period is an elegant engine for the brief's "simultaneous" requirement: each era's climax is a pass of the same comet.
- The climaxes come in ch. 16, 17 and 18: Abel is thrown to the Lamp; Isaura finds the Gull and Corin dies; Til harpoons the *Hymn* and Oz dies. They resolve in ch. 19, 20 and 21: Maren is sold as "the Purchase"; Isaura's broadcast and the egg is sent round again; the egg goes into Gall.
- The rising actions match beat for beat:
  - the dispatch, the siege and the job (ch. 1–3);
  - the egg, the Plate's numbers, and the radar ping/light (ch. 7–9);
  - Asset Seven, the register and the dark moments (ch. 11–15).
- The only small unevenness is that the Bonded era's emotional peak (ch. 16) comes a round earlier than its true reveal (ch. 19).

### 3. No spoilers: **6/10**
This is the book's real weakness. The later eras state the first era's broad outcome very early, and reveal the fate of the Siege era's object before that era resolves.

- **Ch. 2 (Siege):** the founders "bought their air"; "the city of Patience is a free city, established… in the year twenty-three ninety-one". This tells the reader that the bonded keep the ship and the Camp survives before ch. 4 has even set up the closure and the Levy transfer. The book later shows the "Purchase" was a myth, but the outcome it reports is true.
- **Ch. 6 (Last Return):** the Combine "lost" Corva in 2391, the lane later "went dark", and Kettle has been silent for 200-plus years. This foreshadows that the Siege ends in isolation, with the city surviving (the radar sweep and the light in ch. 6 and ch. 9). The anchor's destruction itself is only shown in ch. 20.
- **Ch. 8 (Siege):** an item of Company property was "never recovered" and is "still in the system". This comes before the bonded seize the egg in ch. 10, so the seizure's success is given away in advance.
- **Ch. 11 (Siege) and ch. 12 (Last Return):** the register reads "Seized by bonded mutineers 17.xi.2391. Not recovered", and the Plate's elements are the Lamp's orbit. Together these confirm that the throw succeeds, before ch. 16 shows it.
- **Ch. 6 and ch. 12 (Last Return):** in 2917 Hollis believes the asset is still on the Lamp. This implies that the 2654 expedition does not bring it home or use it, before ch. 17 and ch. 20 resolve that.

Mitigation: the specific secrets are carefully protected. Abel's guilt (ch. 13), who actually rides the Lamp (ch. 16), Hale's sale of Maren and the meaning of the Blank Share (ch. 19), and the second carving and the recorders (ch. 20–21) all land as genuine surprises. The Tether-Woman legend (ch. 14) misdirects nicely. But the arcs' outcomes are largely known in advance.

### 4. Coherence and continuity: **9/10**
- The book is very consistent:
  - The dates line up: seizure on 17 November (ch. 10 and ch. 13), matching the register in ch. 12; Abel's recording on 30 November; 2391 + 263 = 2654 + 263 = 2917.
  - The egg's 40-kelvin dormancy rule governs both rescues.
  - Quist's "nothing… except a falling mountain" (ch. 10) is paid off in ch. 21.
  - The Gull is knocked onto its side by the 2654 shelling and is found that way in 2917. The second anchor and the craters on the ridge carry through.
  - The Plate passes Kettle → Lamp → Kettle.
- Minor issues:
  - Isaura's broadcast says Corin "shot at it yesterday" while also placing his death "an hour" after they found the egg (ch. 20).
  - The crew watches Hale chisel off Maren's name, yet the "Company's share" myth takes hold. This is plausible, but asserted rather than shown.
  - The Til/Szabo/Marchetti plot leans on a conventional double-agent reveal (ch. 15).

### 5. Craft: **9/10**
- **Prose and characters.** The prose is strong and controlled, and the characters are vivid.
  - Hale is a superb soft-voiced villain.
  - Quist's "gardening" and roses arc is a quiet, original redemption (ch. 10, 19).
  - Abel's drugging of his sister and taking her coat (ch. 16) is the emotional high point of either book.
  - Isaura's arc runs from "Nobody dies on the floor" to turning every valve to the stop and Pilar saying "Breathe" (ch. 20), and is beautifully closed.
- **The ending.** The lamps coming on across Kettle with "There's air" (ch. 21) is moving.
- **Faults.**
  - The Last Return thread (mercenary broker, Directorate spy, harpoon gambit) is noticeably more generic than the other two. Til's secret about the cut line is predictable once set up.
  - Recurring tics ("as if… about the weather", "like a dropped tray", "the way you'd…") and near-verbatim repeated descriptions of the ice room (ch. 17, 18, 21) border on repetition.
  - The "cut the line" motif is pressed hard.

### 6. Overall: **8/10**

## Verdict

**The first book (R) is better. Strength of preference: clear.**

These are two genuinely accomplished novels, and on several criteria they are level. R wins for three reasons:

1. **Spoiler discipline on the hard part of the brief.**
   - P's later eras state the first era's outcome almost immediately: the bonded win the ship and found a free city (ch. 2), and the asset is seized and "not recovered" (ch. 8, 11, 12). They also strongly imply the Siege era's outcomes before those arcs climax.
   - R's later eras leak only inferential hints (the heirloom tags, the wards page). Its official histories actively misdirect, and it holds every major reveal until the round in which the earlier era reaches it.
2. **A tighter causal chain that is also the theme.**
   - In R, each climax is the instrument of the next: Maren's log and key, then Ysolde's sealed reconciliation, then Wren's transmitted "receipt". The ledger/debt motif is the actual mechanism of the resolution, planted as early as ch. 6.
   - P's comet is an elegant synchronizer, but its third era resolves through a more conventional action plot (harpoon, betrayal, cable) than through anything the earlier eras built.
3. **Evenness of quality across all three threads.**
   - Every R thread is morally dense: Dov's betrayal, Quist's defensible lie, Rook's substitution of Wren.
   - P's Bonded and Siege threads are as good as anything in R. Its Last Return thread (Szabo, Marchetti, Til's secret) is noticeably more generic, and P has more repeated phrasing and recycled description.
