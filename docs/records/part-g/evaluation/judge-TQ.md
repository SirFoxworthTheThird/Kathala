# Judgement: the first book (T) vs the second book (Q)

I read both books in full: all 21 chapters of each, the first book (T) first.

- **T:** *The Crossing* (CR 611, Annick Vell, purser of the colony transport *Merciful Increase*) / *The Account* (CR 929, Hollis Fen, Assessor, told in the first person) / *The Salvage* (CR 1317, Yara Tull, diver, told in the present tense). About 99,000 words.
- **Q:** *The Closing* (2387 CE, Dagny Ostrova, Wick-tender on the hollow rock Lantern) / *The Owing* (311 AC, Tamsin Hask the cutter and Orrin Sallow the counter) / *The Healing* (3089 CE, Corvin Saal, debt adjuster, with Fen Adaire). About 75,000 words.

Both are strong, finished novels that take the brief seriously. Both rotate the eras strictly (1-2-3, seven times) and both bring their three climaxes into the last three chapters. They differ most in how well the later eras keep the earlier eras' secrets, and in how deep the writing goes.

---

## The first book (T)

### 1. Interlacing, three distinct eras and casts: **10/10**
The rotation is strict, the three casts are entirely separate, and each era has its own narrative voice: third person past for 611, a first-person retrospective for 929 (which Ch20 reveals is being told from inside the sealed well), and present tense for 1317. One ship serves as the anchor for all three eras: the *Merciful Increase* in 611, the Hulk station in 929 and the falling wreck in 1317. Other objects also carry across the centuries: the strongroom and its legend "WHAT IS WRITTEN IS OWED", Key 0447, the privy box, the dead-books shelf with its "gap between II and IV", Ballast Tank Four (the Fourth Cistern in 929, the Widow in 1317) and the coil-gang song. The song changes from era to era: "when the Book goes down the sea", then "when the ash is paid, says he", then "down the Widow, down we go". The anchors are subtle and they carry the plot.

### 2. Synchronized pacing: **9/10**
The three arcs build together and peak together:
- **Set-up (Ch1–3):** a debt passes to a dead man's children, a Keeper is murdered, and the writ arrives.
- **First turn (Ch7–9):** the mutineers' arms cache is raided, the Ostrava sale is revealed and an assassin attacks Fen in the spoke lift, and first satisfaction is ordered.
- **Moral nadir (Ch10–12):** Annick gives up Marit's name and Marit is spaced, Fen is framed and goes on the run, and Mitya's betrayal comes to light as the Crown burns up.
- **Eve (Ch13–15):** the night before the Fork, the transports arrive, and Mitya dies at the vault door.
- **Crises (Ch16–18):** the rising at the Fork, Burning Day, and the empty vault.
- **Climaxes (Ch19–21):** Annick discharges the debts and drops the Book down the ballast chute, Fen lodges his oath and recovers the Book, and Yara broadcasts it.

Each era also runs on a clock: the Fork countdown in 611, the days to the Burning in 929, and the number of perigee passes left in 1317. The only unevenness is that Ch16 and Ch19 are so climactic for era 1 that era 1 peaks slightly before the other two, but that is by design.

### 3. No spoilers: **7/10**
The later eras do reveal outcomes of earlier eras, but almost always as in-world myth that later turns out to be false, so the real secrets survive. Cases:
- **Ch2 (929), about era 1:** The Catechism tells us the *Increase* reached Absalom and not Shale, that the Founders "burned the Book", and that Annick Vell "would have sold us". This settles era 1's central question (Shale or Absalom) by chapter 2, which is a real cost. However, the myth is false: Voyage III was burned, the Book went down the ballast chute, and Quarry invented "Liberty". None of that is given away before Ch16 and Ch19.
- **Ch3 and Ch6 (1317), about era 2:** These show "the Night of the Hulk", the Houses later gone, the Dimming, and the "Fen the Turncoat" song (he sold the Hold and fled). They reveal that era 2 ends in a cataclysm in which the Houses fall. On Fen's own fate they say two contradictory things ("he went out on the tide" against "Fen's in there somewhere, counting"), so the misdirection holds. Ch14 later shows the "turncoat" label being invented as Houses propaganda.
- **Ch11 (929), about era 1:** Thorne shows that the founding page was composed nine years after Landfall, without a purser's seal. This hints, before Ch16 and Ch19, that Annick never sealed the Founders' patent. The hint is mild.
- **Ch12 (1317), about era 2:** Kade says that on the Night of the Hulk, Fen took "something sealed" that would have destroyed the Account. This forecasts era 2's climax before Ch17 and Ch20.
- **Ch18 (1317), about era 2:** This is the most concrete case. Yara finds a white-haired man in grey wearing the Office chain with its scales medallion, tied to the cradle pedestal by his own belt, next to an open, empty privy safe. Two chapters later, in Ch20, Lucan Marsh dies exactly like that. The text misdirects (Yara takes him for Fen), and the real twist, that Fen went down the well with the Book, is kept back. But an attentive reader of Ch2 and Ch17 can recognise Marsh and work out that the safe was opened.

### 4. Coherence and continuity: **8/10**
The plot is unusually tight and the cross-era logic holds:
- The intervals add up: 611 to 929 is 318 years, 929 to 1317 is 388, and "706 years ago" in Ch21 matches.
- The Voyage III gap is planted in Ch5 and Ch11, explained in Ch19, and proved by the "VOY. III" plate in Ch21.
- The poisoned Fourth Cistern (Ch11) is the Widow (Ch3 and Ch6) and the ballast well (Ch19 and Ch20).
- The recovery-port lid jammed by the salvo in Ch20 matches the bent frame in Ch21.
- Fen's borrowed coat with the burned cuff (Ch17) is the coat on his body in Ch21.

Stretches and loose ends:
- **The Clerk's ruling.** In Ch21 he certifies an Article Eleven reading that reaches him over the radio band, which is convenient.
- **Annick's appeal to Article Nine.** She invokes it to drop the Book although she had sealed Szell as Master herself. The book can argue that a Master who murdered his way in is not lawfully in command, but it never addresses the point.
- **The missing Account.** In Ch18 the crystal has been torn from its bands and is gone, and nobody explains it.
- **The surviving claim.** It is never made clear how the Paschal Company's copy kept its standing after Thorne voided the Account in Ch20. Fen's entry partly covers this.

None of these breaks the experience.

### 5. Craft: **9/10**
- **Prose.** It is precise, physical and patient. The zero-g Hold, the drowned Index full of drifting cards, the Crown burning up across Absalom, and the thaw in the vault are all superbly realised.
- **Character.** The characters are morally layered. Annick trades Marit for her sister and then writes that crime against herself in the Book. Tobiah agrees to "Liberty" because "somebody's got to pay for the air". Szell is the pilot and also Benn's murderer, and Quarry is the urbane architect of the new debt. Marsh's "every ledger is a forgery" gives the antagonist a real philosophy.
- **Dialogue.** It is spare and loaded: "Write it truly, Purser" and "Choose the one who can fly".
- **Structure.** The motifs of counting, the voice that "shakes" in the lattice, the level clerk's voice and "Don't sell it" all pay off.
- **Ending.** Yara's "Before witnesses. Who were awake" answers Annick's "Who were asleep". The Book burns up with the Hulk, held by no one, and the final line, "nobody, anywhere, is counting", lands with real force.
- **Faults.** The motif repetition occasionally tips into tic, with "I counted" and "four in, hold, four out" used a great deal. The book is long, though it is seldom padded.

### 6. Overall: **9/10**
An ambitious, intricately built and moving novel. Its three timelines are not merely parallel: each one's lie is the next one's foundation, and the final era undoes all of them.

## The second book (Q)

### 1. Interlacing, three distinct eras and casts: **9/10**
The rotation is strict, the three casts are distinct and the three eras are clearly marked. The anchors work well:
- the bell of the *Ardent Mercy* and its iron Hook, rung for the dead in every era;
- the root console with its slots and grille;
- the hollow rock Lantern and its Wick;
- the *Covenant*, revealed as the "Hulk" the Count was built on;
- the Hask family line, from Agathe and Tamsin to Senna Hask.

All three eras are told in the same third-person past voice, so they feel less distinct as experiences than T's do.

### 2. Synchronized pacing: **8/10**
The arcs rise together and climax together: the quit-claim, the shear and Dagny's death (Ch19), the Wick relit (Ch20), and the names spoken at the root (Ch21). The middle chapters line up as well: the Caul revelation, Hull 7731 and the sleepers in the hold (Ch10–12), and the betrayal at the root, the Calling and Ode's death (Ch16–18). Era 3's first third (Ch3, Ch6, Ch9) is slower: an approach voyage with a lot of travel and set-up, held together by the slow reveal of the hold. Era 3's climax also depends almost entirely on what eras 1 and 2 set up, which makes Corvin's arc feel reactive until Ch18.

### 3. No spoilers: **5/10**
Several later-era chapters reveal concrete outcomes or mechanisms of era 1 before era 1 reaches them, often word for word:
- **Ch2 (311 AC), about era 1:** The founding story says "the Company ran… took the bell… left the Tally, and the Account, and the people". This answers whether the Company leaves and the Tally stays before Dagny's campaign begins. It is partly myth (the *Covenant* came back), but the core of it is true.
- **Ch3 (3089), about era 1:** The *Ardent Mercy*'s bell, with "Lemuel Adeyemi" freshly cut on its shoulder, turned up in the Concord 30 years after the Closing on an unidentified hulk. This reveals that the bell gets out through the Throat, which is the outcome of Kas's run, before the bell plan is even conceived in Ch13.
- **Ch5 (311 AC), about era 1: the most serious case.** The first root entry, dated "the forty-ninth day of the Closing", reads "ACCOUNT ESTABLISHED. CREDITOR: CAUL… ACCOUNT HOLDER: NONE", followed by "AUTHORISED BY A KEY NOT KNOWN TO THE TALLY". This is word for word what Dagny's quit-claim produces in Ch19. It tells the reader in chapter 5 that someone will succeed at the root on day 49 with a secret key, which undercuts Basu's later "there's no back door" and the whole plan at the root.
- **Ch6 (3089), about era 1:** Fen identifies a band of hundreds of names cut "all at once, at the end" in hurried hands. This anticipates the cutting of the 904 Caul names (Ch13 and Ch16).
- **Ch9 (3089), about era 1:** Roach's log ("Asked the Concern again for confirmation of the closing-phase figures. No reply… They told me someone would come for me") signals that the closing is fake one chapter before Basu reveals it in Ch10.
- **Ch12 (3089), about era 1:** The band is matched to the full Caul roster before Odile even agrees to cut it in Ch13. The same chapter repeats that the Tally changed its keys on day 49, signalling again that the root plan succeeds.

The later chapters often do this deliberately for dramatic irony (Ch19's console text "lands" because we have seen it). But the brief asks that the future must not spoil the past, and the case at Ch5 in particular gives away the shape of era 1's ending.

### 4. Coherence and continuity: **7/10**
The plot logic is mostly sound and some of it is elegant:
- The *Covenant* is "Hull 7731" and the true "Hulk".
- The returned Company staff become the "Founders".
- The log reveals that the Count turned "owed to the dead of Caul" into a ghost story.
- The sleeper count is consistent (3,012 falls to 3,011 after Marisol Tey's death).
- The Tally's key change on day 49 matches across eras.

Weaknesses:
- **A dangling thread.** In Ch2 and Ch8 the dead woman in Company coveralls clutching a child's red shoe lies outside welded Aft Store 3, with a child's bones inside and "SHE SAID SHE WOULD COME BACK" scratched on the wall. It is the most haunting image in era 2, and it is never explained beyond Tamsin's "whoever she was".
- **The bell's survival.** It comes out of a shear-collapsed Throat intact on a "hulk", which is hand-waved.
- **Quick turns.** Haste concedes the instrument is void on the strength of one certification, and Achterberg turns into the honest Reader, both quickly.
- **Kas.** His last-minute redemption run comes after a betrayal, and the book signals it only through Pim's observation.

### 5. Craft: **8/10**
- **Prose and set pieces.** The prose is clean, controlled and often affecting, and the set pieces are excellent:
  - Joss falling outward from the Wick;
  - the miners frozen mid-stride in the clear ice, with the fingernail gouges on the hatch;
  - the *Saint Ishmael* folded by the closing lips;
  - Marisol Tey's question, "whether it attaches";
  - Ode's "Oh, you stupid boy".
- **Ending.** It is cathartic: the names spoken one by one to the bell, and "VALVES RELEASED TO OPEN".
- **Thin antagonists.** Haste, Vosk and Lusk are smooth, menacing administrators with less inner life than T's antagonists.
- **Passive protagonist.** Corvin spends most of the book acquiescing, which is the point, but it is static.
- **Repeated phrases.** Some recur often enough to show: "two letters leaning on each other like tired men"; "the way a man walks toward a door he means to close" (twice); "the way you would take something from a child" (twice); and Kas's "smile from when he was five and had broken something".
- **The final line.** "Nobody owed anything at all" ties things up more neatly than T's ending does.

### 6. Overall: **7/10**
A well-made, emotionally satisfying novel with a strong central conceit: a debt assigned to the dead and payable only in their names. Its pacing is good. It is let down by the later eras telegraphing era 1's climax, and by thinner characterisation away from its central figures.

## Verdict

**The first book (T) is better. The preference is clear.**

The three most important reasons:

1. **It keeps the past's secrets better.** T's later eras reveal earlier outcomes mainly as false myth: the "burned" Book, the Purser "who would have sold us", "Fen the Turncoat". The truth then overturns each one, so foreknowledge becomes a source of irony rather than a spoiler. Q's later eras print era 1's climax mechanism word for word in Ch5 (the "creditor Caul, holder none" root entry, authorised by an unknown key, dated day 49). They also reveal that the bell escapes (Ch3) and that the 904 names are cut (Ch6 and Ch12) well before era 1 gets there.
2. **Its three timelines are bound together more deeply.** In T each era's founding lie is literally the next era's legal instrument. Annick's sealed discharge and her Voyage III substitution, Fen's lodging, and Yara's broadcast are three links in one chain, and each depends on objects the earlier era left behind: the key, the plaque, the ballast well. Q's linking is good, with the bell, the root and the *Covenant*. But era 3 mostly retrieves what eras 1 and 2 set up, rather than adding its own turn.
3. **Its craft and characterisation are richer.** T has three distinct narrative voices and morally complicated figures on every side: Annick's betrayal of Marit, Tobiah's compromise, Szell, Quarry and Marsh. Its motifs are woven tightly, and its ending answers "who were asleep" with "who were awake". Q is clean and moving but more conventional. Its villains are thinner, its phrasing repeats more, and its ending is tidier.
