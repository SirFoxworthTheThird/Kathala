# Judgement: the first book (T) against the second book (R)

I read both books in full, all 21 chapters of each, starting with the first book (T). They are close in length, about 99,000 words for T and about 98,000 for R, so length gives neither an edge. Both are serious, accomplished novels. Both use the same broad structure: a strict 1-2-3 rotation of eras, and an object or act from the first era that is carried forward and completed by the second and third. The difference between them lies in how well each keeps the eras' secrets, how distinct its voices are, and how firm its logic is.

---

## The first book (T)

**Eras:** *The Crossing* (CR 611), *The Account* (CR 929), *The Salvage* (CR 1317).

### 1. Brief compliance: interlacing, three eras, three casts: **9/10**
The chapters rotate strictly 1-2-3 from Chapter 1 to Chapter 21. The casts do not overlap: Annick, Dace, Benn, Quarry, Szell and Tobiah in the first era; Fen, Marsh, Ottilie, Thorne, Calla and Wren in the second; Yara, Pim, Mitya, Sabeth, Kade, Quell and Dunn in the third. The eras are also told in three different modes: close third person in the past tense, a first-person retrospective confession, and present tense. That makes each era recognisable from its first line. The anchors are many and handled with skill:
- the ship, which becomes the Hulk and then the falling wreck;
- the red motto WHAT IS WRITTEN IS OWED, which decays to "WHAT IS WRIT — — — OWED" (Chs 9 and 15);
- key 0447, which becomes the card D-0447 (Ch 2) and is then hung on Pim's neck;
- the coil-gang song, which changes its last line across the centuries;
- Ballast Tank Four, which becomes the "Fourth Cistern" and then "the Widow";
- the burned box, which becomes the Ash Case.

### 2. Brief compliance: synchronised pacing: **9/10**
Each era has its own ticking clock: thirty-five, then seventeen, days to the Fork; the countdown to Burning Day; "Seven passes … Four passes … Two passes." The three clocks run down together. The beats match closely across the eras:
- **Chs 7–9, an arrest or ambush:** the weapons cache is found and Dace is taken; Fen is attacked in the spoke lift; Yara's line is cut in the Hall.
- **Chs 10–12, a betrayal at the midpoint:** Annick gives Quarry Marit's name; Bastian frames Fen and Wren is taken into custody; Yara learns Sabeth bought Mitya.
- **Chs 13–15, the lowest point:** the night before the Fork; Fen hiding in the Sump; Mitya dies under the beam.
- **Chs 16–18, the climaxes begin:** the rising at the Fork; Burning Day; the empty vault.
- **Chs 19–21, the resolutions:** Annick commits the Book to the ballast, then the Landfall burning (19); Fen in the Fourth Cistern (20); Yara's broadcast (21).

Each era climaxes in turn at the end. Each climax completes the one before it without having needed it to exist.

### 3. Brief compliance: no spoilers: **7/10**
The book's main device is that later eras hold false legends about earlier ones. The Purser "who would have sold us", "They burned the Book", "Fen the Turncoat". Every one of these is overturned only when its own era reaches the truth. The real secrets stay hidden until their era reveals them: Szell murdered Benn; Annick discharged every debt and dropped the real Book down the chute; Fen is in the cistern well with the Book. That is careful work. Some cases still leak too much:
- **Chapter 2 (era 2), against era 1.** The Catechism says the *Increase* "carried us" to Absalom, that the Founders "burned the Book", and that Annick Vell is remembered as the purser who would have sold them. This answers era 1's surface question, Shale or Absalom, fourteen chapters before the Fork (Ch 16) and Landfall (Ch 19). It also tells us that Annick lives to be named. The legend is false in its details, but the outcome frame is given away.
- **Chapters 3 and 6 (era 3), against era 2.** Divers say "Fen's dead … Fen's in there somewhere, counting", and the books speak of a "Night of the Hulk", of Fen stealing "something from the Office", and of "nobody's been into the vault since the Night of the Hulk". Before era 2 has properly begun, the reader learns there will be a violent night on the Hulk and that Fen ends up dead inside it. Chs 3 and 9 add that the Account survives as a certified copy sold on to Vey, which partly answers whether era 2's struggle destroys it.
- **Chapter 18 (era 3), against era 2's Chapter 20.** Yara finds a white-haired man in a grey coat wearing a chain with a scales medallion. He is tethered to the cradle pedestal, the purser's box is open, and the Account has been torn away. She takes him for Fen. But the chain is the Chief Assessor's (Ch 2, Ch 17), so an attentive reader learns, two chapters early, that Marsh dies in the vault and the box gets opened.
- **Mild clues rather than spoilers:**
  - Ch 5: VOYAGE III is "lost before Landfall".
  - Ch 11: the founding page was grown nine years after Landfall under a council seal and has no voice. This hints that the purser refused the Founders.
  - Ch 17: Marsh reads out that key 0447 is "in the Purser's keeping … never recovered", two chapters before Ch 19 shows Annick hiding it.

### 4. Coherence and continuity: **8/10**
The architecture of clues and payoffs is unusually tight. Chapter 1 shows the drop and the recovery port; Chapter 3 has the knocking in the sealed tank; Chapter 5 has the missing Voyage III; Chapter 11 has the "Fourth Cistern. Closed CR 718. Contaminated." All of it pays off in Chapters 19–21, and the "VOY. III" plate on the Ash Case closes the loop. Fen's past-tense narration ("I've had a long time since") turns out to come from inside the well, which reframes the whole era without contradicting it. The dates hold (611 to 929 to 1317; "seven hundred and six years ago"; "388 years"). I noticed no contradictions. A few points strain belief:
- Fen's face is "whole" after nearly four centuries of ice and thaw.
- Kade accepts a sealed box without checking it (Ch 21).
- The Clerk certifies a seal sent over a radio link.
- Quarry, and probably Tobiah, notice the drop, keep quiet, and no Founder or House checks the ballast in three hundred years.
- The seal-reader and Quell's cable are convenient.

### 5. Craft: **9/10**
- **Prose:** precise, textured and quietly witty: "honest for so long that it had worn smooth, like a step"; the Account sitting "like a great grey animal crouched on a stool too small for it".
- **Character:** morally complex.
  - Annick's trade of Marit for Dace is the novel's moral centre. Annick never excuses it and finally records it against herself.
  - Tobiah agrees to "somebody's got to pay for the air" and still sings at the burning.
  - Quarry and Marsh are intelligent antagonists with real arguments: "every ledger is a forgery … who holds the pen".
  - Mitya's betrayal and his death are earned.
- **Dialogue:** restrained and full of subtext.
- **Ending:** the Book keeps the voice, and a voice "shakes" on one word. The three closing entries rhyme: "Who were asleep." / Fen's lone reading / "Who were awake." The Ash Case turns out to be the wrong book, and Quell decides to leave the plaque as it is ("It's true now. It just took longer"). Yara chooses to leave the Book in the falling Hulk so that no one holds it, and the book ends on "Don't sell it." It is moving, and it closes all three eras on a single idea.
- **Faults:**
  - Some verbal tics are shared across eras ("which was one of the things she loved him for", the level voice, "four in, hold, four out").
  - Every protagonist is a counter, so under the different tenses the voices sometimes run together.
  - A few devices are convenient.

### 6. Overall: **9/10**
An ambitious, tightly built, emotionally serious novel. Its three narrative modes really do feel like three eras, and its three climaxes form one thought. Its spoiler handling is good, though not perfect.

## The second book (R)

**Eras:** *The Bond* (2431), *The Ledger* (2794), *The Door* (3190).

### 1. Brief compliance: interlacing, three eras, three casts: **8/10**
The chapters rotate strictly 1-2-3. The casts are distinct: Maren, Pell, Dov, Hatch, Lucan and Castellane; Ysolde, Piet, Anselm, Quist, Corin and Strand; Rook, Wren, Haddie, Kask and Benno. The anchors are excellent:
- the broken surety discs, which become the divers' tags and finally rejoin as 0212 (Ch 20);
- "Count what's owed / And not a mark more";
- the breathers' hymn, sung with the third line wrong;
- Hesper High, which becomes the Nave and then the dead Hulk with its Count Board fallen;
- Gallows and its "Hanged Men";
- the three knocks.

Era 3's first-person voice (Rook) is distinctive. Eras 1 and 2, however, share one close-third, past-tense register and sometimes blur.

### 2. Brief compliance: synchronised pacing: **9/10**
This is R's greatest strength. All three arcs converge on one place, Gallows, and climax in consecutive chapters: Maren inverts the pins (Ch 19), Ysolde restores them and seals the reconciliation (Ch 20), and Wren sends the receipt (Ch 21). The middle beats line up:
- **Organising:** the union on the water (Ch 7); the Prelate's dinner (Ch 8); the Kell Deep raid (Ch 9).
- **Escalation:** the inversion plan revealed (Ch 10); the First Book (Ch 11); the receivers arrive (Ch 12).
- **Catastrophe:** the dock massacre and Pell taken (Ch 13); Piet's audit (Ch 14); Kask takes Wren (Ch 15).
- **The run to the Door:** Dov's betrayal and the key (Ch 16); the long fall (Ch 17); Haddie at the switch (Ch 18).

The three journeys to the same station are paced almost to the day.

### 3. Brief compliance: no spoilers: **5/10**
R also uses the false-legend device, "the Door is shut because the account is open". But era 2 lets the reader work out era 1's ending well before era 1 reaches it:
- **Chapter 2 (era 2), against era 1.** The Door is shut, and the account is "held by the Combine beyond the Door". The reader learns at the start that the Throat closes with the colony inside, which is era 1's stake.
- **Chapter 8 (era 2), against era 1.** Quist's story: twelve Stewards were left with thirty thousand people. "A child … very young when the Door was shut … learned her numbers at the Stewards' knees … wrote the first Count". She wrote the Tull family's own phrase at its head, "Count what is owed and not a mark more". Together with Pell's "Aptitude A+ (numeric)" (Ch 4), this tells the reader that Pell is not sold to Brannock and becomes the First Auditor. That is era 1's central question, answered eleven chapters early.
- **Chapter 11 (era 2), against era 1.** It comes one chapter after Maren's idea in Chapter 10 to shut the Throat "in front of them". The Compact then reads: "the Door is closed, and shall be taught as closed against *them*", "No soul shall go to Gallows. What was done at Gallows is accounted and closed", followed by a WARDS list. By here the reader can work out that Maren's plan succeeds and that the Combine's officers are stranded and become the Stewards. Era 1's Gallows climax (Ch 19) is eight chapters away.
- **Chapter 6 (era 3), against era 1.** The receiver's notice, "placed in Recovery upon the closure of the passage", confirms the closure.
- **Chapter 9 (era 3), against era 2.** The Hulk is the dead Nave, "dead for two hundred years", with the Count Board fallen in a heap. The end of the Nave and of the Count's institution is shown before era 2's climax.
- **Chapter 15 (era 3), against eras 1 and 2.** A Steward's skeleton in a grey coat lies in the "STEWARD — He kept the account" niche. This anticipates the reveal that Castellane stayed on Vigil.

Chapter 20's twist, that the Stewards were runaways caught by a diver, is still powerful. But the reader has largely assembled it by halfway through the book.

### 4. Coherence and continuity: **7/10**
The relay logic is elegant and consistent:
- the restoration estimate of 380–420 years matches Ysolde's 2794 setting and the Door "breathing" by 3159–3190;
- the receiver's Chapter 6 promise that "Debtors may present statements of payment, under seal" is paid off by the reconciliation sealed with Castellane's ring;
- Haddie's oldest Count-book charges "pins" to "Gallows".

There are some soft spots:
- The *Grace* returns with every officer, the marines and the crew, yet the Compact has exactly twelve Stewards, and Ysolde reads them as "the Factor and his officers". How so few came to rule, and what became of everyone else, is never addressed.
- The receiver capitulates instantly and transfers its own title "in settlement of balance", which is close to a deus ex machina.
- The Prelate's secret oath conveniently holds Pell's name, and the quotation of Maren's log is subtly altered so that the name lands ("I hope it's you, Pell").
- Pell, at twelve, is called "very young".
- Dov's treachery is telegraphed: the missing fourteenth lamp in Ch 7, "thirteen" in Ch 13.

### 5. Craft: **8/10**
- **Strengths:**
  - Vivid set pieces: the downdraft dive, the silent refinery head, the climb through Kell Deep and Mattie's fall, Haddie holding the switch, Corin's "Count it, Captain", the slingshot through the giant.
  - Rook's confessional voice ("I'll not tell you …") is strong.
  - Maren's log at the console, and the three knocks closing both eras 1 and 3, are genuinely moving.
  - Castellane ("It's arithmetic") and Quist (beans and the window) are memorable antagonists.
- **Weaknesses:**
  - The book relies on a run of near-identical noble sacrifices: Lucan, Hatch, Corin, Haddie, Quist, and Dov by implication. Each is affecting alone; in series they become a formula.
  - Kask and Strand are fairly stock villains, smiling and "a hand on the back of your neck".
  - The refrains "count what's owed / not a mark more" and "do the sum" recur so often that they lose force.
  - The receiver's pods and drones tip era 3 toward pulp.
- **Ending:** thematically complete and warm (the receipt, "Credit to debtors", Rook holding the Door, Wren going through). It is slightly more sentimental than earned.

### 6. Overall: **8/10**
A strong, emotionally generous novel, with the best convergence of climaxes either book achieves. It is let down mainly by how much era 2 gives away about era 1, and by a repeated pattern of sacrifice.

## Verdict

**The first book (T) is better. The preference is clear.**

**The three most important reasons:**

1. **Spoiler discipline, the hard part of the brief.** T's later eras hold legends about earlier ones that are wrong: the treacherous Purser, the burned Book, Fen the Turncoat. Each era's real secret is first revealed in its own era: Annick's discharge and the drop in Chapter 19, Marsh and the cistern in Chapter 20. R's era 2 lets the reader deduce era 1's ending by Chapters 8 and 11: the Door is shut from Gallows, the Combine's officers are trapped as Stewards, and Pell becomes the First Auditor. That drains much of the suspense from Maren's climax eight to eleven chapters early.
2. **Craft and distinct eras.** T tells its three eras in three narrative modes (close third past, first-person confession from inside the well, present tense). Its characters are more morally complex: Annick trading Marit for Dace, and Tobiah's compromise. Its motifs are used with more discipline: the voice that "shakes" on one word, and the motto decaying on the vault door. R is vivid and moving, but its eras 1 and 2 share a voice, and it leans on a repeated pattern of noble self-sacrifice and on refrains that become rote.
3. **Tighter architecture and logic.** T's clues (Voyage III, D-0447, the Widow's knocking, the recovery port, Fen narrating from the well) all pay off without contradiction, and the three entries "Who were asleep / … / Who were awake" close the book on a single idea. R's relay is equally elegant in design, but it has gaps: the twelve Stewards set against a full returning crew and marines, a receiver that capitulates and hands over its own title, and a convenient Prelate's oath carrying Pell's name.

R's single clear advantage is the convergence of all three climaxes on one station, Gallows. It is superb, and it keeps the margin at "clear" rather than "strong".
