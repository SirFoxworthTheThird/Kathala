# Judgement: the first book (T) against the second book (P)

I read both books in full, every chapter, the first book (T) first.

The two novels are built in very similar ways. Each one:
- has three eras with different casts;
- rotates strictly through the eras, one chapter each in turn, for 21 chapters;
- uses a central object that passes from era to era (T: the purser's Book, key 0447 and Ballast Tank Four; P: the Pattern H "egg", the Share Plate and the comet called the Lamp);
- gives the later eras an origin myth that distorts the earlier era's truth;
- resolves its eras one after another, one chapter apart (earliest era in ch19, middle era in ch20, latest era in ch21).

Both are accomplished. The differences between them are real but narrow.

---

## The first book (T)

**Eras:** *The Crossing* CR 611 (Annick Vell, assistant purser of the colony ship *Merciful Increase*). *The Account* CR 929 (Hollis Fen, Assessor of the Office of the Account, first person). *The Salvage* CR 1317 (Yara Tull, a diver working the falling Hulk, present tense).

### 1. Interlacing; three distinct eras and casts: **10**
The book rotates strictly through Crossing, Account and Salvage from ch1 to ch21. Each era has its own cast, its own social order (Charter bond, the Houses' "Liberty Debt", the Paschal writ) and its own voice: close third person in the past tense, first person in the past tense, and third person in the present tense. They are woven into one story, not merely placed side by side:
- the Book, key 0447, Tank Four (the "Widow", the "Fourth Cistern"), the plaque of Ordinances, the strongroom/vault, the Ash Case and the coil-gang song recur in every era;
- each later era's institution is built on the earlier era's act. Quarry invents "the Founders" and "Liberty" in ch16, and Severine's fleeing copy of the Account (ch20) becomes the Paschal writ (ch3, ch9).

### 2. Synchronized pacing: **9**
The three arcs move in step:
- Inciting shocks come together: the courier (ch1), Ottilie's murder (ch2) and the bailiffs (ch3).
- The investigations deepen together: the conspiracy (ch4, ch7), the cipher and the Ostrava sale (ch5, ch8), and Fen's cache (ch6, ch9).
- Each protagonist betrays or is betrayed at the midpoint: Annick gives up Marit (ch10), Bastian and Marsh frame Hollis (ch11), and Mitya and Sabeth are exposed (ch12).
- The catastrophes come together: the fold to the Fork (ch13), the squadron's arrival (ch14) and Mitya's death (ch15).
- The climaxes run ch16–18 (the Fork rising, Burning Day, the vault) and resolve in a cascade: ch19 (the Book goes "down the sea"), ch20 (the lodging, and the well), ch21 (the broadcast).
- Countdown devices (days to the Fork, days to the Burning, perigee passes) keep the eras level.

The only deduction: the final resolutions are staggered by a chapter each rather than truly simultaneous. The staggering is deliberate, because each era's revelation has to land before the next era can use it.

### 3. No spoilers: **7**
The book's main device is a later era's myth that misstates an earlier era's truth. This mostly protects the secrets, but it does give away some outcomes early:
- **Ch2 (929) shows the outline of era 1's ending.** The Catechism says the *Increase* "carried us", "the Purser would have sold us… Annick Vell… written in ash", and "the Founders… burned the Book." Before the Crossing has properly set up its conflict (the Shale order arrives in ch4), the reader knows the ship reaches Absalom, the bonded go free of the Charter and Annick is branded a traitor. That drains much of the suspense from "Shale or Absalom?" The real secret (she was not a traitor, and the Book was never burned) stays hidden until ch19.
- **Ch3 and ch6 (1317) reveal the shape of era 2's ending.** "Fen's dead. Fen's in there somewhere, counting" and the skipping-rhyme about "Fen the Turncoat" on "the Night of the Hulk" are followed by "then the Dimming, and then [the Houses] were gone." Ch6 and ch9 also say the Account has gone unpaid for 388 years, which dates its end to 929. So the reader knows early that the Houses fall in 929 and that Hollis dies inside the Hulk. The "turncoat" part is false, and ch14 shows it was the Speaker's propaganda. The death is true.
- **Ch18 (1317) shows Marsh's death two chapters before era 2 reaches it.** Yara finds a white-haired corpse in the vault, wearing the Chief Assessor's chain, belted to the cradle beside the opened privy box. She takes it for Fen. An alert reader can see it is Marsh after his walk to the vault in ch17, and so knows how ch20 will go in part before ch20.

No later chapter gives away a mechanism, the Article 9 secret, the VOYAGE III substitution, Szell's guilt or Bastian's guilt before its own era reveals it.

### 4. Coherence and continuity: **9**
The plot holds together tightly, and the planted details pay off:
- the empty slot for VOYAGE III on the vault shelf (ch5, ch11, ch18) matches the burned box, whose plate reads "VOY. III" (ch21);
- Hollis's borrowed coat, "too tight across the shoulders, with a burn on one cuff" (ch17), is what identifies his body (ch21);
- the knocking in the Widow is heard in ch3, ch11 and ch13;
- the Clerk's rule that a writ falls to "a certified contrary record… of prior date or superior authority" (ch9) is exactly what defeats the writ (ch21).

Small problems:
- Annick's age arithmetic is loose (ch1).
- She invokes Article 9 ("not lawfully in command") against a Master she sealed herself, which is argued but strained.
- The slang "don't vell me" appears only in ch21 and was never planted.
- The Clerk's bead "hearing the old band" is set up only one chapter before it matters.
- An unexplained hand has torn the Account loose from its cradle between 929 and 1317.

### 5. Craft: **10**
The prose is precise, concrete and unhurried, and it never strains for effect. Rather than tell the reader what to feel, the book lets small repeated details carry the theme:
- the purser's voice that does or doesn't shake, ending in "Who were asleep" and then "Who were awake";
- "four in, hold, four out";
- the shadow ledger of the dead.

Characters are morally complicated. Annick buys her sister's life with Marit Osei's. Tobiah knowingly accepts a murderer as pilot and a new debt. Quarry, Szell and Marsh are articulate rather than villainous ("every ledger is a forgery… the only question is who holds the pen"). Hollis carries 309 strikes. Yara burns through a man's helmet seal and leaves Mitya to die.

Standout scenes:
- Marit at the lock, "Write it truly, Purser" (ch10);
- the door overheard at the Fork (ch16);
- the reading in the Hold (ch19);
- the narration of ch20, which turns out to be Hollis speaking from the sealed well;
- the Clerk's ruling (ch21).

The ending pays off almost every planted detail without seeming mechanical, and closes on a quiet, earned line ("nobody, anywhere, is counting").

### 6. Overall: **9**

## The second book (P)

**Eras:** *The Bonded* 2391 (Maren Tsolo, tether-chief of the gas-skimmer *Patience*). *The Siege* 2654 (Isaura Venn, air-warden of the city of Patience, first person, present tense). *The Last Return* 2917 (Til Brask, salvage-tug captain, present tense).

### 1. Interlacing; three distinct eras and casts: **10**
The book rotates strictly from ch1 to ch21. Each era has a distinct cast, society and voice: a bonded skimmer crew, a stratified city under siege, and a decaying debt-house frontier. The comet that returns every 263 years is an elegant device: it physically links the eras and explains why each one meets the same object. The Plate, the drum of names, the seed-with-a-tail drawing, the line knife, the saying "the Lamp takes the dead out" and the phrase "cut the line" all recur.

### 2. Synchronized pacing: **9**
The structure matches the first book closely:
- the eras open together: Lusine's fall (ch1), the siege (ch2) and the commission (ch3);
- the weapon is discovered across ch7, ch8 and ch11–12;
- the pivots come together: the seizure (ch10), the "measures" vote (ch11) and the register (ch12);
- the climaxes run ch16–18: the throw, the Lamp landing, the comet passing inside the Roche limit;
- the resolutions cascade across ch19, ch20 and ch21.

Each era is under real time pressure: the *Admonition*'s arrival, the air-reserve board, and the comet breaking up. The deduction is the same as for the first book: the endings are staggered one chapter apart.

### 3. No spoilers: **8**
P uses the same device, and some outcomes are given away early:
- **Ch2 (2654) shows era 1's ending in outline.** "We bought our air", "the year of the Purchase" and the Company's share being "struck off" tell the reader the bonded win the ship before era 1's crisis has begun. The device is turned, as in the first book: it was a seizure, and the blank line is Maren's name, gouged off (ch19).
- **Ch6 and ch12 (2917) reveal the egg plot's outcome early.** Vey's register says "Asset 7… seized by bonded mutineers… not recovered", and ch8 and ch11 (2654) have the Concord searching for a lost Company item. Before Maren decides at the end of ch7 to "take the egg", the reader already knows the bonded will seize it and the Combine will never get it back.
- **Ch14 (2654): the Tether-Woman legend gives away the outline of ch16.** The tether-chief "went out on the Lamp" in her coat. It turns out to be a misreading (Abel went in Maren's coat), but it gives away that someone named Tsolo rides the comet.
- **Ch6 (2917) hints at era 2's ending.** The lane has been silent for 200 years, the anchor station is debris, and Oz says "it was a city once". Ch18 shows a second anchor at the shaft. Both hint at the quarantine and at an era-2 return visit before ch20. The hints are mild and ambiguous.

The false legends take the edge off these, as in the first book. Overall the giveaways are a little gentler than the first book's, because nothing in a later era states what becomes of Isaura or Maren before her own chapters do.

### 4. Coherence and continuity: **9**
Causes and effects line up well across the centuries:
- Maren cuts the orbit, the location and "FORGIVE ABEL" into the Plate and paints them over (ch19). Isaura uncovers that inscription (ch8, ch14, ch17, ch20).
- Isaura adds "SENT ROUND AGAIN 2654" and leaves the Plate and her recorder in Abel's hands (ch20), and that is exactly what Til finds (ch21).
- Grieve's destruction of the anchor station (ch20) explains the dead lane and the silent radio of ch6.
- The rules of the weapon (it wakes above 40 K; only the depths of the gas giant can kill it) are set up in ch10 and drive the solution in ch21.

Small problems:
- Maren reconstructs six orbital elements from memory.
- The cryostat survives days on the sunlit surface in 2654 without warming.
- The tightbeam (laser-link) and harpoon set-piece in era 3 is convenient.
- Ama Ocampo says "we've always known" it was coming back, which is plausible but only asserted.

### 5. Craft: **9**
The prose is excellent: vivid, physical and emotionally exact. The tether sequence in ch1 is a superb opening. Strong scenes:
- Quist hands over the key (his roses speech, ch10);
- Abel drugs Maren and takes her coat (ch16);
- Mattias: "I won't conserve" (ch20);
- the valves turned to the stop and Pilar's "Breathe" (ch20);
- the lamps coming on across Kettle at the end.

Weaknesses compared with the first book:
- **The central moral test is run three times.** In each era someone wants to use the egg (Hale, Corin, Marchetti and the Directorate), someone refuses, and someone goes off into the dark (Lusine, Corin, Marchetti).
- **The motifs are pressed hard.** "Cut the line", "that's what you do", "I'm witnessing" and "pretend it's me" come back so often that they start to feel engineered.
- **The villains are flatter.** Hale and Lucan Strand are smoothly reasonable but one-note.
- **Era 3 is the weakest third.** Szabo and Marchetti are stock figures, and the harpoon gambit is action-movie mechanics.
- **Ch21 repeats material the reader already has.** It replays Abel's recording, the Plate text, and Til's Harrow confession almost word for word from ch15, before it moves on to new action.

The ending is warm and earned, but it is more sentimental and less surprising than the first book's.

### 6. Overall: **8**

---

## Verdict

**The first book (T) is better overall. Strength of preference: clear.** The margin is narrow. Both books are of a high standard, and the second book is slightly cleaner on spoilers.

**The three most important reasons:**

1. **A tighter causal braid.** In T a single legal object and its rules (Articles 1, 9, 11 and 20) govern all three eras. Annick's entry of 611 and Hollis's entry of 929 are literally the evidence that defeats the writ of 1317: it is "prior in date… of superior authority" and "identifies the copy". Each era's climax is mechanically necessary to the next, and the solution is surprising yet fully planted (VOYAGE III, the recovery port, key 0447). In P the earlier eras mainly tell era 3 *where* the weapon is and *what* it is, and era 3 solves the problem by its own means (dropping the egg into the north lobe as it falls).

2. **Deeper moral complexity and characterisation.** T has no clean heroes. Annick trades Marit for Dace, Tobiah agrees to "Liberty" knowing who killed Benn, Hollis owns his 309 strikes, and Yara leaves Mitya. Its antagonists argue a coherent philosophy of debt and record. P has very fine individual scenes, but it stages the same moral choice three times (use the weapon or send it round), with more clear-cut villains and more insistent motifs.

3. **Less repetition, and a stronger third era.** T's final chapter turns the replayed voices into the climax itself (the broadcast and the Clerk's ruling) and adds new turns. P's last chapters re-deliver material the reader already has (Abel's recording, the Plate text, Til's confession almost verbatim), and its era 3 rests on stock antagonists and a convenient action set-piece. Against this, T's legends give away more early: Fen dies in the Hulk, the Houses fall in 929, and Marsh's corpse is seen in ch18. That is the main point in P's favour.
