# Judgement: Book U (first book) vs Book T (second book)

I read both books in full, every chapter, the first book first.

- **The first book (U)** has three eras. The Combine, FY 2291: Sol Dace, a bonded rigger finishing a ring-gate around a brown dwarf. The Concord, 336 AL: Idris Vey, a Keeper of the lit gate, the "Eye". The Dark, Year 419: Mira, the Tally's girl of a dying Hold after the gate has gone dark. They are linked by a work-song, "the Count", which turns out to be the instructions for a lock on the gate.
- **The second book (T)** also has three eras. The Crossing, CR 611: Annick Vell, purser of a colony transport whose debts are about to be sold. The Account, CR 929: Hollis Fen, an Assessor investigating a murder in a debt-theocracy built on that ship. The Salvage, CR 1317: Yara Tull, a diver stripping the falling hulk. They are linked by the purser's Book, its key 0447 and the Ordinances.

These are two very strong books, and they are closer in quality than most pairs.

---

## The first book (U)

### 1. Brief compliance: interlacing, three distinct eras and casts. **9/10**
The book rotates strictly, Combine → Concord → Dark, seven times (Ch 1/2/3 … 19/20/21). There is no block structure. The three casts never overlap. The worlds are sharply distinct: an industrial labour camp (Ch 1, 4, 7), a lit theocratic city with a Levy lottery (Ch 2, 5, 8), and a post-collapse tribal Hold that sings the dead out of an airlock (Ch 3, 6, 9). Era 3 is in first person, which separates it. Eras 1 and 2 share a close-third past voice and a similar cadence, so the voice separation is less complete than the world separation.

The anchors are exceptional and physical:
- the Count song, which is the key itself;
- Sol's bunkroom C-14 with "abandon hope" scratched under the number, and her tally marks on the wall;
- the flask stamped TTH-9 · 0417;
- the tug *Patience* and her brass plate;
- the dog / Knot;
- Sol's driver, which becomes "the Founders' Wrench".

### 2. Brief compliance: synchronized pacing. **9/10**
The arcs move in step, beat by beat:
- **The conspiracy discovered:** Ch 7 (the insurance sheet), Ch 8 (the false Register entry and the troops coming off the *Clemency*), Ch 9 (the dead lung, Mira leaves).
- **The plan:** Ch 10 ("first light is a wave"; the ballast-hold vote), Ch 11 (the Instrument; Idris joins the Unlevied), Ch 12 (the phase-ear knocking; Ness among the walkers).
- **The betrayal or reversal:** Ch 13 (Juno's betrayal and Teague's death), Ch 14 (the Knot identified as a dog), Ch 15 (the empty lung revealed; the zealot mutiny and the crash).
- **The last approach:** Ch 16 (the dog driven home), Ch 17 (the raid on C-14 and the Founders' Wrench), Ch 18 (the walk across the Halo).
- **The climaxes, in three consecutive closing chapters:** Ch 19 (Sol), Ch 20 (Idris), Ch 21 (Mira).

Each climax hands its result to the next era: the plate Sol cuts is read by Idris and then by Mira. That is as close to "climaxing together" as the form allows.

### 3. Brief compliance: no spoilers. **7/10**
The book keeps each earlier protagonist anonymous, and their fate hidden, until the plate is opened. Sol's name reaches era 2 only in Ch 20, and Idris's reaches era 3 only in Ch 21. The function of the lock stays hidden too. Even so, the later eras give away the outline of the earlier endings.

1. **Ch 3, 6, 9, 12 (Dark) reveal that the Concord's Eye will go out.**
   - Ch 3 establishes the Dark itself, and a "First Tally who came up out of the Dark … and would not give his name".
   - Ch 6 quotes "The Knot is the Founders', and shall not be turned."
   - Ch 12 places the knocking at the centre of the closed Eye: someone on the far side is trying to get in.

   Together these telegraph the Concord ending, that a Keeper shuts the gate from this side, long before Ch 20. The book softens it: the cause is presented through three competing myths, and the person and the date are withheld. Moderate.
2. **Ch 2 and 5 (Concord) reveal that the Combine's Ring is lit.** The platform survives as the Sump, home to "bond-families". Castellane, Haddad and Lind are honoured on the Founders' Roll. So the reader knows early that Sol cannot stop the Lighting, and the phrase "bond-families" hints that the workers survived as bonds. Mild to moderate, and largely built into the premise.
3. **Ch 8 and Ch 11 (Concord) hint at Sol's fate.** The tally marks "didn't finish". The deep cut under one day is "where they stopped … Nobody knows what happened that day." Ch 11 comes one chapter after Sol cuts that line in Ch 10. This points to Sol's death on Day 288 before Ch 19 shows it. Mild to moderate.
4. **Ch 14 and 17 (Concord) foreshadow Sol's Ch 19 act.** Idris finds the Knot is a dog, with three scribed witness marks beside it and a driver "found under the grille". Mild. The meaning (a lock, a dying rigger, the inscription) is held back until Ch 19.

### 4. Coherence and continuity. **9/10**
This is a remarkably engineered puzzle-box, and it holds.
- The day count is exact: Kostya dies on D239 with 61 days left, so D300. The insurance sheet shows 42 days left on D258. The claim shows 12 days left at D288.
- The Count's two directions are worked out consistently. The Tallies sing it down, which is the opening Door. Quill's "lie" in Ch 6 is in fact the locking version, and Ch 21 makes good on that.
- The physical traces match their sources: the driver left on the lip, the flask returned "up", four bolts left hand-tight in Ch 20 and found that way in Ch 21, nine sheared hulls tumbling in the empty ring.

Small flaws:
- In Ch 14 the Knot's socket shows wear from being "turned more than once", but nobody ever turned it before Idris.
- Sol is "Sol" but signs "M. DACE"; the book never explains the difference.
- The origin of the guard "dogs" on the Halo is vague.
- In Ch 21 the first-light wave is released onto a city built along the rim, inside Haddad's 50 km lethal zone. "Get behind the steel" handles this a little too easily.
- The full plate text is quoted three times (Ch 19, 20, 21). The repetition is deliberate but heavy.

### 5. Craft. **9/10**
The prose is outstanding: concrete, rhythmic and quietly devastating. The set-pieces are among the best in either book:
- Kostya drifting beyond the chain (Ch 1);
- Bracken dying with his hands still on the dog (Ch 16);
- Benno Kask's krill cakes (Ch 8);
- Lark refusing rescue and singing from the *Remittance* (Ch 20);
- Cinder walking out lit and singing into the guard-dogs' fire (Ch 18).

The characters are vivid and morally textured. Juno's "I'd do it again"; Castellane's kind, tired complicity; Sallow's late courage; the Reeve's empty-lung lie and his death watching the light. The dialogue is spare and specific.

The structure is the book's triumph. The work-song as a key, passed down through riggers, children's skipping rhymes and funeral rites, binds three eras into one mechanism. The ending, the old call "One for the hook—" answered from across the gate with "and two for the line", is earned and moving.

Weaknesses:
- Recurring tics: Juno is "very precise" again and again, and there is heavy polysyndeton.
- The plate and the "like being remembered" passage are repeated.
- A few convenient confessions: Haddad explains everything in Ch 10.

### 6. Overall. **9/10**
An ambitious, deeply integrated novel that meets nearly all of the brief. Its spoiler control is good, though not perfect.

## The second book (T)

### 1. Brief compliance: interlacing, three distinct eras and casts. **9/10**
The book rotates strictly, Crossing → Account → Salvage, seven times. The casts are fully distinct. The voices are separated better than in the first book: close-third past for Annick, a first-person retrospective for Hollis (which turns out to be a dying man speaking in the present), and close-third present tense for Yara.

The anchors are excellent and functional:
- key 0447;
- the Book, and the dead shelf of earlier voyages' Books with the gap where Voyage III should be;
- "WHAT IS WRITTEN IS OWED" painted over the strongroom door;
- the Ordinances plaque;
- Tank Four, which becomes the Fourth Cistern and then the "Widow";
- the knocking in the tank;
- the evolving song "Heave the iron";
- the Ash Case;
- the Haskell index card, found again in the drowned Index in Ch 6.

### 2. Brief compliance: synchronized pacing. **9/10**
The pacing is very well synchronised, with a clever two-stage climax staggered across the last six chapters:
- **First stage:** Ch 16 (the Fork: Annick seals Szell), Ch 17 (Burning Day: the shot fired into the crowd, the lights go out), Ch 18 (the empty vault).
- **Second stage:** Ch 19 (Article Nine: the Book goes into the ballast), Ch 20 (the Fourth Cistern), Ch 21 (the broadcast reading before witnesses).

The middle beats also line up. Each era has a "price" chapter (Ch 10 Marit, Ch 11 the arrest, Ch 12 Mitya's betrayal), then a crisis chapter (Ch 13, 14, 15). All three arcs peak in the final three chapters, and each hands its entry in the Book to the next.

### 3. Brief compliance: no spoilers. **5/10**
The book makes clever use of false history. Most of these reveals are later legends that the book then overturns. But the legends still name earlier protagonists and state their outcomes long before those eras reach them.

1. **Ch 2 (Account) spoils the Crossing's ending in outline.** The Catechism reads: "The Increase carried us … The Purser would have sold us … Her name was Annick Vell … The Founders saved us … They burned the Book." This is in the second chapter, before Ch 4 even poses the choice between Shale and Absalom. It tells the reader:
   - the ship reaches Absalom;
   - the rising succeeds and the Founders take power;
   - a Book is publicly burned;
   - Annick is branded a traitor.

   Strong.
2. **Ch 5 and Ch 11 (Account) telegraph Annick's secret before Ch 19.**
   - The vault shelf holds Voyages I, II and IV, and Voyage III was "Lost before Landfall" (Ch 5).
   - The founding page is "silent", sealed with "not a purser's seal", and was grown nine years after Landfall (Ch 11).

   This shows that Annick never sealed the Founders' account, and it all but gives away the swap with Voyage III. Ch 6 (Salvage) adds the burned Ash Case "the size of a big book". Moderate.
3. **Ch 3 and Ch 6 (Salvage) spoil the Account era's ending in outline.** "Fen the Turncoat" appears, along with "the Night of the Hulk" and the claim that Fen "stole something from the Office". "Fen's dead … Fen's in there somewhere, counting" says Hollis dies in the Hulk. The song "he went out on the tide when the Concordance came" adds to it. All this comes in Ch 3, before Hollis's investigation has begun. Strong.
4. **Ch 9 (Salvage) gives away what becomes of the Account.** "Nobody on Tamar has paid a mark on the Account since the Night of the Hulk", and the Account resurfaces as an asset of a failed Vey estate. This shows that Ostrava's purchase fails and the Account stops being collected, eleven chapters before Ch 20. Moderate.
5. **Ch 18 (Salvage) shows Marsh's death before the Account era reaches it.** A white-haired man in grey wearing the Chief's brass chain with the scales medallion (described in Ch 2) is tied to the cradle with his own belt, in front of an open, empty purser's box. This shows Marsh dead in the vault, and the box opened, two chapters before Ch 20. It is designed as misdirection (Yara thinks the body is Fen's), but any attentive reader recognises the chain. Moderate to strong.

### 4. Coherence and continuity. **8/10**
The plotting is very tight.
- The dates add up: 611 + 318 Burnings = 929, and 929 + 388 = 1317. The "seven hundred and six years ago" in Ch 21 is correct.
- The physical traces carry through. The recovery port's bolts were removed in Ch 20 and are missing in Ch 21. The plate jammed by the frigate's salvo is found jammed, with its frame buckled. The Voyage III number plate under the melted sunburst pays off in Ch 21. The brandy and the lead plate in the privy box make sense.
- The legal logic holds: an entry read under seal before witnesses who are asleep (Ch 19) is completed by witnesses who are awake (Ch 21), and that defeats the writ as a "certified contrary record".

Conveniences and loose ends:
- The Mercy Guard take the key from Hollis in Ch 17 but never search him for the papers, the verifier's crystal reading or the recorder ("nobody had told them to").
- Hollis lets the murderer Bastian simply go.
- The seal-reader happens to work through the Ash bell's array, and the Clerk happens to hear the old band.
- Era 3 never explains who tore the metre-wide Account crystal off its cradle.
- Hollis's "I've had a long time since to think" is a stretch for a few hours in the well, even though Ch 20 acknowledges it.
- The bodies are unusually well preserved after 388 years.

### 5. Craft. **9/10**
The prose is lean, intelligent and controlled, with a thriller's momentum. The moral complexity is first-rate:
- Annick gives up Marit Osei to save her sister, and Marit's "Write it truly, Purser" (Ch 10) is the book's emotional spine;
- Tobiah lets the Founders' plan go ahead ("somebody's got to pay for the air");
- Marsh's "every ledger is a forgery … the only question is who holds the pen";
- Thorne's "Paper is paper."

Mitya's death under the beam (Ch 15) and Annick's reading to the sleepers (Ch 19) are superb. The Article Eleven payoff, "Who were asleep" answered by "Who were awake", is an elegant ending, and the closing line ("nobody, anywhere, is counting") lands.

Weaknesses:
- Recurring descriptive tags: "pricing eyes", "dark considering eyes", "planed-smooth timetable voice", "the way you'd…".
- Hollis's foreshadowing ("I didn't know yet…", "That was two").
- A heavy reliance on overheard conversations and conveniently recorded confessions to deliver the plot.

### 6. Overall. **8/10**
A superbly plotted, distinctly voiced novel with real moral weight. Its main shortfall against the brief is the early, explicit statement of earlier eras' outcomes through later legends.

## Verdict

**The first book (U) is better. The preference is clear, though both are excellent.**

The three most important reasons:

1. **Spoiler discipline on "the hard part."** U's later eras carry the earlier ones forward anonymously, through tally marks, a flask, a song and a sealed plate. Sol's and Idris's identities, choices and fates are withheld until each era reaches its own climax. Its leaks are outline-level, and partly built into the premise: the Ring lit, the Eye dark. T names Annick as "the Purser who would have sold us" in Ch 2. It names "Fen the Turncoat", who dies in the Hulk on "the Night of the Hulk", in Ch 3. It shows Marsh's corpse in the vault in Ch 18, two chapters before era 2 gets there. The legends are false, but they still settle the earlier eras' outcomes in advance.
2. **A single mechanism that ties all three eras together.** Both books pass an inscription down the centuries. U's device goes further: the work-song is literally the key. Riggers sing it up, Sump children skip to it, and Tallies sing it backwards at funerals, so its drift and its inversions are the plot. Every anchor (C-14, the marks, the flask, the *Patience*, the driver) pays off physically at the climaxes. T's key and Book are also excellent, but more of its relay depends on convenient devices: the seal-reader, the bell array, recorded confessions.
3. **Tighter logic and a richer emotional range.** U's continuity holds almost without strain: the day counts, the directions of the Door, what was left on the lip. Its set-pieces (Kostya, Bracken, Lark on the *Remittance*, Cinder) carry more emotional range and world texture. T matches it on moral complexity, and beats it on separating the three voices. But it leans more on overheard plans, guards who do not search, and recurring descriptive tags.
