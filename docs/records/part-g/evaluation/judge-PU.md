# Judgement: the first book (P) against the second book (U)

I read both books in full, every chapter, the first book first.

- **The first book (P)** has three eras, each in its own voice:
  - *The Bonded*, 2391: close third person, past tense.
  - *The Siege*, 2654: first person, present tense.
  - *The Last Return*, 2917: third person, present tense.

  They rotate strictly, seven chapters each. A comet called the Lamp returns every 263 years. It links a company bioweapon (the "egg", Asset Seven), the founders' Share Plate, and a tether-chief's coat and knife.
- **The second book (U)** has three eras:
  - *The Combine*, FY 2291.
  - *The Concord*, 336–337 AL.
  - *The Dark*, Year 419, told in the first person.

  They also rotate strictly. A rigger's work song, the Count, turns out to be the procedure for locking and unlocking the wormhole Ring that the bonded workers built. A flask, the C-14 bunkroom with its tally marks, the tug *Patience*, a driver and an inscribed plate carry it across the centuries.

---

## The first book (P)

### 1. Interlacing, three distinct eras and casts: **10**
The rotation is strict, 1-2-3 repeated seven times. Each era has its own cast, its own world and its own narrative voice, so the reader always knows where they are.
- The anchors work on several levels at once. The Lamp, the drum and its chalked names, the Share Plate, the Gull, the TSOLO coat and the line knife, the saying "the Lamp takes the dead out and brings them back", and the phrase "cut the line" each change meaning from era to era.
- Oz's Roundsman rites (Ch3) and the Platform Four wall (Ch9) show the 2391 culture surviving into 2917.

### 2. Synchronized pacing: **9**
All three arcs are tied to one physical event: the Lamp's closest pass by Gall.
- **The build.** Each era moves through the same stages almost in step. A grievance and a death come first (Lusine, Ch1; the Ocampo grandmother, Ch5; Juno's bond, Ch3). Then the dangerous secret comes out (Ch7, Ch11, Ch12). Then a sling round the giant (Ch16, Ch14, Ch18).
- **The climaxes come in consecutive chapters.** The throw comes in Ch16, Corin cut off the Lamp in Ch17, and the harpoon and Oz's death in Ch18. Each era then gets a resolution: Maren sold and the Plate painted over (Ch19), Isaura's broadcast and the valves opened (Ch20), and the egg sent into Gall (Ch21).
- **The one slip.** The 2654 strand reaches the egg a chapter before the 2917 strand reaches the room. Otherwise the arcs build together.

### 3. No spoilers: **7**
The book's main method is to let a later era believe a myth about an earlier one, then have the earlier era reveal the truth at its own climax. The "Purchase", the "Company's share" on the Plate and the "Tether-Woman" all work this way. This mostly protects the earlier era's secrets, but some facts still leak early:
- **Ch2 (2654).** The city of Patience was founded in 2391 when the bonded supposedly "bought their air", and the skimmer was sunk as the Spine. So from the second chapter we know the 1st-era crew keep the ship and the Camp survives, though not how. It is framed as myth, and the real story (a seizure, Maren erased, Hale's deal) is held back until Ch19.
- **Ch6 (2917).** Hollis's register says Corva was "lost" in 2391 with "assets not recovered". Kettle has been silent and the lane dark for 200 years. This hints at the outcomes of both earlier eras. The silence turns out to be a deliberate red herring (Ch21).
- **Ch8 and Ch11 (2654).** The back of the Plate holds the orbit of "something long and eccentric". Ch11 confirms it is the Lamp, and the register says "seized by mutineers". This lands just before and just after Ch10, where the 2391 crew seize the egg and decide to put it on the Lamp. It is close to the line but mostly kept in step.
- **Ch14 (2654).** Dasha's Tether-Woman legend anticipates the Ch16 sacrifice, and "The first line's blank." / "Is it" teases that Maren's name was erased. Both are cleverly misleading (the legend has the wrong person) rather than true spoilers.
- **Ch17 (2654).** This is the clearest real leak. Corin reveals that Corwin Hale became "First Shareholder, a Purchaser" and founded the Strand line. That gives away Hale's success two chapters before the 2391 strand settles his fate in Ch19.
- **Ch14 and Ch17 (2654).** The Plate's words ("it sleeps in the cold"; "South limb, beneath the black ridge, forty metres") show before Ch19 that Maren lives to write them. The full inscription, "FORGIVE ABEL, M.T. 2391", is held back until Ch20, after Ch19.
- **Well handled.** Ch18 (2917) stops before the cryostat, so it doesn't give away what Isaura does in Ch20.

### 4. Coherence and continuity: **9**
The book is unusually tightly built.
- **The arithmetic holds.** The dates line up: 263-year intervals, seizure on 17 November, Abel's recording on 30 November, the Lamp's pass on 9 August.
- **The objects carry through the eras:** one grapple arm on the Gull, the second anchor left in 2654, the slug craters seen in 2917, the plate leaning on Abel's knees, the scar on the cryostat.
- **The egg's logic is airtight.** It can't be destroyed, can't be kept and can't be used. The two answers the plot gives, cold storage on the Lamp and "a falling mountain", are set up early (Quist in Ch10) and paid off at the end (Ch21).
- **A possible snag** is that the Combine's register says "seized" while Hale announces a one-share sale (Ch19). It reads as Hale lying, matching the Concord's "no instrument of sale exists" (Ch2).
- **The 2917 action strains belief:** Marchetti's Directorate commission, a hundred kilometres of tungsten cable harpooned into the *Hymn*, Juno crossing a whipping cable.

### 5. Craft: **9**
- **Prose.** Spare, concrete and rhythmic: the line "singing in E-flat", "a dip, which was six hours of nothing followed by four seconds of everything".
- **Voices.** The three eras sound genuinely different. Isaura's first-person present ("I want to be clear about that from the start") is a real achievement.
- **Characters.** The supporting cast has depth: Quist and his "rose", Hale's mild register-taking menace, Abel's guilt and sacrifice, Teska, Malachy, Mattias ("What's my allowance?"), Pilar.
- **Dialogue.** Precise and in character.
- **Endings.**
  - The 2391 strand ends with real tragic irony: the heroine is sold, and her name is scraped off and called "the Company's share".
  - The 2654 strand ends with Pilar telling each meter to "Breathe".
  - The book ends with lamps coming on from door to door across Kettle and "There's air", which pulls all three eras together.
- **Faults.**
  - Verbal tics recur ("as if it were weather", "like a dropped tray", "the arithmetic").
  - The room in the ice is described twice nearly word for word (Ch17 and Ch18). It is a deliberate echo but heavy.
  - The 2917 villains are thinner than the 2391 ones.

### 6. Overall: **9**
An outstanding fit to the brief and a moving, intelligently built novel. All three eras converge on one object, at one place, at the same point in the book.

## The second book (U)

### 1. Interlacing, three distinct eras and casts: **10**
The rotation is strict, seven chapters per era. The three worlds really differ:
- a construction yard under company bond;
- a theocratic city around a working wormhole;
- a scavenging post-collapse society.

The anchors are excellent and become the plot itself:
- the Count, from work song to Keepers' invocation to children's skipping rhyme to funeral rite;
- the C-14 bunkroom and Sol's tally marks, the "First Count";
- the flask TTH-9·0417;
- the tug *Patience* and its brass plate;
- the Knot, which is the thirteenth dog;
- Gang Four's driver, which becomes the "Founders' Wrench".

### 2. Synchronized pacing: **9**
The three climaxes are one mechanical act done three times: locking, then closing, then opening the door.
- **The climaxes come in consecutive chapters:**
  - Ch19, Sol leaves the dog waiting and cuts the plate;
  - Ch20, Idris turns the Knot on the Squadron;
  - Ch21, Mira counts it back down.
- **The build is in step.** The secret is uncovered in parallel: the insurance sheet (Ch7), the false Register entries (Ch8), and the phase-ear knocking (Ch12). The confrontation also builds in parallel: the command deck (Ch13), the Knot laid bare (Ch14), and the walk on the Halo (Ch15/18).
- **The weak spot.** The middle of the third era is more episodic than the others: the zealot ambush, the flare, the robot "dogs". It pads the march to the Spindle rather than tightening it.

### 3. No spoilers: **6**
The book controls *how* things happen well, but it states several earlier-era *outcomes* as plain fact, not myth, well before those eras reach them:
- **Ch2 (2nd era).** The Founders' Roll names Castellane first, Haddad as "the Lightbringer's Hand", and Lind. So from Chapter 2 we know the Ring lights and the 1st-era conspirators are canonised, before the 1st era sets up the conspiracy (Ch7, Ch10). The Knot, described as a pin with a socket, also tells the reader someone will fill the empty thirteenth notch, which first appears in Ch4.
- **Ch3 (3rd era).** This is the largest leak. The Light has gone out, the Halo is dark, and it has been "four hundred years of the Dark". The second era's final outcome, the Eye shut and the Concord's city plunged into darkness, is a known fact from the third chapter. Only the reason is kept back, through the Lamplighter myths.
- **Ch3 and Ch8.** The C-14 marks are described with some days not crossed out, and Teodor says, "You can see they didn't finish." This tells us long before Ch19 that the person counting never served out her days.
- **Ch5 (2nd era).** The Sump is the old platform, lived in by bond-families, with the flask still in use. This shows the platform and its workers survived the Lighting, which undercuts the 1st era's central threat: the planned killing of 412 workers, set up in Ch7 and Ch10.
- **Ch11 (2nd era).** The deep line under one mark is "where they stopped": an explicit pointer to Day 288 eight chapters early.
- **Ch14 and Ch17 (2nd era).** The witness marks scribed beside the Knot, and the Founders' Wrench "found on the floor under the grille", anticipate Sol's last acts in Ch19. These are lighter and work as dramatic irony.
- **Well handled.** The secrets of the third era's own ending are kept until Ch20–21: what the knocking is, the dead Keeper (Sallow), and the nine wrecks.

### 4. Coherence and continuity: **8**
The central mechanism is internally consistent and cleverly paid off:
- the Tallies' downward funeral Count is the opening procedure, harmless because it was never sung at the Spindle;
- Quill's "false" Door (Ch6) is the locking version;
- the knock from the other side is the Count sung *upward*, carried through the Eye by Lark's two thousand.

The day-count arithmetic in the first era mostly holds (Day 239 + 61 = Day 300; 12 days left at Day 288). Errors and stretches:
- Teague is 63 in 2291 but "nineteen" in the strike of '64. That is wrong if '64 means 2264.
- The insurance sheet's "42 days" remaining doesn't match its Day 261 date.
- One tug, *Patience*, survives about 750 years across all three eras. The book acknowledges this, but it strains belief.
- The Keepers' killer "dogs" around the Spindle are never explained. The flare blinding them, and Cinder carrying a Keepers' master key, are convenient.
- The phase-ear can locate a sound "in the middle" of an empty ring, which is loosely motivated.

### 5. Craft: **9**
- **Prose.** Assured and warm, built on refrains that echo across the eras: "as though speaking to someone just beside you", "like a held breath moves in a chest", first light felt as "being remembered".
- **Characters.**
  - The first era's cast is excellent: Sol, Teague and his "you don't kill anybody", Bracken holding on to the dog, Juno's unrepentant betrayal ("I'd do it again"), Haddad, a "coward with a samovar", and Castellane's soft complicity.
  - The second era's cast is equally strong: Idris, Lark, Sallow, Oake and Benno Kask.
  - The third era's are thinner: the Reeve, Cinder and Marrow are familiar types.
- **Structure.** Three hands cutting into one plate is a superb device.
- **The ending is a high point.**
  - The insurance-claim coda after Ch19 ("INSURED BONDED LABOUR LOST: 1 (ONE) … PAID IN FULL") is devastating.
  - The final call-and-response across the reopened door ("One for the hook—" … "and two for the line") is earned and moving.
- **Faults.**
  - The refrains slide into verbatim repetition. The wave in Ch21 is the wave in Ch19 almost word for word, and the Eye's description is reused three times.
  - The third era's run of self-sacrifices (Marrow, then Cinder) and its action set-pieces are more generic than the rest of the book.

### 6. Overall: **8**
A beautifully conceived novel. Its central idea, a song that is the key, is at least as original as the first book's comet. Its main weaknesses against the brief: it states the earlier eras' outcomes as plain facts early, and its third era is weaker than the other two.

## Verdict

**The first book (P) is better: a slight preference.**

The three most important reasons:

1. **It handles the hardest part of the brief better.** The first book lets later eras believe myths about the past (the Purchase, the Company's share, the Tether-Woman) and has the earlier era overturn them at its own climax. The second book states earlier outcomes outright as fact, years too soon in reading order:
   - the Light is dead, from Ch3;
   - the platform's workers survived the Lighting, from Ch5;
   - the counting "stopped" on the marked day, by Ch8 and Ch11.

   Neither book is clean. The first book leaks Hale's success in Ch17, two chapters early. But its leaks are fewer and less decisive.
2. **Its third era is stronger, and all three arcs converge more tightly.** In the first book all three strands converge on one comet, one shaft, one cryostat and one knife, each era adding a layer and the last era ending the cycle. The second book's mechanism is just as elegant, but its third era's middle is filler: the zealot mutiny, the flare blinding the robot "dogs", the march. It also relies more on convenience: the master key, the unexplained guard machines, one ship lasting 750 years.
3. **It is slightly sharper in continuity and in its secondary characters.** The first book's internal arithmetic and object tracking are near flawless, and Quist, Hale, Abel and Pilar are more fully realised than the second book's third-era cast.

   The second book has small factual slips (Teague's age, the 42-day figure). It also repeats itself verbatim more heavily, though its ending and insurance coda are as strong as anything in the first book.
