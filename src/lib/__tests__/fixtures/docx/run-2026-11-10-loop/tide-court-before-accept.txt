The Tide Court sat at noon the next day, the eighth of Thawmonth, in the old hall under Saltmarket Cross, which had been a fish market before it was a court and still smelled faintly of it in wet weather. It was not wet weather. It was the first bright day since autumn, a hard blue sky and a wind off the sea that sent the gulls sideways and rattled every shutter in the town, and the hall was full to the doors.

Tamsin had not known so many people would come. She stood at the witnesses' table with what she had brought laid out in front of her: the Crown's copy sheets for the fortnight, sealed at the springs of the twenty-second and broken by her own thumb; a brown calf tide book with the year stamped on its spine in gold; and the ledger, the second book, with the sacking still on it and the smell of the drowned chapel. She had swum the nave for it herself at the noon low water the day before, with Iska holding the lantern on the gallery, and the stone saint had given it up as if he had been waiting to be asked. Iska stood beside her now. She had not changed out of her Guild black, and she held herself very straight.

Justiciar Pell sat on the bench under the Margravine's arms. He was a thin, dry, middle-aged man who had come down from Hesk the year before and was said to dislike Quell, its smell, its tides and its Guild, in roughly that order. He had read the ledger already. He had sat with it for two hours that morning in the robing room, and he had come out looking like a man who had bitten something bad.

Mother Agathe Sorrel sat at the other table in her high-backed chair, which four Guild men had carried down from Sorrel House, with a rug over her knees and a lawyer from Hesk at her elbow whom she had not once let speak. She did not look at her daughter.

Tamsin had expected it to be quick. It was not.

"My lord," said Agathe, when Pell asked her to answer, "you have in front of you a book in a hand like mine, brought to you by a girl who ran from my house in the night, and who has wanted to be anything but my daughter since she was twelve years old. You have some loose sheets from the Exchequer cellar, copied by an old man who is half blind, which do not say a thing that the register does say. And you have the tide book of a priest who, God rest him, set his own tower on fire with his own lamp. That is what you have." She turned her good eye on the room, slowly, the way she had turned it on Tamsin in the room above Guild Row. "Sorrel House has carried salt for the Crown for three hundred years. In that time the sea has taken sixty-one of our ships. I can name them. I keep a ledger, my lord, like any merchant. Ask me which of my ships went down and I will tell you, and I will tell you who drowned."

It was very good. Tamsin watched it work on the hall, and then on Pell, who sat back and put his fingertips together and looked at the second book as though it might, after all, have been written by anyone.

"Miss Orrey," he said. "The copy sheets. A copy can be careless."

"It can, my lord." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted, and she made it bigger. "So I'll not ask you to trust the copy. Ask the tide." She opened the brown calf book at the fifteenth of Rimefall and laid it beside the green register, where the *Gannet* came home laden on the morning flood under the Chief Assessor's anchor in a ring. "Low water at the bar at ten past seven, my lord. A neap, with a north wind holding the water off. Less than three feet over the bar until nearly noon. The *Gannet* drew seven. She could not have come in that morning, and the register says she did, and the copy made a week later says she didn't. Mother Agathe can call Iska a liar, and Mr Faraday blind, and Brother Hale careless. But she can't call the sea anything. The sea was there."

The hall was quiet. Somewhere at the back a gull was walking on the roof.

Pell turned to the side of the hall, where Edric Vane stood between two watchmen. He looked very old. He had been up all night in a room in the Tidegate with a clerk of the justiciar's, and his gold spectacles were folded in his breast pocket and he did not take them out.

"Chief Assessor," Pell said. "Was the *Gannet* in Quell on the fifteenth of Rimefall?"

"No, my lord. She was on the Teeth."

"Then why does your stamp say that she was?"

Vane turned his head toward Agathe Sorrel, and everyone in the hall turned with him.

"Because Mother Agathe told me to put it there," he said. "On the second of Thawmonth, my lord, in my office, with the door shut. As she told me to put the others."

Agathe did not move. The rings on her hands lay still in her lap. When Pell asked her whether she had anything more to say, she looked at him for a long moment, and then past him, at the tall windows over the harbour, where the light lay on the water.

"Only what I said," she said. "I keep a ledger. So does the sea."

On the way out, in the crush at the doors, Tamsin heard a watchman tell another that Captain Mell had been seen at dawn on the north road with two horses and a heavy saddlebag, and that the justiciar had sent riders after him. She found she did not much care whether they caught him.

Corin came out of the Tidegate at four o'clock, when the justiciar's order reached it. Tamsin was waiting on the quay outside with Wren, and he came out blinking in the sunlight, thinner, with a week's beard and his split knuckles healed to pink, and he stood on the top step with his weight on his good leg and looked at her.

"Did you feed the child?" he said.

"She fed me, mostly."

"I did," said Wren.

They walked back along the harbour to the Quarter, the three of them, slowly, because of his leg. The wind had dropped and the tide was making, the last big flood of the springs coming in over the mud in long gold sheets, filling the channels one by one. On the far side of the water, on the spur of rock at the harbour mouth, Tidewatch stood black and roofless against the sky. There were men on it already with ladders and a block and tackle. The Margravine had sent word: there would be a new light by the next dark of the moon.

"Who'll keep it?" said Corin. "There's nobody who knows the tables."

Tamsin thought of the brown calf book on the table in the Tide Court, and the forty years of others that had burned, and the old man writing by candlelight with the great lens turning over his head.

"Someone will learn," she said.

They came to Fishbone Walk, and the *Patience*, and there was smoke from the stovepipe, because Iska Sorrel was sitting on the cabin roof with her Guild black hitched up to her knees, fishing, badly, off the side. She looked up as they came along the Walk.

"I had nowhere else to go," she said. "Is that all right?"

Corin looked at her, and at Tamsin, and at Wren, and at the boot that was still sitting on the cabin roof beside Iska, the good boot, the one that had come up on Wren's line a week ago.

"There's fish in the box," he said.
