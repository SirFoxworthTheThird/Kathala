When she came down the ladder her uncle had not moved. He was sitting exactly as Thale had left him, with his hands flat on the desk, and he did not look round.
"He knew I was up there," Tamsin said.
"Yes."
"He looked right at me."
"He wanted you to know he knew. That's how he does it. He doesn't hide things, Tamsin, he shows you that he could have." Orrin turned his head at last. "Idris never understood that. Idris thought if you found the thing, you'd won. Thale doesn't care what you find. He cares what you can bear to do with it."
She went round the desk and knelt and pulled the drawer out from under it, and took the warehouseman's book out of it, and held it against her chest. Her uncle watched her do it and did not try to stop her.
"You'll come," she said. "Tonight. With the other one."
"I'll come."
"And after. When Pell's home. You'll come to Pellmouth with me, and tell the Assize what you told me. All of it. The debts, and Marra's doctors, and the nineteen ships."
He did not answer for a long time. Outside the gulls had started up along the Mole, the way they did when the fishing boats came in, a long ragged screaming that went on and on.
"Your aunt used to say," he said at last, "that I could never finish a thing I'd started. Not a fence, not a letter, not a pipe of tobacco. She said I'd leave my own funeral halfway through to see what the tide was doing." He almost smiled. "I'll finish this one. I promise you that."
It was not what she had asked. She knew it was not what she had asked. But he had put his hand out, across the tide-tables, and after a moment she took it, and it was warm and heavy and shaking very slightly, and they stayed like that in the grey office until the light was full and the first pilot came banging on the door for his chit.
